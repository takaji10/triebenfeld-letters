--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Alarmed reporting from Vienna on Napoleon's return: he stands almost at the Rhine with over 200,000 men, Ney moves toward Switzerland, Russian troops pass Prague on 7 April, Wellington has only just left, and Prussia risks fighting alone; Poland rejoices at Napoleon. Hardenberg promises much, Stägemann obstructs, General Amstetten favours indemnification in estates. Encloses a Pro Memoria costing the Kaemen and Trąbczyn claims (Prussiemski, Broniewski, Chełmski, Leixner) at 390,100 Rthl, and asks about Eberhardt, blank forms and Beugelin.

Most Serene Prince,

Most gracious Lord!

The present epoch will no doubt remain the most remarkable in the annals of the world, and whatever also also comes to pass will only ever be a consequence of it. I am bound to fear that the sovereigns will everywhere come too late. For Napoleon stands today almost at the Rhine, and the Austrians, [uncertain: Huße] etc. are only beginning to negotiate travel monies — Wellington departed only yesterday, and will draw together the English or Hanoverian troops — Only our troops are being pushed forward, and were these to be beaten, then God help us, the parvenu has already more than 200000 men assembled. Shameful it is that such a thing could come to pass before the eyes of all the sovereigns, and grievous it is that one still holds one's peace so cheaply. On the 7th of April the Russian troops pass through Prague — thither the monarchs will go and then return here — but when would they come to the Rhine? The war is thus as good as fully declared. — Napoleon is away from Paris, hastening toward the Rhine; Ney has gone with a strong corps toward Switzerland, and at present one corps after another is being formed by Napoleon, so that one will not know whither to turn first.

That amid all these confusions little can be effected here, Your Serene Highness may readily judge. Prince Hardenberg promises henceforth to do everything. Herr von Stägemann, however, thinks and acts as it pleases him.

Something will nevertheless happen before one departs hence and goes to the army. And since the two [illegible] of the Prusimska have given back the Polish estates, I have submitted the accompanying Pro Memoria (which is meanwhile well worked out) — upon which I await the decision — Your Serene Highness could not be dismissed with it. General Amstetten is the only one who has good will — and he assures that you must be compensated by means of estates.

For the rest, everything seems to want to take on another shape, for nothing is more changeable than the fortune of war — the snail-like course of all business through 7 months, and the butter-soft yielding and most wrongly applied

generosity has made everything thick-skinned and has spoiled much — one also doubts whether men, after the previous treatment, will set to it again with such glad courage. In Poland things already look motley; the beasts are already letting their joy over Napoleon's reappearance be heard aloud, and are already dancing on their heads instead of on their legs — [uncertain: Maxalles] meanwhile may go as it will; my only wish is only that Your Serene Highness read the Cosmar and [illegible] the whole of the entailed estates; the rest will find itself. For the rest, if I were in Your Serene Highness's place, I should just now know very well what I would do. I would take merely the 98714 Rthl and say to the King and Emperor, they have disposed of my property. — This must be made good to me. It amounts to 367164 Rthl, [uncertain: gestett] the foregoing court; let the Lord Emperor and King keep these, and let me be given a receipt for the Courland debt as also for the 35000 Rthl, and let my property at once be made clean and clear to me. My son may be content in the meantime with the Hohenlohe estates. The [uncertain: Canz] princesses stand in very high favour with both lords. I fall silent in reverence, and I remain, until death,

Your Serene Highness's — N. B. Is it not possible that Eberhardt could come here? I beg for the most expeditious answer of all and for several

blank forms. The upright privy councillor of state Beugelin wished to have a letter from Your Serene Highness, so that he might be effective in the one matter. I asked for [uncertain: Er: Blan:]; this was written, and here is his answer; this is a [uncertain: brown] man who now does not let go. Vienna, the 31st of March

1815.

faithful and obedient servant

Pro Memoria. The most highly blessed King in 1796 conferred upon the Prince of Hohenlohe the Kaemener and Trąbczyn estates, which had fallen to the treasury after the absconded Starosta Prussiemski, on [illegible] terms. The said Prussiemski had, however, pledged the Kaemener estates to a certain von Broniewski for 80000 Rthl and the Trąbczyn ones to von Chełmski for 36000 ducats. A taking of possession of these estates on the Prince's part was now as little conceivable as the drawing of a revenue — by course of law everything had first to be sued for and decided — the suit on that head was not concluded by 1804; the Prince found himself compelled, in order to be able once to call the estates his own, to conclude a compromise with Broniewski and to pay him 55000 Rthl to stand off. The costs of the suit amounted to 6000. This amounts to — 61000 Rthl. From Midsummer 1805 until Midsummer 1807 the Prince therefore possessed the estates only, during which time he sold the same to the bank director Leixner. In 1807, however, the Polish treasury, and indeed 14 weeks after the Peace of Tilsit, took the estates from him by force. During these 8 years, while the daughter of Prussiemski possessed the estates, there are due to the Prince the interest on the foregoing 61000 Rthl, and this amounts to 24400 Rthl. The Prince's cash claim upon the Kaemener estates accordingly amounts to 85400 Rthl. During the period of the Prince's possession he had the estates judicially assessed; the assessment came out at —

180000 Rthl. From this, deducting the 55000 Rthl brought into account above,

there remains a surplus value of

125000 Rthl. This surplus value, however, now belongs, together with the 8 years' yield of the estates, to the bank director Leixner as purchaser of the same. The 85400 Rthl of so-called pledge-money belong to the Prince solely and alone — the Jew Meyer Bernhard [uncertain: had with him the money]; the Prince gave for it a mortgage upon Winniec etc., which estates the said Meyer Bernhard has to this day held in pledge. As concerns the Trąbczyn estates, these too were assessed at — 316000 Rthl — upon which the Prince contracted debts

The 14th of April. Now the French history begins already to grow very [uncertain: guilty]. Napoleon has been proclaimed Emperor by the whole country and people; the English declare that they will not meddle in the internal affairs of France — so Napoleon will no doubt remain Emperor — the Russians are indeed drawing nearer, but — they are made to march criss-cross to no purpose — they are only to eat their fill in Germany and lay everything waste. It goes the same way with the [uncertain: O—n] troops; these move very ponderously, for there everything is wanting — the English seem to have set the whole affair in motion and remain standing quite at a distance — Saxony is as little to be trusted as [uncertain: Poland] — in the end the Prussians, with the Dutch, the Hessians, the small states etc., are left standing quite alone, and the two Emperors will demand that these should first fetch the chestnuts out of the fire so that they may eat them. Napoleon will put himself into communication with Murat or Italy and, if possible, take the combined forces in the flank and in the rear; these must now face him, while he sends his confederates in haste with several bands toward the Netherlands — and where are men to be got in haste, to ward off disasters —?

A madder piece has not occurred since the founding of the world. Here indeed there appear, in secret, quite execrable lampoons; I fear that His Highness will vex himself to death, or will one way or another go under.
