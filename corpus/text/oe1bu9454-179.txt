Durchlauchtigster Fürst
Gnädigster Fürst und Herr!
Ewr Hochfürstliche Durchlaucht höchst gnädige Schreibe von 19 und 27ten Merz habe
darum so späth erhalte, weil selbige sehr lange in Słupca gelegen habe,
Es ist für mich mehr als traurig, daß ich von Ewr Durchlaucht so weit ent¬

wie alle Sachen gediehen sind.

hierher nach Berlin gemacht um hier zu würken und die Sache wo
möglich zu finaliſiren.
Die Dismembration ist wie Ewr Durchlaucht wißen sehr ehrenvoll genehmigt
und Selbst des Königs Majestät habe mir hoch Dero Beifall gewürdigt.
Auch die Betscher Sache ist bey der Kammer genehmigt von General Hinrich
Departement, dem König zur Approbation vorgelegt, und eben heute
solche von König vollzogen hier eingegangen. Diese Sache welche
Ewr Durchlaucht große Gefahr drohete ist nun auch ganz in reinen.
Die Taxen der Kalischen Kammer sind ebenfals beendiget, sie sind
wie die Abschrift sub A zeigt ausgefallen und liegen eben dem
General Finanz Departement vor von welchen ich Morgen ein Authen¬

möglichen Prüfungen festgeſezt iſt. — Jedoch išt hierbei weder der

nach der Sub. B. beigebogenen Designation nicht nieder groß und
wichtig und gehören eine Reihe von Jahre dazu bis alles rein ab¬

Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

Your Most Serene Highness's most gracious letter of the 19th and 27th of March I received so late only because the same lay a very long while in Słupca. It is more than saddening for me that I must live so far removed from Your Serene Highness and have news so seldom; it is particularly hard for me to act when Your Serene Highness leaves me so long without any answer at all. Meanwhile I do myself the honour of reporting to Your Highness most obediently how all matters have prospered.

Having put everything in order upon the estates, I betook myself here to Berlin again, in order to work here and, if possible, to finalise the business.

The dismemberment has, as Your Serene Highness knows, been approved most honourably, and His Majesty the King Himself has been pleased to grant me His high approbation. The Pszczew matter too has been approved at the Chamber, laid before the King for approbation by the General Hinrich Department, and this very day the same, executed by the King, has come in here. This matter, which threatened Your Serene Highness with great danger, is now likewise entirely in the clear. The valuations of the Kalisz Chamber are likewise concluded; they have turned out as the copy under A shows, and lie at this moment before the General Finance Department, from which tomorrow I am to receive an authentic attestation concerning the permanently established revenues of the estates

and in which the yield is fixed, after every possible examination, at 40260 Rthl. — However, in this neither the forest nor the transfer fees (Laudemia) and jurisdictional incidents have been taken into account, but all has been left to Your Serene Highness's disposition. Yet great as the yield has turned out to be, the debts, according to the schedule appended under B, are not less great and weighty, and a series of years will be required before everything is cleanly paid off.

It was a piece of good fortune that I arrived here just at this time, for the matter of the Courland post and the interest payments was again pending, the more so since the Minister von Stein is now clearing everything up and has to that end requested the appointment of a commission, consisting of Minister von Voss, the Grand Chancellor, and himself, over against the Minister Count von Schulenburg, which is to investigate everything and have everything secured, so that light may be had especially in respect of the Bank and the Maritime Trading Company, where great deficiencies are suspected. I have conferred much on this matter with the Lord General von K[öckritz], and have in the end agreed with him thus far, to request of His Majesty the King as many treasury notes as are required for the payment of Your Serene Highness's debts altogether.

The whole sum is, as the schedule states, however 698000 Rthl, exclusive of the items due to myself, with which I shall wait until happier events. I have wished to write on that account under my own name, as the enclosure

under Letter C shows, and the more so since the King is now according me the justice due to me. Meanwhile the Privy Cabinet Councillor B[eyme] has not yet given his fiat, and the worthy General feared, with every right, that the matter might founder altogether. Therefore that upright friend proposed that the bosom friend of Mr B. — the Court Fiscal Pochammer — should conduct this business.

With him I have only now come to an agreement; he will see to everything and demands for his pains an honorarium of like measure to that which he has been promised by the Prince of Hesse. In the meantime he must have a special power of attorney, and to that end he has this very moment sent me the form appended under Letter D, which I transmit to Your Serene Highness with the most obedient request that the same may be made out there in probative form / insofar as everything has Your Highness's approbation / and executed, and sent hither with the greatest speed, so that I may work quickly and bring everything to completion — the more so since Midsummer is at the door — and since in these most sorely pressed times not a Groschen is to be had from anywhere else, all the treasuries being especially [uncertain: barred], with which to pay the most urgent debts.

Everything will turn upon this, that Mr Pochammer so dispose the Cabinet Councillor that we obtain the monies, or treasury notes, at 3 per cent. The Prince of Orange must indeed give 4 per cent; meanwhile Mr Pochammer believes he shall entirely succeed and prise the capital loose at 3 per cent. Were this so, Your Serene Highness might count yourself exceedingly fortunate. You would be quit of all your debts at a single stroke, would have after some 30 years the cleanest of estates, and would meanwhile nevertheless draw yearly some 10000 Rthl from the two lordships, and would be free and quit of all cares and sorrow, and especially of the vexation of the dishonouring lawsuits. — The plans of amortisation I have drawn up to that end, and particularly for Your Serene Highness's survey; they are respectfully appended here under Letter E. Deign, most gracious Prince and Lord, to examine everything, and send me above all the power of attorney for the Court Fiscal Mr Pochammer — so that this work, so exceedingly important and tending to Your Serene Highness's welfare, peace and honour, may be pursued most zealously and at length happily concluded. Most rewarding for me is it that the Monarch alone could not withhold his approbation, and that this matter has excited the astonishment even of the Cabinet Councillor Beyme; but what has pleased me most of all is that the Minister von Voss has set up my conduct as a model to his Chambers.

Now as concerns the Glenck letter of the 28th of February, I answer thereto most deeply obediently that the Prusimska matter is as foreign to Glenck as colour is to one born blind. Were it possible for me to be everywhere at once, I should pursue this affair too with equal force. But since this cannot be done without arrogating to oneself divine attributes like a madman, I must keep to the chief matters, and work upon these with honest zeal, in order to establish Your Serene Highness's peace. For the rest, I console myself with this, that Mr Glenck has merely feigned this matter with his year, in order now to have material for writing to Your Serene Highness and to recall his [uncertain: Pracheregen] to Your Highness's memory.

His purpose he has attained, in that I must now see and speak with him. — My judgement was nevertheless correct, for of the Prusimska matter he knew nothing; only the consent for the sale of his lands was the object upon which he discoursed. He vaunted his many years of service, and that he had made 42 journeys in Your Serene Highness's affairs — for which, in his opinion, he believed he had deserved a far greater remuneration than this trifle. To make an end of the thing, I asked him what he actually wanted, if he were to sell the lands back to Your Serene Highness. His answer was that he could not let them go for [illegible] under 8000 Rthl. For he had already made a gift to Your Serene Highness of 330 Morgen of land which Your Highness had promised him besides the bailiwick and the 300 Morgen of forest — this surely was already more than reasonable, and in case one should wish to refuse him the consent, he would bring suit and demand these as well. — In order to make an end of all these [uncertain: bachensee] of Glenck's, I have promised him the 8000 Rthl sub spe rati, and I submit myself herein to Your Serene Highness's commands — but the consent

to his sale Your Serene Highness cannot give him, unless You should prefer some 20 new lawsuits — for every peasant and cottager who has land within the 700 Morgen would be unwilling to yield, and would protest against the dismemberment and against everything. I shall now see to make it possible to raise a part of the monies and to pay Glenck off. As concerns the consent regarding Zielomyśl, I return the piece received thereof / for the middle sheets are wanting / with the submissive request that it be at once annulled. — Zielomyśl is an appurtenance of the lordship of Pszczew which has been sold to the subjects — be it a demesne or a rustic parcel — enough that everything has been sold to the people, and everything is now exactly [uncertain: reckoned] which has been attached to this or that matter. — Your Serene Highness has reserved to Yourself no right over the parcels, but only the right of superior ownership, in such manner that You will approve everything which those people, who are now called proprietary possessors, lay before You, and will receive for it Your 10 per cent transfer fees (Laudemia). The so-called hereditary tenants have also already taken the Landeberg heirs to task, and demand the estate back, since the contract of the said [uncertain: Radki] is false. Were Your Serene Highness now to approve matters contrary to this and to the contract, and to consent to a sale without the knowledge of the hereditary possessors, then the people would have a right to complain, and every single individual could frame against You so costly a lawsuit as would [uncertain: drive out] all the advantages gained. Besides which, it is again amply [uncertain: doubtful] to grant a burgher now demesne

rights. That is altogether forbidden by a new cabinet order, and draws after it the loss of the right of residence. Even alienations out of these estates to persons not qualified to possess are altogether prohibited, and are in no wise to be tolerated. For the Oleśnica hereditary lessees this cabinet ordinance and the edicts published upon it are [uncertain: most] salutary to me, for by these alone must the [uncertain: sub-tenants] clear out. But above all, besides all this, I must further point out to Your Serene Highness that You Yourself / even if You really had the right to it / would lose frightfully, if for the sake of the wretched 6000 Rthl You approved this shameful plan. Zielomyśl will thereafter, when the present hereditary possessors parcel it up, be worth some 100/m Rthl. Every 20 years the holdings round about are sold, and there Your Serene Highness and Your descendants have the estates paid for anew through the 10 per cent transfer fees. This is surely so essential a thing that no lord of the soil gives it away for eternity. As concerns the 30000 Rthl, I see as yet no possibility how[?] I am to procure these so quickly. If I obtain the capital from the King, Your Serene Highness does indeed obtain much — but all of it goes entirely upon this, that all debts are to be paid. The latter must be so, because the mortgage [uncertain: certificate] must be clean. Whence, then, to take it? The one case would be if I could get money upon one of my mortgages on Świniary and Konotop, or that the hereditary purchasers in Zagórów and Trąbczyn [uncertain: frequently scarcely] and should bring money to perform, but [it belongs to that] that I could be at home or in those parts; but that cannot be, for I must absolutely work here. If nevertheless there be a possibility, Your Serene Highness may count upon my zeal to realise Your wishes by every means.

By the next post I shall write to Your Highness further upon this.

Perhaps some means may somewhere be found of obtaining something further behind the great loan. Perhaps it will succeed, for everyone now wishes to help, since the King Himself has loudly praised my proceedings and done me justice; it served to pacify the creditors. The saddest thing for me is that Your Serene Highness now lives so far from here, and that I must bear all these burdens so entirely alone. Upon no man can I rely; I may order what I will on my departure, and nothing whatever is done — when I come back again I must simply thank God that I find it as I had left it. — Grattenauer is now continually ill, can neither write nor yet less instruct. — Schenck has quite transformed himself into the idlest of hands. — Grimm lets everything lie until he thinks I might climb on his roof again. Melzern I had better not depict at all, for his brutality and idleness are well enough known to Your Serene Highness.

But if God now helps me to obtain the loan, pay all the debts, and thereby to remove the great part of all the lawsuits, then I shall also lay before Your Serene Highness a plan by which the useless [uncertain: appointments] can be put into [uncertain: Steat]. For now I must still go warily, so that the rogues do not turn traitor and become harmful to the good cause. If it be in the world possible, may Your Serene Highness deign to answer me with the utmost speed, and in any case by express courier, for otherwise I must lie here too long in vain. — In deepest reverence I remain, until death,

Your Serene Highness's
