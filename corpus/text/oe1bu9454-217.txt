--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Sanitz explains his removal from Silesia and Brau after nearly 49 years to pursue the loss of his fortune and the recovery of estates taken by the Dąbska woman; his former legal agent Weltzer in Josen holds out little hope of Polish justice, the Dresden court excuses the Polish judges and denies the Peace of Tilsit's application, while the French envoy may take up his cause if Imperial orders are lacking. He asks for copies of the judgment and tribunal sentence in the Prince's Trombschyner suit, and reports rumours of imminent war in the North.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Right Honourable Sir, most especially Highly Honoured Sir Lieutenant! Handsomely must I indeed [uncertain: reading], Your Right Honourable's kind letter to have left so long thankfully unanswered; did I not count upon your kind indulgence, it would not now be from anew of carriages to burden the same with my present ones. That I have had to leave Silesia and Brau, wherein I lived for nearly 49 years, mostly happily and gladly, and chose [this place] for my residence, in order to press my most urgent affairs, the loss of my fortune, better here, and if possible still to salvage something, is perhaps not entirely unknown to you. Only the prospects in this are still not very clear, and even my old former legal agent in Josen, Herr Weltzer, who is now also staying here, and who believes he understands the Polish justice, has little hope. The Foreign Department has, on my behalf, [uncertain: entrusted itself] to the Dresden court; but this one too, though he holds that the debts previously paid

could be pursued, yet as concerns the estates themselves he seeks — apart from there being no official command of the Emperor for this — to excuse the Polish judges of that time, and to render the articles of the Peace of Tilsit, so far as they bear upon this matter, inapplicable to it. The French envoy here, with whom [there was] much [dealing] during my earlier stay here, holds out hope to me in this manner: that if no express Imperial command exists for this, and I could give him the official [documents], he would take up my [cause]. Finally I have [word] himself from the Saxon court, who says that the Dąbska woman petitioned the Emperor at Dresden for the restitution of the estates — that His Imperial Majesty merely forwarded her petition to the then Pluish government. Upon this the Pluish commissioners ordered that the estates be given back to the Dąbska woman. At present I am occupied [in having] this [passed over / overlooked], and shall see how far

the French envoy here will take up this matter, and I have also not failed to give Your Right Honourable notice of it at once, so that His Most Serene Highness — to whose graces I beg to be commended — might perhaps likewise make use of it. [How] shall I forget the benefactions which I received through this Prince; my attachment can and will never die. Has then no sentence yet come out of the first tribunal concerning the Prince's Trombſchyner estates? Would Your Right Honourable have the goodness to let me know, 1) if possible in copy — what the Peace [uncertain: Ruhte] has adjudged, and 2ndly what the sentence with the tribunal has stated? News I would gladly [send] you in plenty, but what is given out here as certain on one day is doubted the next, and on the third a second, often contradictory [report] displaces

the first. So much, however, does seem to shine forth from it all, that an early outbreak of the war in the North is presumed. Were it clear in the evening, then it would be burning in the North — that, I believe, is the conductor. In expectation of a [uncertain: kindly] reply, and of pardon for my negligence, I remain, in the most perfect esteem, lifelong, Your Right Honourable's Berlin, the 15th of August 1811 Stralauer Str: No 13 wholly obedient and faithful [uncertain: Euder] servant v Sanitz
