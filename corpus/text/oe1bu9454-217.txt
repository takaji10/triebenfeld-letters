--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Sanitz explains his removal from Silesia and Brau after nearly 49 years to pursue the loss of his fortune and the recovery of estates taken by the Dąbska woman; his former legal agent Weltzer in Josen holds out little hope of Polish justice, the Dresden court excuses the Polish judges and denies the Peace of Tilsit's application, while the French envoy may take up his cause if Imperial orders are lacking. He asks for copies of the judgment and tribunal sentence in the Prince's Trombschyner suit, and reports rumours of imminent war in the North.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Right Honourable Sir, Most particularly Highly Honoured Lieutenant! I must indeed be ashamed to have left Your Right Honourable's kind letter so long unanswered with my thanks; did I not count upon your kind indulgence, I should not now venture anew to burden you with the present one.

That I have had to leave [uncertain: Silesia] and [uncertain: Brau], wherein I lived near 49 years mostly happy and content, and chose [illegible] for my residence, in order the better to pursue here my most pressing affairs, the loss of my fortune, and if possible still to salvage something, will perhaps not be entirely unknown to you. Only, the prospects in this matter are not yet very clear, and even my old former counsel in [uncertain: Josen], Mr Weltzer, who is now likewise staying here, and who believes he knows the Polish justice, has little hope; the Foreign Department has [uncertain: pledged] itself on my behalf to the [uncertain: Dresden Huffe], but this one too would have it that the previously paid

debts might be recovered; but as concerns the estates themselves, he seeks — setting aside that no official command of the Emperor exists for this — to excuse the then Polish judges and to render the peace articles of Tilsit, so far as they are in favour of and herein, not applicable to the case.

The French envoy here, with whom [uncertain: formerly] there was much during my stay here, gives me hope in this manner: that if no express Imperial command exists for it, and I could give him the official [document], he would take my [cause] upon himself. At length I have it from the Saxon court itself, which says that the lady Dąbska in Dresden petitioned the Emperor for the restitution of the estates — that His Imperial Majesty merely forwarded her petition to the then [uncertain: Prussian] government. Upon this the [uncertain: Prussian] commissioners ordered that the estates be given back to the lady Dąbska. At present I am occupied in having this [uncertain: transcribed], and shall see how far

the French envoy here will take up these matters, and I have also not failed to give Your Right Honourable notice of it at once, so that His Most Serene Highness — to whose grace I beg to be commended — might perhaps likewise make use of it.

I shall never forget the benefactions I received through this Prince; my attachment can and will never die.

Is there then still no sentence out of the first tribunal concerning the Prince's Trąbczyn estates? Would Your Right Honourable have the goodness to let me know, 1) if possible in copy — what the peace [uncertain: rod] adjudged, and 2nd, what the sentence with the tribunal has declared?

News I would gladly [give] you in plenty, only what is here given out for certain on one day is doubted on the next, and on the third a second, often contradictory, one displaces

the first. This much, however, does seem to shine forth from it all, that an early outbreak of war in the north is surmised. Were it clear in the evening, then it would be burning in the north — that, I believe, is the conductor. Awaiting a kindly answer and the pardon of my negligence, I remain in the most perfect esteem, lifelong, Your Right Honourable's

Berlin

the 15th of August 1811
