verlangte, daß die Ottosche Taxe noch einmahl revidirt und nach Südpreise

--- SUMMARY (English) ---
A despairing report that the Invalids' Fund loan has collapsed: Court Councillor Lengnich, engaged by building councillor Glenck for 25 Fr., recast the Otto valuation downwards to 78,000 Rthl, wrecking credit in Frankfurt an der Oder and the prospects of sale. Stubenrauch has withdrawn; Glenck's written undertakings to disreputable speculators may bring 50 lawsuits. Amelang reports Beyen's offer of the King's debts as a gift instead of compensation for the Brzyzer estates, with the confiscated Kolzig estates proposed instead. Triebenfeld will seek 120,000 Rthl on Kontop and Zagorowo.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

The loan from the Invalids' Fund, which building councillor Glenck assured so confidently, has likewise come to grief, and for the following reasons. The Invalids' Department required that the Otto valuation be revised once more and recast according to the principles of the South-Prussian [uncertain: price] Chamber. Glenck, who believed he was taking the shortest way to this end, applied, on the advice of a certain Captain Lange, to Court Councillor Lengnich; the latter promised to have the valuation recast quickly for 25 Fr. Glenck believed this, departed, and merely requested Lengnich to have the new valuation delivered to Councillor of War Seelmann. The latter has been done, but to my horror I learned yesterday on my arrival that the newer valuation had been reduced to 78000 Rthl, and that it has been handed over in that form; Your Most Serene Highness may judge the consequence — the saddest thing of all, however, is now that the estates have been utterly degraded in worth by this mutilated valuation, and that the sale of them will be made very, very much harder. Even in Frankfurt an der Oder, since these people were counting on a loan of 80 to 100000 Rthl,

everything will now come to grief there too, for since Leixner must [have it] of Your Most Serene Highness for the sake of the loan, he must also see the [uncertain: documents], and in that way the lower valuation comes before his eyes, which, had the loan not been necessary, one need never have produced. Even if I now obtain 60000 Rthl lent from somewhere on the lower valuation, it will be extremely damaging to the sale and perhaps hinder it entirely. My sole hope is now cast upon Stubenrauch, to whom I have promised to furnish warranty of title and every conceivable guarantee. This evening he means to give his declaration — should this plan fail, then all is lost here, for all the other projects are not worth a single Groschen either and are merely the designs of a certain Dietz, Werner, the Jew Borchart,

Captain Lange and such rabble, who have neither head nor credit, and who needed written undertakings from Glenck only so as to be able to borrow something upon them, that they might not perish of hunger. It is a sad thing that Glenck reckoned upon the swindles of this brood and frittered away the time in so highly culpable a manner.

God is my witness that I cannot sleep for grief. My most heartfelt wish is that Heaven might but govern Stubenrauch's heart, for then it will indeed go well — gladly would I pledge him my goods and my poor afflicted soul.

Beyen yesterday had Councillor Am[elang] called to him, and told him that His Majesty could give Your Serene Highness neither South Prussian estates nor money. Your Serene Highness, however, owed the King very considerable sums, besides a loan from the Duke of Courland, on which the King had hitherto paid the interest. Since His Majesty doubted of recovering a single Groschen of the sums lent and of the interest paid by way of advance, His Majesty was willing, out of especial grace, to make Your Serene Highness a gift of these sums, as also of the interest hitherto paid to the Courlanders, as a compensation for the lost Brzyzer estates. In return, however, Your Serene Highness should from now on satisfy the Courlanders both as to capital and as to interest. This was not His Majesty's own notion. With the best heart, and with the love he bore Your Serene Highness, he had not been able to bring about more than this. He ought also to have given Your Serene Highness this resolution at once, but as he did not know whether it might be agreeable to Your Serene Highness, he had

held it back for the present, and wished that Amelang might first sound out Your Serene Highness on the matter. Amelang thereupon answered him: he knew that Your Serene Highness wished for no gift from the King; that Your Highness would faithfully pay His Majesty everything that was lawfully owed to him; that Your Serene Highness demanded nothing but what you could claim by right, and that you believed you had the first right, when you pressed, directly and indirectly, for a compensation for the estates taken from you, for which Your Serene Highness had to pay the [uncertain: charter] stamp duty and other dues to the amount of many thousands, and where Your Highness had besides advanced much money.

Amelang then proposed to him that Your Serene Highness be given, by way of compensation, the Kolziger estates confiscated from Count Schlabrendorff — whereupon he replied that the estates had not yet been taken in; that the King would probably prefer to keep these estates himself, because His Majesty had a [uncertain: deposit] from the former government for the [uncertain: seizure] of the estates; yet in the matter there was still the first possibility of obliging the Prince. — Amelang meanwhile knew everything, for it agreed word for word with what the honest General had said. — It was particularly

evident that it was through his mediation that the fine answer had been held back. All this raised Am[elang]'s courage, and, being prepared, he spoke more freely. A[melang] also now lives in the first hope that they will be civil and will offer no gross insolence, but will hold it back altogether — and also that they will make haste to execute the confiscation, or the judgment to that effect, so as to be able to confer the Kölziger estates upon Your Serene Highness.

Braun will keep the General [uncertain: K.] in play and guide the matter along this road — Am[elang] will do the same with B[eyen] in his own manner — but neither knows anything of the other, and even on this plan both must in the end, without having known it, meet at one point.

For the rest, A[melang] will draw up a letter to B[eyen] and perhaps to the King, which Your Serene Highness is to execute and send off. I have not yet spoken with Herr von Hard[enberg], but tomorrow it shall certainly be done. The King is by no means willing to enter into any war; meanwhile Russia is said not to be willing to let the matter rest this time with empty remonstrances. His Majesty set out for Pomerania for the review in a very ill humour and will be home again on the 5th of next month.

God have mercy! What a perverse creature this Glenck is — now all is over. Stubenrauch has been put off by the running-about of bad fellows and will have nothing to do with the business. The Invalids' Fund will give no money — and just so everything is spoilt with the Wards' Board; what am I now to set about?

Sell Kontop? Why not Kolzig then — O God, my fine plan! O had Your Serene Highness but put the fool on a golden chain and had him fed with Strasbourg pies, then his keeping would indeed have cost much, but he could neither have undermined your peace nor, still less, have deprived you here of credit and good name. It is more than shameful that the man has here got himself involved with all the base brutes of M[uncertain: ü]ller and given every knave written undertakings for sums and entered into partnership contracts with them. It will in the end become necessary to have all these slips declared null and void through the public papers, since otherwise Your Serene Highness might yet be burdened with 50 lawsuits.

My head swims when I think of the approaching term, and when all at once the last spark of hope too is extinguished. It is now 1 o'clock in the night — for anxiety I can neither rest nor be still, and the beast in human shape sleeps without a care — O righteous God, what is a man without true feeling of honour?

O had the unreasonable man only not, to my very greatest misfortune, allowed the Otto valuation to be so destroyed and mutilated, then, with Amelang — who shows himself active and who is now gaining credit through Beym[?] — I should have found much [uncertain: further] counsel, and should have been able to raise the moneys on my own name or bill of exchange (because I am held to be rich). But this prospect too has been robbed from me by the annihilation of the Otto valuation. I mean at any rate to try, and in an hour I ride to Potsdam to Pliehen; he told me some days ago that he had 300000 lying in Maritime Trading Company obligations, and that he wished to make a trial for once whether he could not gain something by a purchase of estates — Perhaps God gives fortune along this road — More therefore when I return. Money would now be to be had. Rietz has money — Prince Ferdinand too will give it through Stubenrauch's mediation — but sure mortgage security is demanded. Where am I to get this from?

The valuations of the Zagorowo estates are only 124 Rthl, and these are encumbered with 56000 Rthl, thus [uncertain: indebted] above half; for that the estates are worth four times as much I know — but the valuations argue against it, and consequently no reliance is to be placed on it. Kulm and Kaemen are botched, for the present valuation is 110000 Rthl and 66000 Rthl have been awarded to Bronicurln, so that here too no fund remains upon which money could be taken or demanded with security.

I shall now try this further — I shall make trial whether anyone will lend 120000 Rthl upon Kontop and Zagorowo, for which I shall offer a bill of exchange besides. If this succeeded, everything might yet be retrieved, for then one would have time to bring the Kaemen affair soundly to a close and to procure a good sale, and also to dismember the Zagorowo and Trąbczyn estates at leisure. If I accomplish nothing, then I hasten quickly to Frankfurt an der Oder and try there once more [illegible]. In deepest reverence I remain, until death,

Your Most Serene Highness's

Berlin, 26th May 1804

most faithful servant

v. Triebenfeld
