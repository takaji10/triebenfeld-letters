--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Reports Hardenberg's remark to Prince H. of Bartenstein that Hohenlohe is the worst-treated of men and that he now champions his cause; urges an autograph letter of thanks and a declaration confirming Triebenfeld's Memoirs, lest he be compromised with Hahn, so the Curl heirs may be paid, the settlements annulled and Cosmar cast out. Asks for advance copies of the Commission and Ober Landes Gericht reports, blank forms, and 100 ducats, pleading hunger and 50 ducats borrowed. Suggests Meyer's French claims (240000 Livres from Pr. Artois) be pressed via Humboldt under §19 of the Peace of Paris.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

That His Serene Highness the Prince Hardenberg now truly means well by Your Serene Highness is proved by the Prince's remark to Prince H. of Bartenstein. The discourse turned upon Your Serene Highness being named, whereupon the Prince at once replied: Yes, this honest man is the one who has suffered most, he has been shockingly treated, I am now taking up his cause in the most active manner — I want him helped before all others — Everything now depends upon Your Serene Highness's declaration and upon the confirmation of my memorials by His Serene Highness — here there must be no contradiction whatever, for otherwise I am compromised with Hahn and the affair [takes] a tragic turn — whereas if everything is substantiated in accordance with both our memorials — the Prince will at once have the money paid out in order to satisfy the Curl heirs, the King will annul the settlement, and Cosmar will be cast out and called to the heaviest account — everything else will then follow of itself — Friend ß moreover wishes, together with me, that we might obtain the very speediest knowledge, by good copies, of the outcome of the Commission and of the report of the High Court of Appeal, and indeed, wherever possible, one post-day earlier than the report comes in to His Serene Highness the State Chancellor — so that one may forestall everything here.

I look forward longingly to the blank forms, but above all to the thanksgiving letter in your own hand to the Prince State Chancellor — Tell this noble man, quite briefly, that you render him your [uncertain: youngest] thanks already for what has been done, etc., that you may thereby look with confidence to the end of your sufferings, which the wicked Cosmar has prepared for you; mention quite briefly / that Connus in the last 3 [years?] therefore gave you, instead of 72000 Rthl, only 2500 Rthl altogether, that he made use of your own son's weakness to cover this up, and that in this time you live so wretchedly and must even struggle with hunger, that Triebenfeld has not yet presented the matter as it is in and of itself, etc.; you have laid, however, both your domestic and your military affairs in his hands, etc. etc.

I wish this because the Prince reads Your Serene Highness's letters — but has no time to read whole files and records himself, and can only hear reports drawn from them. In the Hohenlohe affair work is going forward in the most active manner — a stop has already been put to the King's tyranny, his power against the mediatised princes is being greatly restricted, presumably Oels etc. will be taken as the norm — the note concerning your expulsion, and the otherwise shocking treatment, that concerning

the [illegible] withdrawal of the 2000 mk. appanage of your lord brother I cannot send today, because on account of the [uncertain: street mud] I cannot leave the house, but have no money to ride, while Gärtner, since I can promise him nothing, has not been with me for 8 days.

A time full of torment I pass here — I am to act in so odiously twisted a business — to fight a hydra of more than 9 heads, and I have nothing in my hands and nothing on my body. I could often perish for shame; I beg urgently, most gracious Lord, send only 100 ducats, otherwise I must go hungry — thus he has lent me 50 ducats, and these will be quite gone within a few days — if meanwhile this true friend is not to grow weary, and is to go on trusting, then I must hand the money over to him, for he has his own people to provide for in Berlin, and therefore he himself is in need of it, especially at the New Year. So do send, Your Serene Highness, the 100 ducats without fail, likewise the papers noted overleaf, and support my children in accordance with my request in Breslau. From the accompanying book a great part is borrowed, as to how it is to be conducted in future in the Wüstemberge.

In truest attachment and highest reverence I remain, until death, Your Serene Highness's

Vienna, the 20th of December

1814.

faithful and most obedient servant

v. Triebenfeld

The King of Württemberg departs in a couple of days; his appearance betokens the foulest humour. Only between the wings at the Casperl Theatre does the All-Highest countenance sometimes show itself in transfigured radiance. For the rest, it stands in today's Vienna newspapers that the King of Baum calls upon his subjects to report themselves if anyone has claims in France, be it against the King or the State — whoever is too weak to do this before the French Commission shall bring it before a commission to be appointed by him — the nation is said to have undertaken to pay the King's debts — would it not be advisable that Meyer, or Your Serene Highness yourself, should bring to the Prince Hardenberg's remembrance the 100000 [uncertain: Er dör] advanced to the King of [uncertain: Brandreich] and sent to Coblenz by Meyer — Meyer did in fact deliver the money there to the King and Prince Artois, and received 1000 Rthl for it — Meyer's written undertaking from Prince Artois for 240000 livres could be made good in this connection — His Excellency [uncertain: Münst] von Humboldt will certainly do everything and help vigorously — but the reminder would have to be given quickly, because then the matter could still be set best afoot with His Excellency von Humboldt — in the 19th paragraph of the Peace of Paris all claims are fixed to be paid, and the State has bound itself ex post to pay the royal debts which it contracted in its period of misfortune, to about 22 millions.
