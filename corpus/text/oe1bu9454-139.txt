--- SUMMARY (English) ---
A furious report that the settlement with Broniewski cannot now be undone: Glenck submitted it for approval on the 6th, two days before the Prince's written protest arrived, and it was confirmed and sent to the court despite a rescript about the deposit. Metzer and Wünster each blame the other, while conceding that a deposit of even six days would have broken Broniewski; liquidation proceedings may run 6 to 8 years. Triebenfeld will go to Betsche over Bornstädt's mishandled eviction.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Lord!

My foreboding that every effort here to undo the notorious settlement would be in vain is unhappily borne out. The villains have hedged it about with so many clauses that even with the greatest sacrifices one may not hope to win the least advantage. It is a piece of knavery without equal — the court rescript concerning the deposit was here — notwithstanding which the base settlement was concluded — Glenck submitted it on the 6th for approbation and begged that its execution be hastened as much as possible and that it be drawn up for both parties. Your Most Serene Highness's letter was here on the 8th, in which you protested against the settlement.

This was quickly laid aside; they approved and dispatched the cursed settlement with a plaintiff's speed, and sent it in to the court. What am I now to do? Complain against it, go to Berlin and make a noise — none of that helps, one only makes oneself ridiculous. Good men, of whom there are scarcely 2 here, pity Your Serene Highness, but the great crowd [uncertain: to bed] and [uncertain: sells off] everyone who takes part in this affair. Oh, I could go mad.

The basest thing is now that Glenck does not come — although he received my letters here, he would not wait, but went off to Betsche. I have sent express couriers and a messenger-woman after him — he does not come, and does not answer a syllable. Here I [uncertain: gnaw] sit here in vain — God, it is dreadful what these people permit themselves. The [uncertain: fullest] thing is that not one of them will admit any share in this rascally piece of work. Metzer rails that Wünster did not make the settlement better, and damns Glenck to all eternity for not having concluded it sub sper[atione] [uncertain: sub speirati] — He says he had meant to make the settlement and to declare that he had indeed no power of attorney but would furnish it afterwards / by which one would have had room enough to manoeuvre; but this Glenck did not accept. Hence Glenck concluded entirely alone, without his knowledge.

He could not deny that if the deposit had been made, the outcome would have been very good for the Prince, since even Broniewski had already declared that he would drown himself or shoot himself through the head, and leave his wife and children to the Prince.

For the rest, Metzer says that Wunster made the settlement and arranged everything so — in short, each shifts it onto the

other — but as I see it, they are all equally [uncertain: culpable]. Here I no longer care to remain; rather, if Glenck does not come, I shall set off toward evening for Betsche, so as to neglect nothing there — for with Bornstädt there will be much clamour; they have proceeded with him exactly as wrongly as with the men of Trąbczyn.

That is to say, they sued for eviction, but neither had a liquidated sum established nor, still less, applied for execution — everything here lies in the dark, and it will cost unspeakable trouble to bring it quickly into order. From Betsche I must go back here once more, to dislodge Bornstädt and to see what security [uncertain: Braniewehr] puts up.

This very moment Glenck has arrived — He knows neither what to say in his excuse, nor, still less, anything to propose for the redress of the affair. In short, the man feels nothing, though I could vex myself to death. He has begged Metzer to [uncertain: reject] everything and to uphold the settlement. — This I know for certain. Glenck also let slip that he had seen the court decree before he submitted the settlement for

approbation. — But Metzer had said that, although it did not stand in it that the grounds of arrest were to be produced, it nevertheless went without saying that they would have to be furnished afterwards; with that, however, he would never concern himself. One could go mad over this [uncertain: babble] — for Metzer did after all admit that, had one been able to make the deposit even for only 6 days, Broniewski would have been lost and would have taken what was given him out of grace.

In short, it is enough to vex one to death, when one has long to do with this low, rapacious rabble. To the question how long the liquidation proceedings would last, Metzer opined, very cold-bloodedly, 6 to 8 years. This is precisely the reason why the rascals have twisted the matter so, that they may keep Your Serene Highness always in their hands and be able to empty your pockets. I

I remain, until death,

Your Most Serene Highness's

Posen, the 22nd of July

1804

faithful, humble and obedient servant

v. Triebenfeld
