--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Urges a settlement with the princesses while they need money, warning that delay will cost them 300,000 Rthl, and presses for Councillor von Eberhard to come to Vienna, offering to arrange everything if he travels with his own horses and a passport from the Prince and a border magistrate. Reports that 100,000 Rthl are to be paid over to Prince August in cash, not paper, criticises his acceptance of the presidency others refused, and passes on news of Napoleon's advance on Paris.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most gracious Prince and Lord!

This very moment, and indeed some 2 hours before the post goes out, I receive Your Serene Highness's two letters of the 18th with the enclosure from Eberhardt. I can do nothing further in the matter than to lament Your Serene Highness's fate — you will surely deign, most gracious Prince and Lord, to see for yourself that without a compromise with the [uncertain: short] princesses nothing further can be said here, the more so as the Prince Chancellor has already been lied to in this, in that it was asserted with confidence that the Weiler and 17 wished to come to a settlement — and if the matter cannot be done now, while the ladies are in want of money, then, best of Princes, they are thereby 300000 Rthl the poorer. Councillor von Eberhard is right that his coming here would be most necessary. Only we can beg neither a passport for him here, still less money — but if he does come here, then everything shall be arranged for him and he shall be placed beyond all responsibility. He needs only a passport from Your Serene Highness and from the magistrate there of some border town, and if he travelled with his own horses, then the devil may ask after him — the matter must now be settled in one way or another, and I neither can nor will take that upon myself alone. — For the rest, it may yet come to pass that I, with my friends, shall before long have Court Councillor Cosmar's swan-song and the panegyric of those obliging spirits made public — — if it is possible in the world and I am not led astray from other quarters, then by God I keep my word and go on toward the goal so long as it takes until I reach it, and if this which I already see in outline is only in some measure attained, then assuredly every scoundrel will want to become a [uncertain: knight] in the business and will boast of having done Your Serene Highness God knows what favours in the world. But I stake my honour upon it that not one of them troubled himself the least about Your Serene Highness, still less ever went to bed [uncertain: hungry] or watched through sleepless nights on your account, good Prince. Meanwhile I beg that to those [uncertain: I would] not count a Hahn, Paulle, or Lombardini, for these may indeed sometimes have had the sun in the house rather than the morrow's bread — for themselves and their children. Do think, noble and exalted Prince, upon these faithful people. I know they suffer no tutoring, but hear the counsel of an honest fellow who hates flattery and not, as

my old judge would have you believe — enough of this.

God will and can help.

That Prince August has accepted the post of president, which all men of honour have declined, is heart-shattering — for now they will no longer hold him here to be weak but rather — mad. The Congress has forbidden the King of Württemberg everything and referred him to a general constitution. Even every power of attorney of all the Estates and persons assembled there comprehended the protest within it. How comes the man (against which I myself warned Your Serene Highness) to blow now upon the King's horn, and to assume the character which everyone else thrust from himself — what will Langenburg, what will Prince Kirchberg and others say of this young man, that he accepts the [uncertain: painted] office which everyone else declined with steadfastness and magnanimity — O, that is a wise father of the country. Since yesterday a letter has gone off to Prince August, and one to Berlin, that the 100000 Rthl are to be handed over to him, but absolutely in cash (for perhaps Cosmar will want to give paper) — you did indeed wish it, and I still doubt that he will withdraw the power of attorney. To everything else I say nothing further than that I refer to the letters sent off these 4 days past. The servant of Barbe let Your Serene Highness by all means forward on, as I have begged — he takes my horses, and you will have a stout wicker conveyance given there, with a [uncertain: tarpaulin]. Then also send the two-horse waggon to Prague to fetch the glass — lastly deign to send the drummer and Waurzyn back to Blizanow with a post and a little travelling money. According to today's news Napoleon presses ever further toward Paris — the King [uncertain: is stationing] an army near Mevin, which happened at Grenoble and Lyon also — but Napoleon is so cunning that where danger threatens him he goes around it — and as no one may be much minded to earn the premium of 12 millions of francs set upon his head, he might well succeed in paying his visit in Paris.

The judgements upon it are very various — and the confusion is great. No one here fares better in it than the stock-jobbers. God, were it only possible to send Eberhard here. Most respectfully I remain, until death, Your Serene Highness's

Vienna, the 26th of March

1815.

Glenck I found out today; he commends himself to your grace, and wishes Your Serene Highness in the Op[text lost] at once [text lost] most faithful servant
