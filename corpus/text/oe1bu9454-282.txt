--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Replies hurriedly to two letters of the 18th with Eberhardt's enclosure: without a compromise with the "short princesses" nothing can be done, the Prince Chancellor having been misled that the Weilers would settle, and delay may cost the ladies 300000 Rthl. Urges Eberhard's journey to Vienna, though no passport or money can be obtained there. Reports Prince August's acceptance of the presidency others declined, the Congress's rebuff to the King of Würtemberg, orders that 100000 Rthl be paid in cash, transport arrangements for Barbe's servant, glass from Prag and Blizanow, and Napoleon's advance on Paris.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most gracious Prince and Lord! This very moment, and indeed some 2 hours before the departure of the post, I receive Your Serene Highness's two letters of the 18th with the enclosure from Eberhardt. I can do nothing further in the matter than to lament Your Serene Highness's fate — this Your Serene Highness will surely deign to perceive yourself, most gracious Prince and Lord, that without a compromise with the short princesses nothing more can be said here, all the more since the Prince Chancellor has already been lied to in this, that it was asserted with confidence that the Weilers and 17 wished to come to terms — and if the affair cannot be settled now, while the ladies are in want of money, then, best of Princes, they are thereby [uncertain: reduced] 300000 Rthl the poorer. Herr p. Eberhard is right that his coming here would be most necessary. Only we can beg for him here neither a passport nor, still less, money — but if he comes here, then everything shall be arranged for him and he shall be placed beyond all responsibility. He needs only a passport from Your Serene Highness and from the magistrate there of some border town, and if he travelled with his own horses, then the devil would ask after him — the affair must now be compromised in one way or another, and I neither can nor will take that upon myself alone. — For the rest, it may yet be that I, together with my friends, shall before long have Herr Cosmar's swan-song and the panegyric of the obliging spirits made public — — if it is possible in the world, and if I am not misled from other quarters, then before God I keep my word, and I go on toward the goal so long as it takes until I reach it; and if this, which I already see in the [uncertain: Güste], is even in some measure attained, then assuredly every scoundrel will want to become a [uncertain: knight] in the business and will boast of having done Your Serene Highness God knows what favours in the world. But I stand with my honour for it that not one of them has troubled the wheels about Your Serene Highness, still less ever gone to bed [uncertain: this year] or watched through sleepless nights on your account, good Prince. Meanwhile I beg that among those people there be not counted a Hahn, Paulle, or Lombardini, for these may well often have had the sun rather than the morning's bread — for themselves and their children in the house. Think, noble and exalted Prince, indeed [uncertain: therein] of these faithful men — I know they suffer no tutoring, but hear the counsel of an honest fellow who hates flattery and not, as

my old judge would have you believe — enough of this, God will and can help. That Prince August has accepted the post of president, which all men of honour have declined, is heart-shattering — for now they will hold him here no longer for weak but for — mad. The Congress has forbidden the King of Würtemberg everything and referred them to a general constitution. Even every power of attorney of all the Estates and persons assembled there has comprehended the protestation within itself. How does the man come (when I myself have warned Your Serene Highness against him) to blow now in the King's horn, and to assume the character which everyone else pushed away from himself — what will Langenburg, what will Prince Kirchberg and others say of this young man, that he accepts the painted charge which everyone else declined from himself with steadfastness and magnanimity — O, that is a wise father of his country! The day before yesterday a letter went off to Prince August, and one to Berlin, that the 100000 Rthl are to be handed over to him, but absolutely in cash (for perhaps Cosmar will want to give paper) — you did indeed will it, and I still doubt that he will withdraw the power of attorney. To all else I say nothing further than that I refer to the letters sent off these 4 days past. Barbe's servant Your Serene Highness will indeed have dispatched, as I have begged — he takes my horses, and you will have a firm basket-carriage given him there, with a tarpaulin. Then send also the two-horse wagon to Prag to fetch the glass — lastly deign to send the drummer and Waurzyn back to Blizanow with a post and a little [uncertain: white] money. According to today's news Napoleon presses ever more toward Paris — the King [uncertain: is placing] an army at Mevin, which happened at Grenoble and Lion also — but [uncertain: Kapon] is so cunning that where danger threatens him he goes around it — and since one might not be minded to earn the premium of 12 millions of francs set upon his head, he might well succeed in paying his visit in Paris. Judgments upon it are very various — and the confusion is great. No one finds himself better off here in this than the stock-jobbers. God, were it only possible to send Eberhard here. Most respectfully I remain, until death, Your Serene Highness's — Wien, the 26th of March 1815. Glenck I found out today; he commends himself to your grace, and wishes Your Serene Highness in the Op[text lost] at once[text lost] most faithful servant v Triebenfeld
