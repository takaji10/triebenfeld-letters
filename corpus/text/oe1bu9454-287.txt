--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Written amid the mobilisation against Bonaparte, this reports on Prince Adolph's decision to enter military service: Triebenfeld will approach the King before his departure on the 18th to learn where Adolph should join him and what uniform to have made, expecting a place in the Guard. He defends Hardenberg's slowness on the Prince's business, refuses to show the Hahns' threatening letter, notes Lombardini's and von Beugelin's efforts, awaits Pourtales, and lists the allies' agreed contingents totalling 1,307,000 men.

Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Lord!

Your Serene Highness's most highly esteemed letter of the 3rd of April I received this morning early. [uncertain: Hungary] I saw that the letter to me was not enclosed in a cover, it lay merely in the envelope addressed to the Post Director Mann, naturally he unsealed it and despite his assurance he has certainly read it — even B found that most disagreeable — for one does not let just anyone [uncertain: cool himself in the chart].

In short, it was a wretched business.

Now to the matter: regarding P. Adolph's resolve, I had just now a letter nearly finished in order to advise Your Serene Highness to this. God be thanked that my only wish is that P

How in these sentiments we met one another.

Adolph may still catch the King here, for it is said that the King departs and the Prince H remains here still to arrange everything —

Should this indeed not happen, then I shall go to the King and report to him Your Serene Highness's and Adolph's resolve, and ask him whither he commands that Adolph shall follow him, and in what manner he may equip himself.

For the rest, should he come, I shall already tell him what he has to do. — F H and M H: must on no account be mixed into military affairs, that would be a sin against the Holy Ghost. By this means an even better purpose may be attained. Would to God Adolph were already here.

Now to your affairs — your [uncertain: Irrenrieden] cut my heart in two; but I can compel neither the Prince nor anyone to set aside matters where the weal and woe of millions of men is at stake and to take up your business instead — when the Prince answers to [uncertain: Liende] entreaty — God, I shall certainly help the man to whom I am a true friend before I depart from here — then I, and every one of your friends, must fall silent in reverence. I certainly seek out every road on which I may hope with confidence to reach the goal. Only at this juncture one must not be unjust. It is known that everything stands at hazard, let that be considered — and he is, by God, the only one

who thinks day and night. It is enough for me that he protests his will to help Your Serene Highness. I certainly suffer more than you do with those about you; and so my heart could burst when I receive an enclosure of that kind. Lombardini wrote lately as was fitting — the Privy Councillor of State von Beugelin (from whom Your Serene Highness must now absolutely have an answer) took this affecting letter and gave it to the Chancellor. By God it has not failed of its purpose — but the letter of the two Hahns I certainly must not show, in the judgement of both friends. It contains threats such as even the friend and the upright man does not tolerate. — I acknowledge the burning zeal of the honest Hahn, but this does not suit the course of things — there are men of consequence here these 5 months and they are not yet to have their first audience. — How, and perhaps in centuries, has there been an epoch so important as the present one. The French affair is now the affair of the whole nation — millions will stand against one another, and General [uncertain: Zünger] will often make the greatest diversions, and therefore desperation will hold the helm. It is a good fortune that the harmony of the sovereigns endures — Austria acts assuredly in a great and gallant manner — this has already proved itself in Italy. But on the Upper Rhine matters still look very bad — what one is to think of the Russians one does not know. — Their march goes very [uncertain: schencken förmig] — the Emperor Alexander seems to take little notice of it, and thinking men shook their heads. — The Viceroy of Italy is gone. The scene that occurred must have had its effect upon the citizens, where several barred the Emperor's way and asked him how he could go about so intimately, arm in arm, publicly by day and by night with such a traitor and scoundrel; indeed, where one of the citizens was for the two of them and the gentlemen had to retire into a house — the wretch has gone to Bayreuth on his word of honour,

that the citizens were in the right has now proved true, and the King of Württemberg has had occasion to show himself with [uncertain: Caropin] — the affair was this: a courier coming from Paris hastens through Stuttgart — the King learns of it quickly enough. He has the fellow arrested at once and the dispatches taken from him — he unseals them and finds among them the letter from the Hortenau, in which she writes to Eugen: Continuez d'amusez notre imbécille.

Now, the talk was of the [uncertain: Mißischen] Emperor — the King of Württemberg sent this pretty correspondence here at once by courier, which arrived here the day before yesterday; what sensation this news has made is not yet known. The various amusements continue meanwhile, notwithstanding that Bonaparte has already some 300000 men assembled and Murat is pressing ever further forward. I fear that one rates one's enemy too low, and that the Russian commanders wish to draw the war far out, in order to pursue their rapacity and greed. — In a word, the most active man of all is Prince Hardenberg; for the King certainly cares and thinks with those about him about everything that concerns the military; and as it seems, the gentleman burns with eagerness to let the enemy feel his avenging arm. The King goes from here by way of Berlin to Frankfurt am Main — some even believe that he will lead the advance guard — that would surely be too much hazarded. It is incredible how gallant the gentleman is. Only a sovereign ought surely to spare himself somewhat, and in his truly praiseworthy [conduct] keep the survey of the whole before his eyes.

This very moment I hear that the King departs on the 18th; if that really happens I shall go to him beforehand and lay before him Your Serene Highness's and Adolph's resolve, and beg him to determine whither Adolph is to come, and what uniform he is to have made for himself. It is believed he will certainly take him into his Guard, for a young, brisk, wealthy and able man is a rare thing

I say to Your Serene Highness that one will soon be able to see clearly about France's fate. I wondered that you have no news at all there. Concerning the resolve of the sovereigns there is nothing more to say; it stands firm as a rock — each has declared how many troops he gives and by when they are to be put in the field. Thus Austria gives, by [uncertain: Ober Johann], 200000, who are to stand on the Rhine on the 20th of May, and 120000 for Italy, of whom 48000 are already standing there; Russia gives in all 270000 men — Prussia 200000, of which now more than 8000 stand there; [uncertain: haguland] gives 76000 men — Hanover 30000 men, Spain 100000, Portugal according to the treaty concluded the day before yesterday 30000 men, Sardinia 30000, Denmark 25000 men, [uncertain: beim] 40000, Württemberg 20000, Holland 40000 men, the Electorate of Hesse has not yet declared itself, any more than [uncertain: daran ſtadt]; Saxony must furnish 12000 men — Switzerland 30000 men. Mecklenburg, the Hessian lands, Weimar, Gotha and whatever the gentlemen are all called, together 60000 — the Grand Duke of Baden is to furnish 12000 men — but he cries out that he has no money. — Sweden is quite silent. The whole army of the allies amounts, according to the agreement fixed by treaty, to 1307000 men; against it stands, but for a few small heaps, the whole of France. From the 20th of May all are to press into France. Bonaparte is, as Your Serene Highness knows, declared an outlaw, and his fellow-ruffians or so-called marshals along with him as men without honour. Nothing in France is to be spared. This the Emperor of the Russians has already publicly declared. — All that would be well, but the delaying is good for nothing. — Can the proscribed man not play the prævenire somewhere? and will not the Bonapartist hordes, since they see their death before their eyes, all grow desperate and fight? and what will Field Marshal Hunger say at the last? Whose manoeuvres one ought surely to fear the most. — From Russia much grain and cattle are said to be coming. — I credit [uncertain: the Wünschen] ministers and the government with little good. They will plunder everything away here and our troops will suffer want. — It is said too that even if Bonaparte were given up, still the advance into France goes forward. For the rest, all this seems to hinder Pourtales's arrival here. He has as yet let nothing further be heard of himself. If it does not succeed now, then have your paper returned to you at once. Just so does it stick fast now with the [uncertain: Rohr] affairs — Prince [uncertain: Rodecurii] goes to Posen to receive the homage, and Zerboni goes with him — but it is believed that all this will take on another shape. The Poles are beginning to grow very loud again, and thereby many a thing will get another name. Nevertheless I shall hear what comfort the Privy Councillor of State will give in this matter the day after tomorrow. That Your Serene Highness at last suffers want there I do believe, and feel it a thousandfold, for I suffer it along with my own people — I have neither rest nor dwelling in seeking to relieve the distress, and I hope that my endeavour will succeed — but the [uncertain: Vorblünten] reproaches / that you have sent money here, that you lose patience / that can please me no longer. I certainly lose ten times more than you have sent, and still I hold fast to patience, in order to save you, my Prince, which will also come to pass within a short time. 2. In deepest reverence I remain

Your Serene Highness's

Vienna, the 13th of April

1815.

humble and obedient servant

v Triebenfeld
