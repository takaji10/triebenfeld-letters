--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Regrets missing the Count's passage to Erfurth and lacking money for the journey, doubting that the meeting of the two Emperors will settle anything; plans to borrow a few thousand and attach himself to the Emperors and the Grand Duke, through whom he will press the Prince's cause. Reports Herr v. Stein's fall and Kalckreuth's manoeuvring, urges the Prince to go to Silesia or Königsberg to seek the King, and notes the French Government's partial lifting of the South Prussian sequestration, with monies to come via Warsaw and Kalisz; demands Metzig collect 8,000–9,000 Rthl of laudemial and purchase monies.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord, I have had the honour of receiving Your Most Serene Highness's most gracious letter. I regret nothing more than that this could not happen 4 days sooner, since just then the Count was passing through here and on to Erfurth, and it would have been an easy matter for me to attach myself to him and make that journey with him. Today it is [uncertain: useful] to set out on the road to Erfurth, because it is entirely uncertain how long the two Emperors will remain there; besides, I also lack the [uncertain: Canzes] money to make the journey there, and with empty hands nothing can be done in that place. Moreover I believe that in Erfurth not everything will yet be decided; I mean that there they will only first draw everything up and then deal out the cards for play - It depends who plays along, and whether the greatest player does not, just as well as our master, lose his game codille. The alliance between England, Sweden, Austria and Turkey is considerable, and may perhaps compel Russia to steps which at present it does not suspect it will take. My every thought and endeavour is only that Your Most Serene Highness might be set again in the right place, and to that end I shall in silence contribute everything possible

I was on the road to Königsberg but had to turn back here again. Now I am seeking to borrow some few thousand in money, and with these I shall watch for the return of the 2 Emperors and the Grand Duke. To these I shall attach myself and go with them wherever it may be, cost what it will. Through the Grand Duke above all I shall work on Your Serene Highness's behalf - I have gained [uncertain: one man's] experience, and this I shall put to use, and when the chief matters of the monarchs have been dealt with, to petition the Grand Duke so long until he advances your affair; I have too often made the observation that he who speaks last keeps the right with great lords - [uncertain: Hjostein] has now sunk low, and I believe that Your Serene Highness's affairs will now shortly take the happiest issue. Before this man came forward and took the [uncertain: Muder] in hand, the King and the Queen were very well disposed toward Your Serene Highness, and at that time I hoped for the best. But as soon as Mr. von Stein spoke ambiguously about the command you had conducted, the tone of the court changed as well. Meanwhile I believe there will now soon be a complete want of men who can conduct the [uncertain: Nider] rationally. Therefore people will doubtless look about again for the one man whom nature herself has [uncertain: pelt] for [uncertain: homage], and will have to strive to fetch him in. The hypocritical Kalckreuth is becoming the old W. and was to step forward. There is no one in the state who has meant so honestly by the King as Your Serene Highness. Everyone combines with his actions only his own interest and seeks his pockets

to fill. All tread softly so as to keep themselves in their places and feed themselves well. There is no one who tells the monarch the truth and goes straight at it. Count [uncertain: Lottore] and Count [uncertain: Lohen] are a pair of upright men, but both must proceed with the utmost caution, since otherwise they might easily be laid out in skat - My unpresumptuous advice is that Your Serene Highness should now do nothing at Erfurth but wait and see what happens in the great matters, for as sure as there is a God things will not stay as they are, and in a flash everything may change - the sole endeavour of Your Serene Highness and of your adherents must now be that you first come back into place. Why will you not go to Silesia? I would do it, and report to the King that I was there and that I would come to him. I stake my life upon it - if the King seeks Your Serene Highness again, then everything is at once in its track - all the more if [uncertain: Konckerten] or the Emperor shall have done some preparatory work. You know the King: he has not the heart to propose anything to you, most gracious Lord. Besides, he now holds himself to be poor and stripped of everything; but if you go to meet him and tell him that you wish now to help and advise him, yet by no means to be burdensome to him, then he will idolize Your Serene Highness. What a piece of good fortune it would be if you were at present in Königsberg when the Emperor returns. Everything would go better and be negotiated with more courage.

The present [uncertain: Alantones] of the K. would be mightily terrified at your appearance, but soon every one of them would bow

deeply before you - let Your Serene Highness take this well to heart, procure where it is possible some few thousand together and then take this road courageously - You will not fail, as sure as God is. The South Prussian affairs are, as I have written to Your Serene Highness before, set in train in the best way; the French Government has lifted a part of the sequestration and ordered the revenues to be paid over. The matter has been sent to Warsaw to the French Chargé d'Affaires, so that he should make this known to the Ministry of Justice with the intimation that the moneys are to be allowed to pass unhindered. As soon as this has been made public from Warsaw in Kalisz, I shall see to it that you receive moneys from there.

The letters to Lichnowski and Metzig are good and entirely suited to the matter. [uncertain: Schnomchi] belongs at [uncertain: Traben] and there he must wait until the times change. To Mr. Metzig I would write separately and set him right in such a way that he should long remember it. The burgesses there owe, in various transfer fees (Laudemium) and of the 4000 Rthl remaining purchase sum, upward of 8 to 9000 Rthl; why does the good-for-nothing not drive it in together and report that the money is there? Then we should soon see how it might be got out. The rogue did, after all, receive from my hands at the time, when he helped conduct the business with the burgesses, 200 in cash, which I gave the fellow out of my own pocket. Now he must do something for it, or he shall feel it. In deepest reverence I remain, until death,

Ber[lin?] the 20th of September 1808

Your Most Serene Highness's

humble and faithful servant

Peter
