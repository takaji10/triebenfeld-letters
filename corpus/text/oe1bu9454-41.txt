--- SUMMARY (German) ---
Triebenfeld erhielt das Schreiben zu spät, um mit dem Grafen nach Erfurt zu reisen, und hat kein Bargeld. Er will Geld borgen und bei der Rückkehr der Kaiser über den Großfürsten für den Fürsten wirken. Das französische Gouvernement habe die Sequestration in Südpreußen teilweise aufgehoben und die Freigabe der Revenuen befohlen. Lichnowski gehöre nach Trąbczyn und müsse dort abwarten. Melzig soll zurechtgewiesen werden, weil er die Schulden der Bürger von 8 bis 9000 Rthl nicht eintreibt.

--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Triebenfeld received the letter too late to travel to Erfurt with the Count, and he has no cash. He means to borrow money and, when the Emperors return, to work through the Grand Duke on the Prince's behalf. He says the French government has partly lifted the sequestration in South Prussia and ordered the revenues released. He says Lichnowski belongs at Trąbczyn and must wait there. Melzig is to be reprimanded for not collecting the townspeople's debts of 8 to 9,000 Rthl.

Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

I have had the honour to receive Your Most Serene Highness's most gracious letter. I regret nothing more than that this could not have happened 4 days earlier, since the Count was just then passing through here on his way to Erfurt, and it would have been an easy matter for me to attach myself to him and to make that tour with him. Today it is useful to set out upon the road to Erfurt, because it is quite uncertain how long the two Emperors will tarry there; besides, I also lack the ready money to make the journey thither, and with empty hands nothing can be done there. Moreover I believe that at Erfurt not everything will yet be decided; I mean that there they will only first draft everything and then deal out the cards for play — It depends upon who plays along, and whether the greatest player does not, quite as much as our Lord, lose his game codille. The alliance between England, Sweden, Austria and Turkey is considerable, and may perhaps compel Russia to steps which it does not now suspect it will take. My striving and endeavour is only that Your Most Serene Highness might be set again in the right place, and to that end I shall in quiet contribute everything possible

I was on the road to Königsberg but had to turn back hither again. Now I am seeking to borrow some thousands in money, and with these I shall watch for the return of the 2 Emperors and the Grand Duke. To these I shall attach myself and go with them wherever it may be, cost what it will. Through the Grand Duke above all I shall work on Your Serene Highness's behalf — I have gained experience, and this I shall put to use, and when the principal affairs of the monarchs have been transacted, to beg the Grand Duke so long until he advances your cause; I have too often made the observation that with great lords it is he who speaks last who carries the day — Hjostein has now sunk low, and I believe that Your Serene Highness's affairs will now shortly take the happiest turn. Before this man came forward and took the helm in hand, the King and the Queen were very well disposed towards Your Serene Highness, and at that time I hoped for the best. But as soon as Mr von Stein spoke ambiguously of the command you had conducted, the tone of the court changed also. Meanwhile I believe there will now soon be a complete want of men who can steer the helm sensibly. Therefore they will surely look about again for the one man whom nature herself has [uncertain: pult] to homage, and will have to endeavour to fetch him hither. The hypocritical Kalckreuth will to the old W. and was to step forward. There is no one in the State who has meant so honestly by the King as Your Serene Highness. Everyone joins to his actions only his own interest and seeks to line his pockets

to line. All tread softly so as to keep themselves in their places and to feed themselves well. There is none who tells the monarch the truth and goes straight at it. Count Lottom[?] and Count Palzen are a pair of upright men, but both must proceed with the utmost caution, because otherwise they might easily be laid aside in the skat — My unpresumptuous counsel is that Your Serene Highness now do nothing at Erfurt but wait to see what will happen in the great matters, for things will not remain as they are, as surely as there is a God, and everything may change in a flash — the sole endeavour of Your Serene Highness and of your adherents must now be that you first come back into your place. Why will you not go to Silesia? I should do it, and should notify the King that I was there and that I would come to him. I stake my life upon it — if the King seeks Your Serene Highness again, then everything is at once back in its track — all the more if Constantin or the Emperor shall have prepared something beforehand. You know the King: he has not the heart to propose anything to you, most gracious Lord. Besides, he now holds himself to be poor and stripped of everything; but if you can come to meet him and tell him that you will now help and advise, yet will not for a while be burdensome to him, then he will idolise Your Serene Highness — What a piece of good fortune it would be if you were at present in Königsberg when the Emperor returns. Everything would go better and would be negotiated with more courage.

The present entourage of the K. would be mightily alarmed at your appearance, but soon everyone would bow deeply before you and Triebenfeld

deeply — let Your Serene Highness take this well to heart, procure where it is possible some thousands together and then go this road courageously — You will not fail, as surely as there is a God — the South Prussian affairs are, as I have written to Your Serene Highness before, set in the best train; the French government has lifted a part of the sequestration and has ordered the revenues to be released. The matter has been sent to Warsaw to the French chargé d'affaires, so that he may make it known to the Ministry of Justice with the intimation that the moneys are to be let pass unhindered. As soon as this has been made public from Warsaw at Kalisz, I shall see to it that you receive moneys from there.

The letters to Lichnowski and Metzig are good and entirely suited to the business. Lichnowski belongs at Trąbczyn and there he must wait until the times change. To Mr Melzig I should write separately besides, and set him to rights in such a manner that he shall long remember it. The burgesses there owe inq various transfer fee (Laudemium) moneys and, of the 4,000 Rthl of remaining purchase sum, over 8 to 9,000 Rthl; why does the villain not drive it in together and report that the money is there — then it would soon be seen how it was to be got out. The rascal did, from my own hands, at that time when he helped to conduct the business with the burgesses, receive 200 Rthl[?] d'or in cash, which I gave the fellow out of my own pocket. Now he must do something for it, or he shall feel it. In deepest reverence I remain, until death,

Your Most Serene Highness's

Berlin, the 20th September 1808

humble and faithful servant

Peter
