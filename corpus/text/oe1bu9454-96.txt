--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Reports from Berlin that Colonel v. Köckritz sends his respects and regards the exchange as good as approved and concluded, delayed only by the King's indisposition, which has suspended audience-reports. Urges the Prince to write to the King at once for leave for the Öhringen journey, sending the letter by express courier, and to travel via Berlin rather than Bohemia. Names the King, Köckritz and the Prince of Hessen Darmstadt as his only true friends; denounces Wedel in Breslau over his report on the Krotoszyn affair and an opinion that Prot. could bear 50000 Rthl, papers shown him by Cabinet Councillor Beyme. Secularisation business deferred.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---
Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Lord!

The truly venerable Colonel von Köckritz has repeatedly charged me to convey to Your Most Serene Highness his most obedient respects, and also to give this assurance, that the exchange is now to be regarded as good as approved and concluded. Also that the Monarch holds Your Serene Highness truly in very high esteem, and that He is Your most faithful admirer, and likewise that the matter would already have been concluded here by Bernhardi, had the indisposition of our good King not placed an obstacle in the way, on which account there is at present no audience for presenting it.

That I report this news to Your Most Serene Highness with the most thankfully joyful heart, I surely need not first protest at length to the same. May the Giver of all good things now grant You, most exalted great friend of mankind, the most lasting health, that Your Highness may long enjoy this good fortune, and especially that this may be the first source of blessing

from which Your Serene Highness may draw so many good pure springs as wash away every affliction and suffering into complete oblivion; and then, when Providence grants this my truly sincere wish, then, most gracious Lord, preserve for me your favour and goodwill, which I shall always strive with the most ardent zeal to deserve.

Concerning the journey to Öhringen I have likewise spoken this morning with the Colonel. The Colonel wishes that Your Most Serene Highness might write with the utmost haste to His Majesty and apply for the leave of absence.

My counsel, then, is that Your Highness should write to the King immediately upon receipt of this, and convey the letter to me by express courier; I shall attend to it and hasten it back again, so that Your Serene Highness may then take your road here by way of Berlin and at once render thanks to the King as well. This is, apart from myself, the counsel of the worthy Colonel von Köckritz, and especially that Your Serene Highness should not take your tour, say, through Bohemia, but by way of Berlin.

I reckon now that my letter will be in Your Serene Highness's hands on Friday morning. May Your Highness deign in that very hour to write to the King for the leave of absence, and send me the letter by express courier; I shall receive it on Sunday towards noon, give it the same day to the Colonel, on Monday I receive the answer, and with it I shall arrive in Breslau on Wednesday.

With regard to the secularisation business I shall then lay before Your Serene Highness my unpresumptuous opinions. At present there is nothing to be done in the matter here, and I am of opinion that here too [this] may be the place for pressing forward this business. — German. The Prince of Hesse-Darmstadt, Serene Highness, commends himself to your favour. At bottom Your Serene Highness has only three true friends here: these are the King, Colonel von Köckritz, and the Prince of H. Dams[?]; all the others think much as the man of Breslau does. But among all the knaves, Wedel in Breslau still remains the greatest, for this wretched fellow has, with respect to the Krotoszyn affair, delivered a report or opinion such as can only have been hatched in the brain of an arch-liar and villain. Upon this opinion the other gentleman, who would have it that he has done so much, has then likewise given his opinion in writing to the effect that Prot. could readily bear 50000 Rthl — and these infamies alone have cost me so frightfully much trouble to overcome. The honest Cabinet Councillor Beyhm let me read the papers in the Cabinet myself.
