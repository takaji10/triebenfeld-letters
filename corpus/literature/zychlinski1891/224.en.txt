Count Mieczysław, the known composer of the "Kujawiaki", known throughout the country and founded on folk songs, ended his life suddenly on 16 March 1887 at Włocławek and was buried there. Having received the most careful upbringing in his parents' house and in schools of the first rank, and having then visited foreign capitals and countries, the late Mieczysław came back to his native soil as a young man richly endowed with every quality that opens the way into the first society and makes a man the favourite of the salons. A distinguished family, an ample fortune, a splendid and truly lordly figure, a face of regular and handsome features, rare courtesy and warmth of manner, set in the most refined forms, and with all this the charm of a soul breathing poetry, feeling everything that is lofty and delighting above all in everything that is of the homeland, and lastly an uncommon musical artistry, won the late Mieczysław a wide circle of friends and gained him the hand of a maiden as beautiful as she was radiant with womanly virtues. This unclouded domestic happiness was broken by the death of his beloved wife, and twelve years later by that of his only daughter, after barely two years of married life. Almost at the same time as this bright star went out in his lonely widower's life, Count Mieczysław's sight went out too. He went blind; but even this terrible blow could not break his life. On the contrary, from then on he lived a doubled inner life. The serenity of a clear conscience and an upright character spread over his face; his mind kept the freshness of youthful flight, which flowed from under his fingers in a broad stream of melody when in hours of longing and loneliness he sat down at the piano, or sparkled in quickness of view, deeper learning and knowledge of the world and of men, and in a wit that wounded no one, when he found himself among relations, friends and acquaintances. One often had to admire this aged but hale old man, who bore the most grievous infirmity on earth with such calm of spirit and such ease! Deeply attached, as we have said, to his native soil and its tradition, he knew Polish literature excellently and delighted in its treasures. He liked to listen to the songs of the country people, and from this folk yarn, from these simple flowers of feeling, he spun and then set his "Kujawiaki", now sad and tender like the quiet murmur of a spring rain, now full of that dashing verve by which the melodies of our people are marked. And so Miączyński's "Kujawiaki" will always remain a lasting monument to him in the field [printed "dzisdzinie", for dziedzinie] of Polish music, and will be a precious source of folk song for our composers to come. His was a noble and strong nature; he was an oak of past centuries amid the stunted generation of today, and he too fell, unshaken to the last moment by the storm of misfortunes, like a mighty oak that only a sudden thunderbolt from heaven or a cruel, treacherous axe can bring to the ground.
