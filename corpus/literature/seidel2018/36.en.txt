He further added "that every enthusiast should receive free and unencumbered land on which to build, that he should be exempt from military billeting, that he need fear no monopoly, but rather that free trade and commerce should be exercised." Whoever could not build himself could purchase newly built houses, cheaply and ready to move into.[^19] The founding of the suburb represents an admirable testament to industrial settlement.

The first task was to win applicants for settlement, and this initially stalled. The bailiff (Amtmann) Taurinus and Court Councillor L. Braun had made precise calculations as to which occupational fields would be needed, after they had divided "the local circumstances here, in thought, [...] into classes." Active in the town were: "a red-tanner, a fustian-weaver, a stocking-weaver, a hatter, a soap-boiler, a sculptor, a bell-founder, a clockmaker, a stocking-knitter, a wool-spinner, a gunsmith, a coppersmith, a rope-maker, a potter, a brick-maker, two millers, a paver, two nail-smiths, a bookbinder, a button-maker, three shopkeepers, a brewer, a confectioner." It was precisely this "class" that needed to be increased, since it depended least on the town and its immediate surroundings.

Applicants were carefully selected; thus drinkers were not accepted (FL had informants sitting in the inns!). One craftsman who was indeed "a good worker, but who had shown himself to be a drunkard and had already gotten wildly and thoroughly drunk several times in Ingelfingen" was shown the door (of the town) without leniency. In 1787 a quite different decision had to be made: a Catholic, subject of the Comburg, had applied as the first non-Protestant for permission to settle. He was accepted; the Prince had expressed himself on the matter as follows: "[...] if his character is good and honest, then the Catholic religion is not at all offensive to me, and I am certainly as much for tolerance as anyone can be."[^20]
