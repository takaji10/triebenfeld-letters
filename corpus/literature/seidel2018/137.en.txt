# Seidel (2018), p. 137
# translation by Claude

> *the Prince, to whom I have in the meantime written twice, did not answer me with so much as a syllable and abandons me entirely, as he does his saline works [...] It is now exactly 30 years since the first work [by the Glenck family] was done on the works here. All exertions were in vain, all costs appear to have been fruitlessly expended.*

The present-day reader of these letters may gain the impression that Glenck needed someone to whom he could communicate his worries. August placed little trust in the whole undertaking and was "unwilling to contribute even 1 florin."

Glenck had discovered a gypsum deposit near Marbach from which he could draw profit. He continued the operation at Weißbach only briefly, for private investors. In Wimpfen he was then more successful. For the technique of boring, he had invented what was later named after him, the Glenck grab-bucket (a pear-shaped gripping device that was lowered into the shaft open and then closed, in order to bring up the material at the bottom — stones and the like). Glenck eventually became a wealthy man.

### 12.2 Excursus III: Glenck and Goethe

In Thuringia, where Glenck had lived since 1823, he also searched for salt deposits and acquired a prospecting concession from Goethe, who was responsible for such matters. He struck it lucky near Stotternheim, north of Erfurt. Goethe himself was interested in minerals. Systematic extraction began in 1828. Glenck informed Goethe that he had "at a depth of 1170 feet in a borehole [...] encountered, in wholly pure form, according to the fragment, partly granular and partly foliated crystalline salt."[^105] Goethe wrote about this in June 1828 to a certain Kaspar von Sternberg, saying that Glenck was "just now about to erect the necessary buildings." Goethe and Glenck met at the saline "Louisenhall" – as the Stotternheim works were called – and presumably also nearby in Buffleben.
