# Seidel (2018), p. 142
# translation by Claude

A "financial official" named Hans von Held uncovered abuses very early on, first in a poem:

> *To all the knaves their just reward*  
> *who cheat the state,*  
> *and out of rapacious lust around the throne*  
> *coil themselves like serpents.*

His book, bearing the cumbersome title "The True Jacobins in the Prussian State, or a Documentary Account of the Wicked Intrigues and Fraudulent Conduct in Office of Two Prussian State Ministers"[^109] (1800), was first largely redacted and then withdrawn from circulation by the authorities (owing to the extensive redactions, it is considered the first "black book"!). The author, who sought through this publication to force an investigation and who denounced above all the practices of Count Hoym, was imprisoned and convicted! Held had, however, also hit the mark: Count Hoym had been removed from office on 26 April 1798. As was usual at the time, rumors preceded such events, and, as if in proof of his own vigilance, Triebenfeld reported this to FL as well.[^110]

Distinctly critical voices concerning the events in South Prussia could already be read shortly after the reign of Frederick William III (Klöden, 1840)—though coupled with complaints about the ingratitude of the Poles toward the Prussian state. After 150 years it was then also established that Prussia had performed good work of development and administration in the thinly settled region, especially in the field of schooling.[^111] One should not simply assume that the new owners of the estates had thoroughly exploited the Poles who worked there (for the most part) for their livelihood. They, too, had been obliged to render dues to the previous owners, the monasteries and other public institutions.
