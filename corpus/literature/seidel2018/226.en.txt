The address did not refer to any kinship; the Houses of Hohenzollern and Hohenlohe had nothing to do with one another in that respect. The reason was probably their shared membership in the same generation within the high nobility.

When Frederick William II died, FL mourned his death, but soon noted also that the young successor, Frederick William III (24 years his junior), treated him well. FL would remain in close contact with him until his own death. The relationship was seriously disturbed only by FL's capitulation at Prenzlau, which the King could neither understand nor accept, and probably never forgave him for (see the remark to von Marwitz, p. 108). For several years afterward there was no personal exchange between them — FL was, after all, without military office at that time.

Immediately upon assuming his own reign in 1797, Frederick William III began systematic tours ("reviews") through the individual provinces of Prussia, and in the course of them naturally visited Breslau several times, where FL held his governorship. The festive pageantry staged by FL at Alt-Scheitnig is described in the corresponding section (see p. 70f.). In the few years up to 1806 (the Battle of Jena and Auerstedt), FL was at court several times, both in Potsdam and in Charlottenburg. At both palace complexes he was lodged in historically significant quarters, notably at Sanssouci, the "home" of his idol Frederick II. At the palace in Jena, it was not the King but the local ruler, Duke Charles Augustus, who likewise wished to give him preferential lodging. Although Privy Councillor Voigt in Weimar pointed out that Goethe had only just been given rooms there — Goethe had to give way. FL reported such marks of high regard in his letters; he relished them, as he did the King's visits to him in Breslau.
