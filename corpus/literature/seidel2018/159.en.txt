# Seidel (2018), p. 159
# translation by Claude

FL registered this constitutionally difficult situation in his former territory only at the margins of his attention, but he did correspond with August about it.

A whole series of privileges did remain with the princes: their private property was not touched, and all domains, the right of lower jurisdiction, hunting and fishing rights, and other things that "did not belong essentially to sovereignty" remained theirs; likewise tax exemption for their domains and estates. Of significance for FL, among other things, would have been: the requirement to leave all foreign military service (Prussia now being "foreign"). He would have been permitted to visit his estates abroad only with royal permission (though that would probably not have become a serious problem, since Prince August II already shuttled back and forth between Slaventzitz and Öhringen himself). He would have been obliged, under penalty of forfeiting a quarter of his income, to reside in Stuttgart for at least three months a year, and a good deal more besides.

On the ground in Stuttgart, the Estates Assembly was already in session — strictly speaking, without the basis of a constitution. Draft constitutions from the government authority (the King's Privy Council) were rejected for years, until finally a paper drawn up under his successor William I was accepted.
