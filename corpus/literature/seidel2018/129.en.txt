## 12 Another Contemporary and Companion

### 12.1 Father and Son Glenck. The Salines and the Search for Coal[^100]

For this we must first return to Hohenlohe. In Weißbach in the Kocher valley, a few kilometers downstream from Niedernhall, there had been salines since the Middle Ages — that is, facilities for extracting salt as a preservative. The raw material is brine, that is, strongly saline groundwater, which is either pumped up or emerges as spring water at the surface. Salt was for centuries an important commodity, including as an article of trade. From the eighteenth century onward there was, in addition, the drinking of brine as a curative water and the taking of brine baths, particularly in Ingelfingen.

Brine springs suitable for salt extraction existed at Niedernhall. From there the brine was conveyed in wooden pipes to Weißbach, where two salt works were operated. The salt works functioned on the principle of evaporating the water content of the brine (boiling down salt). This required fuel — wood. In the middle of the eighteenth century wood became scarcer, the salt works had fallen into poor condition, and profitability was in question, particularly since pricing was subject to regulation. The town of Niedernhall was, in the period relevant here, two-thirds in the possession of the Archbishopric of Mainz and one-third Hohenlohe property.

Friedrich Ludwig, already as Hereditary Prince, recognized the necessity of intervention; his father, Prince Heinrich August, gave him a free hand, as presumably did his princely uncle in Öhringen, to whom the Hohenlohe third there belonged (FL would purchase the Mainz share in 1799, becoming thereby "majority owner" of Niedernhall).
