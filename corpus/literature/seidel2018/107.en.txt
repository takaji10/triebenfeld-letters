# Seidel (2018), p. 107
# translation by Claude

> *tone: "You may well imagine that the aides-de-camp I choose for myself are dearer to me than those the King sends me! But whoever has a purpose such as yours will always be welcome to me!" Only through this did it become clear to me that my arrival had been most unwelcome, and now I understood why the Prince's old aides-de-camp had treated me with such extreme coldness. Perhaps they even feared that I might be a Berlin spy sent to headquarters. But all this lasted only a short while; the Prince became very well disposed toward me, and the aides-de-camp became my friends. [...]. This [Prince Hohenlohe], [...] immediately after the dissolution of his army, had gone from Gera to his principality of Oehringen. This had been occupied by the French, who heaped flatteries upon him, which he manfully rejected. He had more or less concluded his inheritance affairs when the Act of the Confederation of the Rhine appeared, by which he and his entire House were reduced to the status of subjects of his hitherto neighbor and fellow Imperial Estate, the Duke of Württemberg, who then also did not delay, with the help of the very same French who had just been flattering him so, in taking possession of the land. Now there was no remaining for him in the Empire any longer. He declared his still-underage son to be of age, ceded the government to him, and, since he was now nothing more than a Prussian general, he departed. How many men in that time would have been capable of acting thus? I ask: is there a second example of such self-denial? The grave of Prussia already stood open! How deeply the King must have felt this devotion of his commander! — He had, moreover, already corresponded with him by means of disguised mounted messengers sent to Oehringen [...].*

Concerning a conversation held with the King in Memel, at the conclusion of peace with England (January 1807) — after Jena and Auerstädt, and after Prenzlau, and after an adventurous flight in civilian dress
