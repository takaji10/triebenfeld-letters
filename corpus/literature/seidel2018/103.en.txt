# Seidel (2018), p. 103
# translation by Claude

FL came in third place.[^74] He was described in an article of ten printed pages, brief in comparison to the other persons. The overall tenor is — unfortunately — related to that of Friedrich von Cölln. Alongside rather little information of genuinely reportorial character, one notices, as with von Cölln, how often the author touches on matters that are only sparsely documented, or cannot be documented at all: "[...] we will therefore attribute it to his youth, that in the earliest period of his military career he distinguished himself only by his advantageous figure and by his virtuosity in riding." Of the same quality of communication was the statement about the marriage to the very young Countess von Hoym:

> *It is said that the Countess von Hoym did not sink into the arms of her husband as innocently as a large part of the public believed. [...] Both pursued divergent paths; and the natural consequence of this was that the prince's economic circumstances not only failed to improve, but even worsened further, and that the husband's debts, under which he nearly succumbed, in turn worked to worsen his domestic circumstances.*

How did the author know this in 1808 — do financial misery and marriage have anything to do with one another? We readers know it now!

Buchholz's portrayal of the already-mentioned War and Forestry Councillor von Triebenfeld, of whom more will be said in detail, was accurate.

Who could have advised FL to take his discharge? Buchholz mentioned this — does he mean Müffling? As a military man, FL is supposed to have lacked all strategic insight, and von Massenbach is said to have been placed at his side as the brains of the operation (Massenbach is likewise presented in Buchholz's work, on twenty-four printed pages, and in a wholly different tone). One wonders how Frederick William II, and also III, could have let FL rise so high in the military hierarchy, if he had indeed corresponded to this portrait
