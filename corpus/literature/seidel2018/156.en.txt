The letter later became, for FL, the starting point for a letter to His Royal Majesty of 5 April 1809, which survives in the archive as a draft. In it FL once again set out the circumstances that had given rise to Triebenfeld's arrest, and assured him that he had found a way out for a small remaining debt. This, however, relates only to the matter referred to above.

As General Plenipotentiary, Triebenfeld truly had a great deal to do. An activity report with a list of 13 points, which he sent to FL on 7 March 1809 from his country estate at Kontopp, is reproduced in the appendix in transcribed form (as far as possible) (p. 241). What strikes me as interesting, among other things, is that administrative officials from South Prussia (Zagorow) sought him out at his residence in these matters. In a postscript he wrote that he had had all of this recorded by a court, "in order to begin this my career in truly brilliant fashion." From this it might be concluded that Triebenfeld had left state service and was now devoting himself entirely to the business of a financial and real-estate adviser — presumably then with further clients as well.

### 14.3 The Proceedings before the Royal Prussian Kammergericht, 1811

An extensive case file, a suit brought by the Royal General Invalids' Fund, has survived as a record of the Berlin Kammergericht and is presented here, despite all difficulties of comprehension, as an example and in unabridged form — as testimony to the Prussian penchant for meticulousness — in the appendix on pp. 243f.

It stands to reason that the King had knowledge of FL's financial circumstances, and Triebenfeld was presumably obliged to carry out a precise survey of the debts. The corresponding schedules have survived. The legacy (bequest) of 80,000 florins was merely a rumor!
