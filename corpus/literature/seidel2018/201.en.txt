# Seidel (2018), p. 201
# translation by Claude

> *One thing among all that has been named still endures, one thing has forever remained ineradicable; one thing bears witness to her former existence and to herself and her convictions and articles of faith, her wishes and the state of her conscience, and her words and deeds. Can you then ask: Where? — Certainly not among us; only with God alone, the All-Knowing and All-Righteous, who metes out reward and punishment according to merit, and more surely than anyone else could.*

The passage "not among us; only with God alone," avoiding earthly judgment, occurring still in the final third of the text, in which "excess and dissolution" and "outrages and misdeeds" had been denounced, gives one somewhat pause, but is probably meant rather abstractly, without reference to any real facts of her life. And at the close of the address the theme of "sin" arises — as is not unusual in a funeral address, and often almost formulaic:

> *Whoever among us is without sin, let him take up the nearest stone and cast it at her. [...] but our own heart, conscious of its shame before God, thus implores and beseeches him, the Lord of Mercy, most fervently, to be infinitely gracious to the late departed Princess Mother in His judgment for the sake of Jesus the Savior, [...] to grant her imperishable peace and let His divine countenance shine forever upon her transfigured spirit, should this not already have come to pass. Amen.*

Indeed, it constitutes the value and the merit of such an address that, even without further specifics, the listener is stirred to reflection — on the deceased, and also on himself. To offer comfort to the bereaved is likewise a meaningful purpose of such a sermon; the matter then concerns less the person of the deceased than the living — as is the case here.
