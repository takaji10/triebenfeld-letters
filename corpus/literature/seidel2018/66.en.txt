In later times there were few such texts from him addressing matters of fundamental importance. In this he was quite unlike his companion in the Prussian army, Colonel von Massenbach (see Chapter 22 on this). At most, among the surviving texts, one might place here his remarks and thoughts on the politico-military balance in Europe, more a strategic memorandum. He set these out in a letter to the publicist Böttiger, with whom he was evidently on friendly terms, in 1805.[^43]

Every residence had a court household attached to it; it could be lavish or modest — in the case of Count Christian Kraft and FL's father, Heinrich August, probably rather modest. For 1738, that is, during the father's reign, a register of the court personnel survives listing 102 persons. When FL moved his (military) headquarters from Ansbach in 1796 to his seat of government (which was, at the time, Ingelfingen), this required a considerable expansion of the court household there. In these times of relative economic hardship, this also had an effect on the prices of foodstuffs, especially meat; every week, slaughter cattle had to be bought up from the Margraviate of Ansbach. There was actually an export ban on this in that territory, but the government (under Hardenberg) lifted it. Karl August von Hardenberg visited FL in Ingelfingen. The two men presumably conversed about high politics, but also about the plans, already under way, for the economic development of the residence town — Alexander von Humboldt, too, was there once.

But already in 1796 FL received a new command, again in Breslau. He moved his court household there as well. Many of the Ingelfingen servants were left behind without employment; they received a temporary continuation of their wages.
