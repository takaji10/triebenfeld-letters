FL's task was to convene the first assembly of the shareholders. Interest and confidence in such an undertaking were great; throughout the Empire shares ("Kuxe", these being securities certifying a share in a mine) were bought, and local lordships also took part.

After the death of Johann Georg Glenk, he was soon succeeded by his son Carl Christian Friedrich Glenck [sic]. He had received a good education for his father's profession: attendance at the Hohe Karlsschule in Stuttgart, study at the University of Erlangen (law, together with mineralogy and geology out of personal interest). He continued to pursue the natural sciences and, from 1799, studied at the Mining Academy at Freiberg in Saxony.

Glenck the younger then became first private secretary to Friedrich Ludwig, his father's employer, and from 1803 worked as Princely Hohenlohe Justice and Revenue Bailiff (Amtmann), presumably chiefly as Director of Salines, resident at Weißbach. He thus had charge of the salt works there — and, with the money from the share issue and the knowledge of mining he had acquired at Freiberg, he continued the search for coal, also committing his own money to the effort.

Coal pits were to be created, among other places at Hermersberg above Niedernhall and Weißbach. Six shafts were sunk there. What was extracted, however, was only inferior material, "which for the most part scarcely deserved the name of coal."[^102] Moreover, a great deal of water broke into the shafts. Glenck set about building a tunnel intended to run some 1.5 kilometers from the valley, meant to drain off the water — the "Ziegelei-Stollen" (Brickworks Adit). In a letter of 15 July 1804 he reports to FL on progress in this work, and he also mentions the name of the English mining expert Baildon. Unfortunately it is not clear whether the latter had by then also brought his knowledge to bear at Weißbach, or whether FL had merely mentioned him earlier on his own account.
