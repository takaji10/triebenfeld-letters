What had moved the mother Marianne to take this step? The Hohenlohe biographer Adolf Fischer, who is well acquainted with the letters in the archive at Neuenstein, reports that she had "observed, with the deepest silence, her entire relationship to her former husband" (but see "Ein Wort des Vertrauens", p. 83f.). She did not, however, remain silent about her hatred of the French Revolution, which had allegedly led to the separation from her children, nor about the "fatal King of France."[^12]

This statement, with its high degree of abstraction, does not really get us anywhere. The usual grounds for separation today were not relevant for the nobility of that time. Childlessness in a marriage was often a reason. Extramarital affairs, even ones resulting in a child, were not unusual. Above all, when claims arose from them, they were kept quiet or simply passed over — though sometimes, too, they were treated quite openly. In any case, quite apart from whether affairs gave Marianne grounds for a separation (probably not), there is evidence of illegitimate children of FL's, which is presented in an Excursus beginning on p. 27.
