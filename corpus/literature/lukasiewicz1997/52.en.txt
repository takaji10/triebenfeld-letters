# lukasiewicz1997  52  en
# Łukasiewicz (1997), p. 52
# translation by Claude

Prussian officials were gladly made use of by Feliks Łubieński, with a demonstrative dislike of them. After 1806 they were indispensable for the continuation of the work of the administration of the Duchy of Warsaw.

If then the state of the Prussian administration in South Prussia was regarded at the time as lamentable, we cannot ignore the fact that in relation to the arrangements before the partitions the very manner of the organisation of the state institutions brought enormous progress and was appreciated by the Poles, which revealed itself bluntly precisely in the Duchy of Warsaw. On the one hand, then, moderation in criticism is necessary; on the other it is hard not to notice that neither in the times of Frederick II nor of Frederick William III did any other Prussian province enjoy such a bad name, as far as the condition of the administrative apparatus goes. In 1800, after the complaint of 77 citizens of Kalisz against the local tax councillor Joachim Denso, who committed financial robberies, Minister Voss judged it necessary, "in a province which possesses so many bad officials", to treat this offence exemplarily and punished Denso with three years of fortress, annulled however in the second instance. The German inhabitants of Rawicz, after the removal of councillor Below, sent in 1797 a deputation to the minister, which asked for an official "honest, not acting for his own advantage and against his subjects"[^122].

Soon after taking power, on 23 XI 1797, Frederick William III ordered the carrying out in the state administration of thorough purges. To be dismissed immediately were to be all the dishonest, those deprived of qualifications, the idle[^123].

There is no doubt that in the first rank these words related to South Prussia. After the journey through Southern Great Poland in 1798 the king wrote of the legacy of Hoym's rule that the Poles have nothing to thank it for[^124]. Again and in far sharper words, referring to the letter of 1798, Frederick William returned to the question of the fatal state of the administration in 1800, where he wrote of the almost wholly extinct spirit of fidelity, selflessness, diligence and order, for which the Prussian "civil service" had once been famed. From 1798 only small steps had been taken — he asserted — or indeed nothing had been done to change this state of things[^125].

After 1797 the estates given away remained however in the hands of the donataries. Frederick William III assured that he would act according to the law and would not trample on the documents signed by the Prussian king[^126].
