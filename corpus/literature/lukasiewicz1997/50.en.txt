Baron von Schilden received the estates from the starosty of Kościan, which before the insurrection had belonged to Józef Chłapowski. He resold them to that same starosta (see the Appendix, Tabela 4, s. 61), a known speculator, who drove the family estate to the edge of bankruptcy, whose spectre only his son Dezydery was to push away[^108]. Of course the legal norms intended to hinder the taking over of donative estates by Poles had an anti-Polish dimension — as Jan Wąsicki writes[^109]. It must however be noticed that the very fact of the issuing of such ordinances meant that the donataries were not very anxious about the keeping of the Prussian possessions in Great Poland. This is confirmed by the royal rescript of 19 XII 1796, which limited the donations to persons born in the old Prussian provinces or to foreigners possessing agricultural qualifications. It also obliged the owners to inform the authorities of the intention of undertaking any transactions with these goods[^110]. Since however the estates were being resold to Poles, another rescript was necessary in 1800, calling attention to the fact that not only the first but also the later owners cannot be Poles[^111]. Another loophole was being found, so in 1801 there appeared an order announcing that donations may also not be given to Poles in perpetual lease[^112]. There now remained only the selling-off by persons put up to it. With the ordinances characterised above one must also connect the letter of Frederick William III to Minister von der Recke. The king writes of the violent endeavours of the townsmen for the possibility of buying noble estates and of the need of expressing agreement to such contracts, but in a limited number of cases („um den Adel in den Besitz der Landgüter zu sichern"). These limitations were soon to come to the foreground; what interests us however is the undoubted symptom of the growth of demand for land appearing here[^113]. Frederick William III regarded the trade in landed estates as an unfavourable phenomenon, but he regarded secret cessions of the leases of domainal estates as particularly harmful[^114]. The affair of the "Black Register" influenced negatively the attitude of the Polish nobility to the Prussian administration and notably undermined the authority of the Prussian state, the more so as it was not an isolated case of irregularity in the functioning of the Prussian administration in South Prussia.

It is however impossible to deny that Wilhelm Klewitz was right when in 1812 he wrote that on the one hand nowhere as in South Prussia could such a number of "base" and "self-interested" persons squeeze into the administration, and yet even now, in the time of the Duchy of Warsaw, "the Prussian book-keepers are to this day still pleasant to you"[^115].
