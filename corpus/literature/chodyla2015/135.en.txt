According to his observations: "the Olęder men were in general shorter than the Polish peasants, but more *"thickset, sturdy and strong".* Their faces were typical, German, shaven; they very rarely wore beards. The Polish peasants wore long *sukmany* (coats), reaching almost to the ground, of grey, coarse cloth, more rarely of coarse linen dyed dark blue, made at home, and under the *sukmany* long cloth waistcoats with shining glass buttons. Their trousers and shirts were of linen; the former were sometimes sewn of the same cloth as the *sukmana*. Their boots were ordinary ones, bought at the fair. On their heads they put in summer a black felt hat with a small brim; in winter the taller ones wore caps of grey lambskin, extraordinarily high, with a blue cloth top and narrow blue ribbons drawing it in, tied in a bow, and the shorter ones a so-called *kaptur*, that is a low four-cornered cap, also trimmed with grey lambskin. The Olęders, like the local peasants, often wore grey or blue *sukmany*, but these were shorter and of a different cut. As headgear they almost never wore hats, but cloth caps, and in winter black lambskin ones. The Polish women dressed in skirts and jackets or bodices in which red always predominated, and only among the older ones was one to be seen the navy-blue cloth *kabat* formerly in general use, fairly short, reaching perhaps half an ell, with a small, roundly cut cape, and gathered at the back. Married women, with their hair cut short, wore on their heads (though not all of them) muslin or tulle caps, bound round the head with a twisted patterned kerchief. Girls plaited their hair into braids, pinning part up at the back and winding part round the ears. Both married women and girls liked to attach bright coloured ribbons to the beads (corals) they wore round their necks. On their feet all the women wore shoes, sometimes adorned with ribbon bows. The Olęder women, on the other hand (married women and girls alike), went about in striped woollen skirts of their own making and aprons of the same, and put on a fustian or other jacket. In summer, instead of the woollen skirts, they wore calico dresses. On their heads they put woollen kerchiefs, but dark ones, of the town type, which they tied in the same way as women of all estates. On their feet they wore shoes.

The customs and manners of the people of these settlements in the second half of the eighteenth century are not known.

As for customs, the Olęders blessed at Epiphany (the Three Kings): amber, myrrh, bryony, marjoram and garlic, which was to protect against an evil person. They also blessed then, for the same purpose, the so-called "devil's dung" (asafoetida), with which they fumigated themselves", giving it also to the cows to eat against bewitchment, especially at the new moon and before Whitsun. They also introduced a way of treating cattle, later known to the Polish peasants, by giving them animal oil. Both they and the Polish peasants, in treating people as well as cattle, set great store by various "ninefold" herbs, grasses, roots, drops and ointments. They also believed that someone could fall ill, for instance, from bewitchment by "*cioty*" [witches]. Besides herbs, a favourite medicine were various fats, such as those of the dog, the bear, the lion [sic!] and the badger, which were to help used internally as well as externally. A remedy against worms in children, to be effective, had to be prepared not in the house but behind it. They also used other, magical ways of healing.
