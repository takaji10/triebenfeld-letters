# Chodyła (2015), p. 112
# translation by Claude

In the Ciążeńskie Olędry, on the other hand, created before 1788-1789 in the district of Pyzdry, in the Ciążeń key of the mensal estates of the bishopric of Poznań, there lived, according to the revision of the Ciążeń key of 1793, 4 Olęders (3 with Polish names: Jan Macieje[w]ski, Mateusz Czajka, Marcin Ławicki, and 1 with a German name: Augustyn Maytler), Poles, Catholics[^236].

The inhabitants of 8 smaller Olęder settlements that arose before 1789, and at the latest before 1792, in the district of Konin, varied in confession and nation. In 1791 in Bukowa/Bukowe (Bukowe Budy), or the Łomowskie Olędry, lived only Catholics, 74 in number; in Imielno lived 21 Catholics and 42 dissenters; in the Myszakówko Olędry 16 dissenters; in Stara Huta – the Zagórowskie Ol. 37 Catholics[^237]. In 1790, in turn, 21 Catholics and 20 dissenters lived in the Kowalewskie Olędry[^238], and the Jaroszewskie Olędry were probably inhabited by Catholics and Lutherans, Poles and Germans[^239].

In the three hearths of the Olęder settlement of Świdle Nowa Kolonia, founded in 1790 in the district of Kalisz by the old village of Świdle, there lived, it seems, people of the Protestant and the Catholic confession, of German and Polish nationality.

Summing up the information above on the confession, nationality and origin of the inhabitants of the 58 Olęder settlements that arose in the years 1746-1789 and 1791 in the Pyzdry Forest, it must be said that information stating directly the ethnic belonging of the Olęders occurs in the sources only three times. There are, however, indirect indications of the ethnic composition. One of them, besides confession, is their first names and surnames, often distorted by being Polonised (first names) and written as they sounded (surnames). In some cases, from the form of the names, Polish Protestants or German Catholics, or Polonised Germans, may be guessed at among the settlers. However, if not all of the colonists, then certainly a fair number of them also understood Polish, as is shown, indirectly, by the fact that most of the foundation charters and other documents or records were written in Polish. Other indications pointing indirectly to the nationality of the Olęders are the dialect of German they used (known chiefly from the pre-war research on part of the German minority in Poland) and the kind and size of the measures of land (rods), which also point to their place of origin, though not in the sense of a locality but of a region or country.

For the localities from which the Olęder settlers came are, for the period that concerns us, with very few exceptions, unknown.
