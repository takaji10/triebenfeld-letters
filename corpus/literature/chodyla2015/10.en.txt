made up the legal distinctness and the greater attraction of Olęder settlement compared with other forms of colonisation under German law, such as settlement under a sołtys (village mayor), in which the settlers received their holdings (farms) for perpetual use, but not, like the Olęders, on the strength of foundation contracts made with the whole commune: by individual charters[^1].

According to research so far, at least about 1,700 Olęder settlements in all were founded in the years 1547-1864 in the lands of the First Commonwealth and, after 1795, in the lands of its three partitions, at least 350 of them with an exclusive or partial share of ethnic Dutch of the Mennonite confession and their descendants, some of them by then turned Lutheran. At least about 925 of these settlements arose in Greater Poland proper, which in the Old Polish period consisted of two voivodeships, of Poznań and of Kalisz, and from 1768, after a third voivodeship, that of Gniezno, was formed from part of the second, of three such administrative units[^2]. In the years 1597-1793 alone, that is from the year the oldest settlement was founded[^3] until the whole of Greater Poland proper had been brought within the borders of Prussia in the Second Partition of Poland, 758 Olęder settlements in the wider sense were created in 8 of its 10 districts (Poznań, Kościan, Kalisz, Konin, Nakło, Pyzdry, Gniezno and Kcynia), at least 711 of them (with 8 ephemeral ones) certain, 22 of a transitional type and 25 whose legal and constitutional character is unclearly documented in the sources[^4]. The transitional settlements, whose legal constitution is not quite certain, could develop towards settlements that were certainly Olęder, or towards sołtys or emphyteutic ones. The settlements whose legal status is unclearly stated in the sources had an elected self-government or a sołtys appointed by the lord, but did not always keep complete equality and solidarity towards the lord, and, besides, because of the sometimes small number of hearths – farms (2-3) – and of inhabitants, they raise the doubt whether an Olęder self-government could have been organised and could have worked in them. Since, however, 1) before and in the years 1789-1793, and later, they were very consistently described as *olędry* and showed other secondary features of Olęder settlements, 2) they were probably for the most part tied administratively and judicially to larger Olęder settlements or formed communes of two or more villages, and 3) by the end of the eighteenth century and the first quarter of the nineteenth they had grown, and their legal status had on the whole probably settled, and most often they had gained a separate administrative and judicial organisation, we count them as such.
