# Glossary of Old Polish terms

**asekuracja** – here: a security, a written guarantee.

**bicie rowów** – digging ditches and lining them with fascines.

**cekowska dzierżawa** (the lease of Ceków) – an organisational unit of the royal/Crown estates in the district of Kalisz, with the village of Ceków as its centre; in the 1770s made up, besides that village, also of the newly founded Cekowskie Stare and Nowe Olędry.

**czeladź** – village servants, hired people, recruited from peasant youth (male and female) (of full peasants, more often of smallholders, cottagers and lodgers), mostly before marriage, employed on peasant farms (chiefly full peasant farms) and on demesne farms, under agreements usually made for a year, and paid with lodging, food, clothing and small sums of money (the so-called *myto*).

**czerwony złoty** (red złoty) – a ducat, a gold coin weighing about 3.5 g of almost pure metal, from 1787 equal to 18 Polish silver złoty.

**czynszujący** (rent-payers) – here peasants, Olęder farmers, who under their agreements paid, as their most important due, a money rent and possibly an additional rent in kind (grain, poultry) in return for the use of the land, most often perpetual.

**dezerta** (from Latin *deserta*), see pustka.

dobra stołowe (mensal estates) – estates forming the endowment, the source of income and the basis of maintenance of a particular office or official connected with the court (household), in this case the mensal estates of the bishop of Poznań.

**efemeryda** (ephemeral settlement) – here: a settlement undoubtedly Olęder in its legal constitution and economy, but existing as such only for a time, turned into a labour-service settlement, not through evolution but for other reasons.

**emfiteuza** (emphyteusis, from Latin *emphiteusis*) – in the feudal period it was most often understood as the long-term (renewable) and perpetual lease of land by peasants, tied to the obligation to pay an annual rent and to inform the owner of an intention to sell, since the owner had the right of pre-emption or 10% of the sale price of the land.

**fundator** (founder, from Latin *fundo*, *fundator*) – here: the feudal founder of a settlement, in this case a rural, Olęder one.

**garniec** – a measure of capacity, from 1764 equal to 3.77 litres, divided into 2 half-garniec, 4 kwarty and 16 kwaterki.

**kasztelan** (castellan) – an Old Polish official, in the Middle Ages the governor of a castle (*gród*, *kasztel*, from Latin *castellum*, castle) and of the castle district (castellany), military commander and judge in the monarch's name in its area, a member of the royal council, in early modern times a senator; castellans were divided into greater (provincial) and lesser (district ones, also called *drążkowi*).

**kmiecie** (full peasants) – the basic stratum of the rural population in the thirteenth to nineteenth centuries, holders of farms regarded as full ones. In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, when German law was introduced, they were usually settled on farms of one łan (one włóka), but then and later they often received larger holdings, of two łany (and as such were also called farmers, *gospodarze rolnicy*), or smaller, of half a łan (half-peasants, *półrolnicy*). In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries the main burdens of the k. were money rents and dues in kind (chiefly in grain) to the lord and tithe to the Church; in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries they were burdened more and more with labour service, reaching in the seventeenth century 5-6 days, and in the eighteenth century sometimes a dozen or so days a week per łan, which forced the k. to employ servants. To till their own and the lord's land the k. kept a considerable team (in the seventeenth century usually 2 horses and 4 oxen). In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the size of full peasant farms shrank considerably, to ½ and even ¼ łan, and the percentage of k. in the structure of the rural population fell. Beside the k. owing labour service there was a far less numerous group of rent-paying k. (for instance the Olęders), as a rule richer and more independent economically.

**komornicy** (lodgers) – a category of the landless rural population found in the fourteenth to nineteenth centuries, owning neither cottages nor land, living most often with farmers (Olęders too) and living by hired work for them or at the manor, often as servants; sometimes owning a few animals and tilling, in return for work, scraps of land; the poorest stratum of the settled rural population.
