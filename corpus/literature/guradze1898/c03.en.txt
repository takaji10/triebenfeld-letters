# **Chapter I. (Introduction).** {#chapter-i.-(introduction).}

Peasant Conditions in the Areas Now Constituting the Province of Posen in the Last Days of the Polish Empire.

The province of Posen fell under Prussian rule in the year 1815; however, the territories it comprises had already belonged to Prussia earlier. The administrative district of Posen, along with the old counties of Wongrowitz and Gnesen, including Powidz, were part of the former province of South Prussia from 1793 to 1806\. The remaining counties of the Bromberg administrative district were part of the Netze District from 1772 to 1806, also in Prussian possession. From 1807 to 1815, both areas constituted parts of the Duchy of Warsaw. Only around fifty or so localities in the Bromberg administrative district had continuously belonged to Prussia since 1772\.

The development of peasant conditions and, in particular, the legislation related to the Netze District, differed significantly from that of South Prussia, necessitating separate consideration. However, to understand them, it is necessary to first take a brief look at the conditions of the peasants under Polish rule.

To comprehensively systematise peasant conditions and gain a clear insight into them, they must be viewed from three different perspectives. These include the personal relationship, i.e., the degree of freedom or servitude, the legal relationship of the peasants concerning their holdings (peasant estates), and finally, the size of these holdings must also be taken into account.

In Poland, in the middle of the previous century,[^1] among the peasants, regarding their personal status, there were three categories: free, subjugated, and serfs.

The personally free peasants were largely of German origin, who had been brought into the country as colonists since the 13th century. They possessed charters and privileges for their rights and were usually obligated to pay rent in money or produce, as well as perform minor services for the landowners.

Among these were particularly the so-called 'Haulanders,' a term originally used to describe colonists assigned to drain and cultivate marshy land according to the so-called Dutch (Flemish) law. However, later usage referred to all colonists assigned under German law, who often were also of Polish descent, especially on noble and ecclesiastical estates, as 'Haulanders,' a term derived from the common usage of 'Holländer' (Dutch). The Germans, sought after for their economic efficiency in the country, were assigned under 'iure teutonico seu Magdeburgensi,' essentially a municipal law, or 'iure et more teutonicali.'

Apart from these free colonists, there were also preserved domains and certain noble estates known as 'Scholtiseien' and 'Wybranzen,' privileged holdings that were often akin to small manors, whose owners were free individuals originally obligated to military service.

Finally, here and there, originally settled under 'iure polonico,' were peasants who had obtained their freedom through special privileges from their lords, often in exchange for a sum of money, or due to an act of saving a life, or other unique circumstances.

Even the free noble peasants[^2] had no recourse to the courts when making complaints against their landlord until 1772\.

In stark contrast to the personal rights of the free peasants were those of the serfs. All peasants assigned under Polish law ('iure polonico seu terrestri') on noble and ecclesiastical estates, along with their families, were serfs of the landlord, unless they had been granted special privileges. They had no access to regular courts and had to be represented in court by their lord if they wished to make a complaint. They had no legal recourse against their lord and were thus entirely without legal rights against them. Instead, their legal jurisdiction was under their lord, where complaints against them had to be brought. Their lord not only exercised lower jurisdiction over them but also had all rights, including the 'ius vitae et necis' (the right of life and death). It was not uncommon for lords to transfer their serfs, with or without their holdings, to others or retain individual persons in property sales. It is even said that in the last decades of Polish rule, lords would gamble away their serfs. Furthermore, lords had the freedom to compel their serfs into various services at any time. The children were required to provide compulsory labour services to the lordship and the large farmers.

The serfs were allowed to buy their freedom, but only with the lord's consent, who also arbitrarily determined the amount of the ransom. The lord could pursue, capture, and punish escaped serfs. Most Polish laws related to peasants discuss this matter ('de kmetone fugitivo').[^3]

The fate of the serf depended solely on whether they were under a lenient lord or one who sought to extract the greatest advantage from them without regard for their well-being. The serf was a slave.[^4]

To some extent, an intermediate stage between free and serf peasants were the domain peasants assigned under 'iure polonico.' They belonged to the subject community of their estate but were never entirely without rights. They had access to regular courts at all times, first to Assessorial courts and later to Referendariats courts. Their obligations were clearly defined and marked in 'Lustrations,' which were supposed to be renewed every 5 years and were last established in 1764 and 1789\. These peasants were not subject to personal arbitrary actions, although they had to fulfil their due services and taxes to the Starost[^5] or lessee. The children of subjugated peasants provided compulsory labour services.

The subjugated peasants also include those noble[^6] and ecclesiastical peasants who had acquired their properties through purchase (okupnicy). While, like all noble peasants, including the free ones, they had no access to regular courts, their personal situation was significantly better than that of the serfs. This was because their obligations were fixed by privileges and, in a way, imposed as real burdens on the properties, so that their person was only subject to the lord's discretion to the extent that the lord saw fit to violate their privileges, which did occur even with the free peasants. However, they cannot be considered equal to the free peasants because their children provided compulsory labour services, they had to buy themselves, their wives, and children if they wanted to leave the estate, with the lord often arbitrarily setting the ransom. Additionally, the extent of their obligations suggests a lower degree of personal freedom, and they exhibited all the other characteristics of subjects.

So, the subjects differ from the serfs in the following ways: The lord has no rights over their person and property, meaning they cannot be sold without the land to which they belong, and their acquisition cannot be taken away by the lord.

It is very difficult to establish the boundaries of the concepts of serfdom, subjugation, and freedom. Even though these concepts are legally defined, in reality, there are so many transitional stages that it is often challenging to definitively categorise a type of peasant under one of these concepts. Individual characteristics are often found in several categories; for example, the obligation to pay a 'lytrum' (ransom, deduction) is by no means, as Knapp[^7] assumes, a primary characteristic of subjugation, but is very frequently found among free peasants in the form of a 'lytrum personale' or 'lytrum reale.' To truly understand the personal status of a peasant, one must also consider their material situation by examining the conditions under which they hold their property and the rights they possess with respect to their holdings. Only by taking these factors into account will it often be possible to make a reasonably secure determination in cases where it is uncertain whether a peasant, for example, is free or subjugated.

If one divides the Polish peasants in the middle of the previous century while taking into account the conditions under which they held their positions and the rights they possessed, the following schema can be formulated:

1. Rent-paying peasants were those whose obligations primarily consisted of rent, either in the form of money or produce, or both, with the addition of occasional moderate services. All rent-paying peasants of that time were free individuals; they were either landowners or emphyteutic tenants.

   Owners, who had either full ownership or just beneficial[^8] ownership of their holdings (hereditary rent-paying peasants). The former, for example, the owners of 'wybranzen,' mostly paid rent in money, sometimes including natural produce; hereditary rent-paying peasants were also occasionally obligated to provide minor services.[^9] They almost always had their own buildings and inventory and, therefore, were responsible for their maintenance. Whether the estate owner had to provide the necessary wood for building maintenance depended on the content of the privileges. The holdings were freely inheritable and could generally be freely transferred, often requiring the payment of a 'laudemium'[^10] in such cases.

   This first category includes the owners of 'wybranzen,' 'Scholtiseien,' and 'Bogteien,' 'Hauländer,' and colonists, as long as they were not emphyteutic tenants, and other peasants who possessed explicit ownership privileges, which they often acquired through purchase.

1. Emphyteutic[^11] tenants were those to whom their holdings were granted for a specified extended period, usually ranging from 30 to 70 years. Within this timeframe, the right to the holdings was hereditary. Emphyteutic tenants often had the right, upon payment of a specified fee (laudemium), usually amounting to double the annual rent, to demand the extension of their contract for an equal duration. Another form of emphyteusis was the grant of estates under specific conditions for multiple generations, such as father and son or father, son, and grandson.[^12] Emphyteutic tenants typically had the right to sell their ownership claims, with the obligation to sell only to a truly capable successor.

   This term 'Emphyteuse' has nothing to do with the Roman legal concept, which expresses a permanent possession relationship, for example, hereditary rent and hereditary lease.

2. Scharwerksbauern were those whose main obligations consisted of providing services, but they also often paid in-kind rent, and less frequently, cash rent.

   They were either hereditary owners without ownership rights, leaseholders for a specific period, or indefinite-term owners.

1. Hereditary owners without ownership rights (hereditary leaseholders)[^13] differed from those mentioned in A. 1\. primarily due to higher services and dues, which is why they were not classified as rent-paying peasants. Additionally, they were distinct in that they required the lord's permission for the sale of their holdings, or at the very least, they had to demonstrate that the buyer was capable of maintaining the estate.[^14] In Prussian times, in case of disputes, they had to prove the hereditary nature of their holdings through privileges since the right of inheritance among Polish peasants was the exception rather than the rule. Even though non-hereditary owners often passed on their holdings through multiple generations, the transition of such a holding from father to son was not a right of the owner but a voluntary act of the estate owner. The estate owner also had the right to determine which child should inherit the holding among hereditary peasants, provided they were not free individuals.  
2. The leaseholders, often referred to as contract farmers, held their holdings for a specified number of years in exchange for providing services and dues. Their buildings belonged to the lord, who was also responsible for their maintenance; only minor repairs were carried out by the farmer. The inventory also mostly belonged to the lord, who had to replenish it in case of accidents. When the lease term ended, the property had to be returned. In cases of fire or other damages, the estate owner usually assisted these farmers by granting them a reduction in rent or services or by providing loans in money or grain, the amount of which was solely at his discretion.  
3. The indefinite-term owners, who held their holdings without fixed contracts, constituted the majority of noble and ecclesiastical peasants. They typically had to pay high dues, mostly in the form of services but also in kind. Like the leaseholders, they had no inheritance rights and could easily be deprived of their holdings, transferred to other positions (translocated), or burdened with increased services and dues. Their living conditions varied greatly on each estate, sometimes even within the same estate, and the level of dues often bore little relation to the size of the fields. Consequently, there is little that can be established as a norm for this type of peasants, except that they generally had lordly buildings and lordly inventory. This means that not only horses, oxen, cows, etc., as well as all farming equipment such as wagons, ploughs, spades, rakes, etc., but also household items such as stools, tables, even the bowl from which they ate, and the spoon with which they ate, were lordly property entrusted to them by the estate owner. In these and all other respects, especially regarding remissions, the living conditions of indefinite-term owners were identical to those of leaseholders, with perhaps even greater leeway given to arbitrariness in their case. In the opinions found in the records, it is often stated that the situation of ecclesiastical peasants was better than that of noble peasants; however, others assert the opposite with equal determination. It is likely that no general rule can be established, and like everything else in this anarchic land, it depended entirely on the individual and local circumstances of each case.

According to the size of their holdings, peasants are classified into:[^15]

1. Large peasants (Großbauern) or whole peasants (Ganzbauern). The average size of their fields is reported to be 7 Hufen[^16] (210 Morgens), in another place,[^17] 12 'Sack' and more of winter field sowing, as well as gardens and meadowland. The noble large peasants were a unique institution. They received from the lord up to 4 horses (in another place, it's mentioned as 2 teams, so either 6 or 8 horses), as well as up to 4 oxen, some cows, and all the other necessary living and dead inventory. During the preparations for the law of April 8, 1823, in the State Council, the estate owners who were consulted[^18] indicated that the size of the whole peasant holdings averaged 33 Berlin Scheffel of sowing in each field, with the prevailing three-field farming system, and the inventory consisted of 4 horses, 4 oxen, and 3-4 cows. In the presence of abundant meadows, the peasants may have held half as much more as surplus inventory. With the horses, for which they also had to provide the necessary labourers ('fornal'), only estate work was done. The oxen were used for farming the peasant's land. In terms of human labour, the large peasant required about 6 additional servants for himself and estate work, who were supplemented from the pool of duty-bound peasant children.[^19]

   We are dealing here with a very convenient but naturally irrational management form of the estate's outlying farms. It is clear that these large establishments only existed to manage the estate's outlying farms and not for their own sake because the estate was only so large because the abundant estate inventory and servants had to live off their income.

2. Half-farmers (Halbbauern) had 2-4 Magdeburg Hufen (60-120 Morgen), and they were often referred to as small team-peasants.

3. Quarter-farmers (Viertelbauern) had 1-1½ Hufen (30-45 Morgen).

4. Gardeners (Gärtner) and cottagers (Büdner) with ¼-¾ Hufen.

5. Subsistence farmers (Käthner).

Other designations such as Hüfner, Halbhüfner, Meier, Halbmeier, Groß- and Kleinkossäth, Damniker, Rathayer, were common in various parts of the province, without necessarily attaching the same meaning to each of these designations every time.

The above size classification should also be taken with reservations; it is meant to provide only a rough idea of the variations in the sizes of peasant holdings. Very often, the term Ganzbauer (full farmer) is applied to the small team-peasants mentioned under 2., and accordingly, those mentioned under 3\. are referred to as Halbbauern (half farmers), and so on.

The Polish words[^20] that are of interest here are:

* kmieć, chłop: Farmer in general, full farmer (Ganzbauer)  
* połrolnik, połownik, półkmieć: Half farmer (Halbbauer)  
* półlanik, połoszlednik: Farmers owning half a Hufe of land (Halbhüfner)  
* zagrodnik: Gardener (Gärtner)  
* chałupnik: Cottager, smallholder (Häusler)  
* chatnik: Subsistence farmer (Käthner)  
* komornik: Cottager (Kossäth), but also a landless farmer (Einlieger)  
* szołtysi: Village mayors (Schulzen) and free/autonomous village mayors (Freischulzen)  
* woitostwo: Bailiff (Vogteien)  
* czynszowy kmieć, czynsownik: Rent/tenant farmer (Zinsbauer), Hollender, Holländer, Olander, Olęder, Ölender, Hauländer  
* osadnik, kolonista: Colonist (Kolonist)  
* okupnicy: Free farmers (losgekaufte Bauern)

After gaining insight into the conditions of the Polish peasant class around the middle of the previous century, let us now examine the changes that occurred in the personal circumstances of the peasants from that time until the acquisition of the land by Prussia.

On December 1, 1764,[^21] King Stanislaus August is said to have told his Chancellor Zamoyski, while discussing the compilation of the country's laws: "As long as the landlords continue to exercise the ius vitae et necis over the peasants, I will not be able to tolerate the judgement of a foreigner about our people."

It can be assumed that during the reign of this monarch, attention was once again directed towards the peasants by legislative factors after a long pause. The extraordinary Diet of 1766 made resolutions that concerned the peasants and incorporated them into the Constitution of February 24 of that year. The most important[^22] of these are:

1. In the royal, noble, and ecclesiastical estates, plots of land that are not arable may be granted to both locals and foreigners under emphyteutic rights, and these grants shall be maintained.  
2. The full ownership of the nobility in their estates and the subjects within them shall not be taken away or restricted; however, the right over life and death shall not be exercised by the lord of the manor over the subjects any longer. Instead, a subject who commits a criminal offence shall be handed over to the rural, town, or city courts in the larger towns.  
3. A murder intentionally and maliciously committed by a noble against a person of peasant status shall be punished by death and cannot be atoned for with money, as was previously the case. Furthermore, injury or mutilation shall also be appropriately punished by the competent courts.

It is interesting that at that time, the principle was stated that emphyteutes should have their contractual rights upheld, meaning they should not be subjected to arbitrary and unlawful increased burdens and services. The fact that this principle was not always followed is evidenced by the numerous lawsuits that were conducted during the South Prussian period, which will be discussed later. Additionally, it should be noted that at that time, there was no court before which emphyteutes could have sued their lords for the fulfilment of their contracts.

With the removal of the ius vitae et necis from the landowner, the first stone was removed from the edifice of serfdom, and thus it gradually and completely declined. We do, however, hear that despite all this, nobles continued to provoke the ius vitae et necis,[^23] but evidence for such cases has not been provided.

The provisions regarding the punishment for injuries and mutilations by the competent courts were entirely worthless, as neither a penalty nor a court for this purpose had been specified. However, the inclusion of the provision that a nobleman could no longer afford the luxury of killing a peasant for 100 marks, and that in such a case, he should be executed as a common murderer, can be seen as a sign that the legislating nobility was beginning to think somewhat more humanely. Klebs[^24] does mention (without citing sources) that the law lost all significance because the plaintiff had to provide 6 witnesses, including three from the nobility, while the nobleman could clear himself through an oath, along with two other nobles.

From the above, one can roughly understand how the landlord-peasant relations were at the time when the Netze District came under Prussian rule in 1772\. To understand the conditions in South Prussia, a brief overview is provided below.

The Constitution of 1784 ordered the Polish courts to intervene ex officio in cases of manslaughter without waiting for a formal complaint. However, von Ostrowski[^25] noted that for the noble murderer of a peasant, there may have been a meagre law, but no judge willing to prosecute.

As Poland made its final effort and adopted a better constitution in 1791, the estates assembled at the Sejm declared that[^26] "the class engaged in agriculture should be placed under the protection of the laws and the state." In the future, leases and agreements, if made in proper form, were to establish mutual obligations, be protected by the laws, and be binding for successors.

However, before these resolutions could have any effect, following the formation of the Targowice Confederation in 1792, the Constitution of 1791 was repealed by the Sejm of Grodno in 1792\. Shortly thereafter, the second partition of Poland occurred in 1793, during which the present-day Poznań Province, as well as the Wongrowitz and Gnesen districts with Powidz, became part of South Prussia under the Prussian crown.
