# Guradze (1898), Chapter V.
# translation by Claude

# **Chapter V.** {#chapter-v.}

Some Insights into the Service Obligations of the Farmers of South Prussia.

During the time of South Prussia, noble landowners still had considerable leeway in their treatment of their subject farmers. For example, until 1803, these farmers had to tolerate arbitrary relocation. When filling the vacancies left by deceased non-hereditary farms—most socage farmers' farms were not hereditary—the discretion of the landowner was unrestricted. With respect to support during calamities, there were no specific rules, and although it was generally the custom for the landowner to take care of major repairs and new buildings while the farmer handled minor repairs, the only standard was the needs of the subjects on the one hand and the goodwill of the estate owner on the other. Non-privileged farmers were supported either through the remission of services, through cash advances, or not at all in maintaining buildings and during calamities.

Grazing and forest rights were generally considered appurtenances of the estates, and heath pasturing was usually permitted for a moderate fee or even for free.

The dues on noble estates were usually higher than on the domains and often so high that it seems astonishing how the farmers could manage. This can be partially explained by the fact that there was often an excess of services available, and therefore some were not demanded by the landowners. For instance, in the Polajewo estate, there were 11,039[^157] more labour services available than were needed; that is, of the 21,069⅓ labour services that could have been demanded, more than half were superfluous. Also, where farmers had high dues to pay, they were often in continuous arrears. This was particularly common in bad years, as frequently mentioned in the official monthly reports of the governments of Bromberg and Posen to the King, called "newspaper reports."[^158]

Whether the services and dues were burdensome in individual cases is hard to say. One would have to at least determine the size of the field and other appurtenances of the estate from the land settlement records to get an accurate picture. Because the size specifications of that time are usually so vague that it is impossible to draw any definite conclusions. The conditions of dues and the situation of the noble farmers were extraordinarily diverse in South Prussia, not only in different regions of the province but even on estates located very close to each other, often even within the same manor. All of the reports in the files talk about the complexity of the conditions, which arose because the services of almost every farmer were determined in a different way and were usually entirely independent of the size of the field belonging to the estate. A uniform presentation of the farmers' conditions concerning dues would therefore be entirely impossible; I refer to what was said in the chapter that deals with the conditions of the Netze District, which generally also applies to the conditions in South Prussia.

Thoma[^159] indicates that one would hardly find a socage farmer in South Prussia who cultivates a Magdeburg Hufe (a unit of land) and is burdened with fewer services than 104 hand days, 100 team days, 30 domestic chores, 20 garden tasks, and 20 forced labour days (tłuki, mostly during sowing and harvest time). This would be an average of 2 team days and 3⅓ hand days per week throughout the year. However, this information seems to be based only on a completely arbitrary estimate of the average.

Overall, the services were only measured up until 1793 insofar as they were measured at all, in that the numbers expressed what the landowner intended to demand from the serfs; there was a very one-sided legal obligation on the part of the farmer, which could be unilaterally increased or reduced by the landlord at any time, and to which no corresponding legal obligation of the landlord was opposed. Even if the numbers quoted may often have sounded extremely high, it would still always have to be determined whether as much was actually demanded as they indicated, and that may often enough not have been the case, corresponding to the above-mentioned conditions of the Polajewo estate.

"The treatment in terms of services," says von Holsche,[^160] "was bearable. For most farmers, they were determined for a series of years, according to the needs of the estate, for certain weekly services, forced labour days during the harvest and sowing season, and for long hauls; rarely was more demanded of them. This had a good reason; because the farm and the entire inventory belonged to the lord. If he thus burdened the farmer beyond his capacity with services, the farmer could not cultivate his own field and could not live from it with his family, his cattle would collapse, and the lord would have to give him back sowing and bread grain along with inventory livestock if he wanted services from him, or he would risk the farmer running away altogether. The strictest lords were therefore, because their own advantage depended on it, induced not to impose more burdens on their farmers than they could bear, and whoever went further was considered by others to be a poor farmer."

"That these farmers could achieve nothing was due to the nature of the matter itself because they had nothing of their own, and if they came into better condition than their neighbours, they had to maintain their buildings and supplement the inventory from their own means. All that the good farmer could have ahead of the negligent one was that the good farmer lived somewhat better than the negligent one."

"The demands of the serfs are certainly not determined by the yield of their farms, but by the needs of the estate or noble property; hence some farmers serve two, others three and four, and still others six days a week, and must also drive to the market for the lord on Sundays. However, this is again due to the fact that the farmer has nothing of his own, and even if personal serfdom is abolished, the lords cannot be forced to reduce the demands." Holsche seems to have had the right feeling that the farmers would have been far better served by improving their material situation than by abolishing the personal restrictions that arose from serfdom. The government probably also intended something similar when it set up the Urbarial Commissions in 1793\. The aim was probably not only to determine but also to reduce the farmers' contributions. Even if this, as it seems to me, has not been directly stated, both the extremely low assessments of the farmers in the tax assessments[^161] and the fact that the subjects were directly pointed to the impossibility claim indicate that even then a relief of the service and tax relationship was being sought where this was absolutely necessary. It is quite natural that the farmers, either by themselves or through the commissioners, had to become properly aware of what they were actually accustomed to doing when taking up the urbaria, and thus the incentive to bring the impossibility claim may have arisen in many cases. However, it seems that the judiciary of the province was by no means in a position where such claims could have been quickly settled in large numbers.

In fact, even considering the above, one cannot assume that the contributions were generally extremely exaggerated; it would have only gone up to the point where, in the sense of the proverb, the other hand would have had to start helping to wash the one. So if the contributions of the peasants seem to have been generally very high, one cannot say, like Rummler does,[^162] 'And what were the counter-contributions of the landowner? A small piece of arable land with 18—20 bushels of sowing.' The arable land may have been the only counter-contribution from the landowner to which the corvée peasants had a real right during Polish times. But the actual counter-contributions of the landowners of South Prussia are the same as those previously said of the Netze District, and these actual counter-contributions had become legal obligations of the landowners through Prussian legislation. Then one can see nothing from the specification '18—20 bushels of sowing.' Because if this should serve us as a useful example, it would still have to be added whether these 18—20 bushels mean 'sowing in winter fields,' as was usual,[^163] or the sum of all sowing, and how large the bushels specifically meant were. Beheim-Schwarzbach has already pointed out the variation of the measure.[^164] The South Prussian bushel 'large measure' has, according to Stenger, amounted to four Berlin bushels. So if this should be about 80 Berlin bushels of winter field sowing, considering the landowner's obligations related to pasturage and forest rights, building and maintenance of inventory etc., it would perhaps be a very appropriate counter-contribution for the services and levies mentioned for the peasants of Althöfchen. Namely, they had to provide 14 geese, 2 capons, 2 chickens, 4 quarters of heather oats, 2 pieces of spun yarn, 28 groschen of guard money, 18 quarters of grain, and 3—4 cart services per week. But one cannot even judge that if one does not know the quality of the soil, the sowing quantity per acre, the sales opportunities, and everything else that needs to be considered.

Another conclusion from Rummler is also incomprehensible to me. He says,[^165] “traces can be found that the free farmer's rights were curtailed and he was pushed down by the landlords into the position of a leaseholder,” and substantiates this claim by stating that the information table of the ecclesiastical property of Barcinek identifies three local residents as emphyteutic free farmers and explains this term as follows: “The emphyteutic lease would last until the fourth male generation.”

Firstly, there is as little contradiction in 'free farmer' (Freibauer) and 'leaseholder' (Zeitpacher) as there is in the term 'free farmer' and 'emphyteutic tenant.' The term 'free farmer' describes a personal legal relationship, while 'leaseholder' or 'emphyteutic tenant' describes the farmer's legal relationship to his landholding. All emphyteutic tenants in Poland were free farmers. Long-term emphyteusis for multiple generations, instead of for a specific series of years, has been a common occurrence. I only refer to the cited document in which Abbot Szołdrski grants free farmer Thomas Kaminski a place with emphyteutic rights 'pro se et successoribus usque ad tertiam progeniem inclusive' (for himself and his successors up to the third generation inclusive). There is therefore absolutely no impairment of hereditary lease rights in the case mentioned, as Rummler may have been suggesting.

During the South Prussian period, as well as previously during the last period of the Polish realm, a trend emerged among the owners of manor estates aimed at improving the conditions of the peasants. This was in the correct understanding that by doing so, they were simultaneously promoting their own prosperity and that of the entire country. Owners of significant estates granted freedom to their subordinate peasants and turned them into rent-paying farmers, such as the Lords of Brzostowski, Chreptowicz, Zamoiski, Felix Potocki, St. Poniatowski, as well as the Provincial Director von Schlichting at Roehrsdorff and the Chamberlain von Bojanowski at Nitsche, in the Fraustadt district.

Such occurrences can also be noted in other parts of Poland, e.g., the reforms of the Kock estate reported by Bochenski.[^166]

So it is evident that among the estate owners in the Polish territories, there were those who wisely progressed with the times by implementing reforms with their peasants, as well as those who remained at the opposite extreme, exploiting their subjects as much as possible. Although the latter are almost exclusively discussed, the majority of the estate owners are likely to be found neither among the former nor the latter, but somewhere in between.

What has been said so far in this chapter about the peasants naturally only applies to the subordinate, noble corvée peasants. I have refrained from further describing their conditions, as these essentially corresponded to those of the Netze District; the same applies to the rent-paying farmers.

To illustrate the variety of service relationships within the same estate, some information about the Polajewo estates is provided here. This originally noble property was owned by the Royal Prussian Maritime Trading Mill and gained domain status at the same time as the confiscation of the ecclesiastical and so on estates. The corvée peasants of this estate, during the South Prussian period, received either the beneficial ownership or the hereditary possession of their holdings in exchange for rent and services.[^167] The procedure was such that an agreement was made with an interested party if the previous owner was deemed unsuitable; the latter was then provided with a settlement and expelled without further ado, in accordance with the procedure approved by the King through a royal rescript dated December 12, 1800\.

I include here the details of the services from the records. Unfortunately, there is again a complete lack of information about the size and nature of the holdings from which these services were rendered; it is only noted that nowhere does the relationship of the fields and services of one peasant correspond to those of another. During the South Prussian era, these services were reduced to "an appropriate measure" upon hereditary and granting, which unfortunately is not specified.

As a general rule, the services of the domain peasants in South Prussia, after their renewed determination for full farmers,[^169] amounted to two days of team service (Spanntage) and one day of manual labour (Handtage) or one day of team service and two days of manual labour weekly. Furthermore, 1—2 bushels of oats, 1 pair of chickens, 1—2 dozens of eggs, and 1—2 pieces of spun yarn per year. Additionally, a number of 'Struzen' (domestic work), 'Darmachen' or 'Dramachen' (unspecified, usually gardening tasks) and tłuki (urgent or forced labour during sowing and harvest time).

This was indeed perhaps three times as much as was demanded of the estate farmers in the Netze District.[^170] However, it is easily understandable that the reforms in East and West Prussia were not taken as a model in South Prussia, since the entire administration, due to the personality of Graten Hoym, was conducted more along the lines of the Silesian model.

This is probably the source of the entire difference in legislation between South Prussia and the Netze District, mentioned at the end of the previous chapter, to the extent that it cannot be attributed to the fact that after the suppression of the uprising of 1794, a large part of the planned reforms were abandoned, rather than implementing them with redoubled energy, as far as they were justified.
