The project for the colonisation of New East Prussia that he laid before the king, and his request that suitable funds be set aside for this purpose, received the consent of the king, who granted 200 thousand thalers for carrying on this action. The help intended for colonists was not as high as in South Prussia. It rested on the exact calculation that for the complete setting up of a colonist receiving 3 łany of land on a new farm a sum of 600 thalers was needed, for a 4-łan farm 800 thalers; thus 200 thal. fell to one łan. It was therefore provided that the state's financial help would amount to 300 thalers for those settling on 3 łany, that is the colonist must possess 300 thalers of his own means, on 4 łany 400 thal., on 5 łany 500 thal., and so on. The amount of financial help was thus proportional to the colonist's own means brought in and to the area of land received. Besides financial help, colonists were to be granted, as in South Prussia, travel money and timber for building. The colonist was to take up the land allotted to him within a fixed time and, on pain of losing it, set about bringing it into cultivation.

Schrötter put his plan of recruiting action into effect, and already in 1802 the first Württemberg emissaries came to New East Prussia to find out what the conditions of settlement in the new province were. Volunteers from the left bank of the Rhine also applied to the Prussian legation, asking about the possibility of going to the new province. To spread these conditions Schrötter ordered them to be printed and sent to Frankfurt on the Main. At this time, too, leaflets appeared encouraging settlement in South Prussia and New East Prussia[^62].

The Prussian envoy officiating at Frankfurt on the Main, Formey, did not, however, keep strictly to the instructions he had received and accepted applications for going to New East Prussia from people who could not prove that they had sufficient means. [^62]: The leaflet printed in 1802, "Der glückliche Bauer. Ein Gespräch zwischen einem südpreussischen Kolonisten und einem schwäbischen Bauern" [The happy peasant. A conversation between a South Prussian colonist and a Swabian peasant], tried to present the condition of the colonists settled in South Prussia in the best possible light. The conversation between the Swabian peasant and the colonist from South Prussia, who came from those parts, begins with the colonist trying to dispel the doubts of the Swabian peasant, who thought that in the new Prussian province there was nothing but hunger, sorrow and dejection. For that was how the situation had been presented to him by those who had returned from there, not having found the promised happiness. The colonist, however, presents the conditions prevailing in South Prussia in an altogether different light. He praises the fertility of the soil, the state's help for settlers, and finally the fact that many colonies had already arisen there, settled mainly by settlers from Germany. To the question why, then, the Prussian government was trying to attract settlers there, and whether the peasants there did not cultivate the land, the newcomer from South Prussia replies that the peasants living in South Prussia are lazy and negligent, because in Polish times they were oppressed by the nobility with labour service. Finally the last revolution that broke out there caused many of them to leave the country, and for this reason new settlers are now needed there. He adds that colonists are settled only on land belonging to the king and are therefore only his subjects, not obliged to the labour service that the peasants sitting on the nobles' and church lands of this province must perform. The colonists are thus free men and all the yield is their property. The Swabian peasants need not fear coming to a province inhabited by a foreign population, since there are already many colonists from Germany there, and besides all the officials speak German. Convinced, the Swabian peasant decides to leave and settle in South Prussia, taking all his property with him. Zimmermann, t. II, s. 37.
