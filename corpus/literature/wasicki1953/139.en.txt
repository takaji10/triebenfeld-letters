# Wąsicki (1953), p. 139
# translation by Claude

Immediately after the taking of the Polish lands in the second partition, Frederick William II thought of colonising his new acquisitions with settlers brought from the Reich. He wrote on this matter to the president of the Poznań chamber, Buggenhagen, that he was very anxious to increase the population of the newly taken province and to raise the standard of agriculture in South Prussia[^11]. It would follow from these royal plans that colonisation was to be undertaken mainly for economic and population reasons. Undoubtedly it is from these two aspects that the colonising aims of the Hohenzollerns in the period of South Prussia should be viewed. For the colonisation carried out cannot be identified — as was done until recently — with an action of Germanisation, all the more so since in the times of the nobles' Commonwealth settlers, mostly German, were drawn to these lands. T. Ladenberger also drew attention to this in his work "On Frederician colonisation in Silesia", discussing both the opinions of Polish and German historians on the matter and the course and results of the colonisation of that period. Bourgeois German historians presented the colonising action of the Prussian kings, both in Silesia and in Pomerania, as a "work of culture", and regarded the colonists as "bearers of culture". T. Ladenberger, showing the real aim of colonisation, states at the same time, and rightly, that "colonisation served above all the royal treasury and the great estates"[^12], and that its cultural effects were downright lamentable.

The situation in South Prussia took a similar shape, although in this period an attempt was made, at least to some degree, to avoid the mistakes of Frederician colonisation. Colonies continued to be founded mainly on uncultivated land requiring clearing or draining. A considerable part of the settlers who arrived, as I shall show below, were settled on the royal domains, on the manor farms of private feudal lords and in the forests as hired labourers. Often two or three families were settled among the Polish population. The motive justifying colonisation was thus the wish to bring into use land not cultivated until then and to increase the number of hands for work on the domains and the manor farms. In these cases it is therefore very doubtful whether this poorest stratum of the rural population, employed as hired labourers on the manor farms, could fulfil that "cultural mission" so loudly proclaimed by German historians. Nor was there a better opinion of the other categories of colonists and of their knowledge of farming, even among contemporaries. Holsche himself, who had the best knowledge of contemporary conditions, states that the Württembergers, who settled in the greatest numbers in South Prussia, were bad farmers and that the whole action did not pay at all[^12a]. The settlers who arrived, and especially those who had been given small pieces of land, farming in hard conditions, left South Prussia when the prospect opened before them of obtaining better conditions of settlement in another country.
