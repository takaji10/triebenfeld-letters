Minister Schrötter came to an understanding on this matter with the Higher War College (Ober-Kriegs-Collegium), promising at the same time the recruiting officers a reward of 5 thalers for each family of a colonist settling on 3—4 łany, and 3 thalers for each family settling on a smaller area.

Minister Voss, in connection with the expected inflow of settlers from the Reich, reminded the chambers to limit the number of newcomers from the Prussian provinces, but there was no longer an express prohibition on settling them. Subjects of the Prussian king could, however, be settled only on waste land (wüste Ländereien), on condition that they received no help from the state of the kind provided for colonists coming from outside the frontiers of the Prussian kingdom[^41]. The Prussian king laid the main stress on developing the colonising action precisely in South Prussia, as a province important to him because of the connection between Silesia and East Prussia[^42]. By an order of 15 May 1799 Frederick William ordered Voss to prepare new land for colonists. The minister was also to carry out the earlier royal order issued with regard to the nobility granted the South Prussian indigenat. Besides, the lessees of the royal domains were to be obliged to settle colonists on the domains in proportion to their income (one family for every 1000 thalers).

Voss's efforts to have the royal order issued on this matter withdrawn had no effect, because the king considered that an obligation of this kind laid on the owners of estates holding the indigenat and the lessees of domains would be an effective means of putting an end to trading in estates and, by increasing the population, would contribute to the improvement of the economy[^43]. In view of the king's position, aiming at an extension of the colonising action, the General Directory issued an instruction to the chambers explaining what land was to be set aside for colonisation. First among these were reckoned: fallow, waste land, pastures, forest land[^44], small manor farms unsuitable for larger-scale farming, abandoned peasant farms, and finally unused land near the towns, which could be set aside without harm to the manor farms for settling cottagers and gardeners.

As to the first point, the instruction explained that for purposes of colonisation those lands could be taken about which there were no disputed questions. Where there were boundary disputes, colonies could not be founded only in those places that were in dispute, with the proviso that if the chambers lost the case the rent paid by the colonists would be handed over to the owner of the land on which the colony had been founded.
