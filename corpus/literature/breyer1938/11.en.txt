On the opposite side of the Warthe valley the order laid out the town of Zagórów in 1445, joined to the monastery by a dike four kilometres long. In a report of 1820 to the authorities in Warsaw, the mayor of Zagórów gives 1307 as the year the town was founded.[^11]

From what is offered here only in short extract, it is easy to see how many-sided and varied the blessed cultural work of the German monastery at Lond was over the centuries. The historian Hockenbeck describes this work of the brothers, exemplary in every respect, as follows:

> *"The monks, familiar with the tending of the field, knew how to make their estates as useful as possible; they turned wild, waste land into fruitful soil, they cleared the dense forest, drained the swamps, regulated the water in the lakes, made roads and bridges, planted orchards and vineyards, and so turned the wilderness into smiling fields. If one considers further that the monks were a model to their neighbours not only in industry and in the sensible tilling of the soil, but were distinguished just as much by sincere piety, modesty, strict self-control and self-sacrificing love of their neighbour, and by zealous pastoral care in their villages, it is no wonder that their contemporaries gave them undivided praise, and that Archbishop Henry of Gniezno compared the order to the morning star that had risen over Poland with new splendour."*[^12]

For barely four hundred years the monastery of Lond was in German hands. Then came an abrupt change. In 1511 the Polish Sejm resolved that the monasteries which could not show their bishops chartered privileges for admitting German monks only should admit Poles to the order as well as Germans. This resolution of the Sejm ended the hitherto wholly German character of the monastery of Lond. In 1539 the archbishop appointed as the first Polish abbot the monk J.
