The municipal court was led by the castle starost, and the adjudicating composition was filled out by the deputy starost, the municipal judge, the municipal scribe and an assessor. The technical and the order matters were secured by the usher. In the 17th century the land courts entered the period of stagnation and of fall, for the land sejms did not gather for long periods, which caused their role to be taken over gradually by the municipal courts, which judged continually, and through that they gradually took over the competences of the making of the entries in the perpetual books, which before had belonged only to the land courts. The sitting of the court was opened by the castle starost, the usher called the case and the parties were called upon to present their case and the proofs in its support. Here a field for display opened for the various substitutes in procedure, who with time grew ever more active as the law grew complicated. The judgment fallen after the bench's deliberation was written into the special books, of which more will be said below, at the discussion of the working of the court chancelleries and of their role in the practice of the administration of justice.

The third kind of the courts of first instance were the podkomorski courts, arisen in the 14th century in Lesser Poland and gradually formed in the remaining voivodeships, though one must grant that their subject-matter competence was limited only to the contentious and the non-contentious matters bearing on the fixing of the boundaries between the landed immovables of the nobility and between the nobility and the royal domains, whose significance and territorial range gradually diminish, along with the grants of the royal domains made by the rulers of Poland. The bench was presided over by the podkomorzy, who was the first in the hierarchy of the land offices after the senators, in accord with the sejm constitution of the year 1611, which formally ordered the sequence of the offices in the Crown.[^12] The court of the podkomorzy had the task of the hearing of the boundary disputes, which required the personal presence of the court in the field, for the marking out of the boundary and the settling of the dispute, and thereby excluded the recourse to the commissioners, the specialists, or a relying only on the documents on which the judgment was to rest. The land offices had an honorary character, gave no place in the Senate, and divided into the dignitary ones (Lat. *dignitates*) and *officia*. The dignitaries were: the voivode, the castellan, the podkomorzy and the land judge. The *officia* are the offices: the stolnik, the podstoli, the cześnik, the podczaszego, the łowczy, the miecznik, the chorąży and the wojski. (More on the offices: Z. Góralski, Urzędy i godności w dawnej Polsce [The offices and the dignities in old Poland], Warszawa 1998, pp. 187 to 192.) The instygators, that is the prosecutors, were also central officials not of the Senate.
