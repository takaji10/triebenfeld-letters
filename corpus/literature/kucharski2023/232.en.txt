A session began with the calling of the case, where the judge in the starost's name, or the starost himself, read out the summons and heard the parties' arguments. The scribe was responsible for the drawing up of the written documents of the court. The subject-matter competence of the land courts covered at first chiefly the civil matters between noblemen, and at times the criminal ones too. Curiously, they performed the functions that we today assign to the notarial offices, namely it was before them that the contracts of the purchase and sale of landed estates and buildings were concluded, the pledge agreements drawn up, the acts of settlements with the clergy over the tithes, the divisions of the estates in the process of inheritance, the marking out of the boundaries between immovables, the writing of the donations for the monasteries, the loan agreements, as well as the suits for the payment of debts and other dues, and the entries into the land books (the perpetual ones) of the titles of ownership. The land courts adjudicated the questions of the disputes over the ownership of land and of other immovables, both between two parties of the noble estate and between a nobleman and a townsman or a churchman. A distinctive feature of that kind of courts was the hearing of the matters of the settled nobility only, the landholders. These courts gradually began to lose on their importance in the 17th century, which is bound up with the fall of the land sejmiks, which did not gather and did not elect judges nor fill the offices. The abuse of the liberum veto brought the paralysis of those sejmiks, and in consequence the nobility had no judges for the disposing of the cases pending before the land courts.

Meanwhile the unsettled nobility was subject to the jurisdiction of the second type of court — namely the municipal court. That type of judiciary had different competences, composition and manner of functioning, and its first beginnings are to be sought in the 14th century in Lesser Poland, where it supplemented the land courts functionally. They were held in the castles (in Lesser Poland at Kraków, Biecz and Sącz) and their territorial competence was close to that of the land courts, yet in its subject-matter that type of courts was proper to the civil and criminal matters of the unsettled nobility.
