They had their substitutes too, called the sub-scribes, who were the district scribes charged with the keeping of the court books for the individual districts, where the rulings of the court concerning a given district were entered. The court scribe had the task of the participation in the hearing and of the noting of its course and of the keeping of the court books, in which the content of the ruling was recorded with a short description of the circumstances of the case. The scribe "wrote into the books the witnesses given by the parties and issued the copies of the witnesses given and of the texts of the oaths that were to be performed by the witnesses, and drew up the registers of the fines adjudged to be collected"[^22]. From the 16th century the matters brought to the courts grow in number — the municipal ones in particular, which appropriated a part of the competences of the land courts (in connection with their rarer activity), which was bound up with the gaining of the so-called right of perpetuity. The chancelleries of the municipal courts specialise in the keeping of the various kinds of books, of which the natural consequence was the growth of the number of the officials. The scribe had the task of the performing of all the commands of the judge in the field of the preparation and the course of the hearing. All the court officials entering the adjudicating composition had to be chosen by the land sejmik and nominated by the monarch. "That same land scribe, who is chosen through the election by the szlachta, and is given by His Royal Grace the King, and no other whatsoever, is to administer the castle acts and to have them in his power" — we read in the statute of the year 1576[^23]. The scribes at the municipal courts were appointed by the starost.

# 3.4. The court chancellery

The court chancelleries were kept by the scribe, who was responsible for their efficient functioning. A curiosity is that the fairly uniform practice of the keeping of the court books fixed itself on the Polish lands in the 15th century.

The court books were destined for the entries of every kind of judicial ruling of a criminal as well as of a civil nature. With the beginning of each year new books were opened, to ease the chancellery work and the use of the data contained in them. The entries in the books were made in the Latin language, in a general manner describing the subject of the proceedings and the content of the ruling.
