A characteristic feature of all those functions was the absence of the requirement of the possession of a legal knowledge. To gain it was extraordinarily hard, and so it was accepted that the judges would gain the knowledge, the experience and would form the skills, according to the principle that practice makes the master, considering the individual cases. The process of that period gradually takes on the formulae and the legal institutions shaped in the European practice, yet it will never take on the uniform and consolidated form that was known, say, from the canon law. That was bound up with the resistance against the changes, which the nobility held for the danger of an "absolutum dominium" and combated by all the means. In the municipal courts the judges are appointed by the starost (with time the nobility will take over that competence too), while in the land courts the land sejmik appoints and the king confirms[^18].
