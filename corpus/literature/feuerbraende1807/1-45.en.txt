# Neue Feuerbrände (1807), part 1, p. 45
# translation by Claude

Yet still more harmful than the partition of Poland was the Treaty of Pillnitz, according to which the French state was to be dissolved. What, in the most fortunate case, could one have gained by it? Austria would then have subjugated Italy to itself, and united Alsace and Lorraine with its own frontier lands. But where lay the provinces that Prussia could have suitably incorporated into itself?

This undertaking was as impolitic as it was dangerous.

The French nation was not, merely because its government had fallen through its own peculiar wickedness and become a victim of the nation, thereby yet robbed of its collective strength. In France one could not proceed as in Poland. Protected on two sides by the sea, and against Spain by the Pyrenees, it opposed to the allies three rows of fortresses, and gave birth to a national strength which, exalted by the delirium of liberty[^3] of that time, obtains in it a mighty point of support, which as a general idea had communicated itself to all.
