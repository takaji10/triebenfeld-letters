# translation by the editor's files

 The German-Finnish offensive in the north begins – just as the newspapers had earlier announced! – in June, precisely on 29 June. General Eduard Dietl, commanding the 20th Mountain Army (Armia Leppland), gives the order to attack. The campaign was given the code name *Silberfuchs* (Silver Fox). As the Finns themselves put it, for them it was a war of continuation – they wanted to win back the territories lost to the Russians.

The Germans wanted something more. The main objective of the operation was the port of Murmansk and, first of all, cutting the route between Murmansk and Petrozavodsk and Leningrad. For the Red Army the railway line, by which military equipment flowing from the USA and England was transported to the port of Murmansk, was of key strategic importance. Finnish Petsamo, lying beyond the Arctic Circle, Russian Pieczenga, where large forces were concentrated, is a settlement lying on the river of the same name, which flows into a fjord whose beginning lies 5 km from the Barents Sea.

From later letters we learn that Mielke was stationed a few kilometres from Petsamo – in Parkkino. In this small locality, situated on the river Petsamojoki, sappers built a bridge through which the entire supply of the attacking troops passed. The task of the company in which Bruno Mielke served may have been, among other things, the protection of this bridge. The attack of the combined Finnish and German formations did not succeed. Already on 17 July the Russians stopped the entire offensive. The terrain, too, proved to be a barrier impossible to overcome. Only 140 km separate Petsamo from Murmansk. The Germans controlled valuable nickel deposits and still tried to resume operations, but on 22 September the führer's order came to break off combat operations. (The bridge at Parkkino was destroyed in a daring action by a Russian flyer on 28 September, thereby cutting off from essential supplies the two fighting divisions that remained in the north-east). The front line on this section remained stable right up to September 1944.
