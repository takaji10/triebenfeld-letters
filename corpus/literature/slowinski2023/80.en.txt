# translation by the editor's files

So for the time being he is doing well. Sleeping, clothing and food are fine. He is even surprised "how they transport all of it so far, to this wild region". And one more astonishing request. Someone stole 25 safety pins from his medical bag. He asks – if they can – that they be sent to him. He will put them back in, and in this way he will not have to report the theft.

It is worth paying attention to the dense correspondence which Bruno Mielke conducts. And it is not merely his "privilege". The OKW – the High Command of the Wehrmacht – attached great importance to the smooth functioning of the field post. It was believed that frequent contact of the soldiers with home, with their loved ones, raised morale in the army, and also had a positive influence on the mood in the families, that is, on the home front. Regardless of where he was stationed on the distant eastern front, in Finland or Norway, the letters circulated efficiently and as a rule reached their addressee. From reading them one can infer that there were no particular restrictions as to the number of postcards or letters sent. What was rigorously observed, however, were weight and formats. Mail to the front reached the battalion, where it was distributed to the companies, for those units were best informed about the whereabouts of the addressee.

Almost two weeks before Christmas, on 12 December 1943, on another Sunday, Mielke writes from "his cozy little medical room". In this time, when services are being held in all the churches, he can again lead his soul to where it feels best and where it will find untroubled peace. "How this certainty of the deep grace of God fills me with strength and joy". There is no good news for home. He cannot count on leave during the Holidays. The long queue of those senior to him still stands before him. Perhaps they will let him go in January, "which may mean a long cold journey, but others too travel in winter, alive there and back, so I suppose I shall endure it as well".
