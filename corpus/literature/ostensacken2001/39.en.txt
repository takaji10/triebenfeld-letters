With John Baildon's support a blast furnace provided with coke firing could then be lit at Hohenlohe-Hütte in 1804, which in its technical equipment did not need to shun the comparison with the state works. Besides, a coal pit lying in "Fristen" was set up again. The outbreak of war between Prussia and France in 1806 led the undertaking, only just begun, into a severe crisis.

"It is hard to judge from afar", the privy councillor Johann Wolfgang von Goethe had once observed. The duties of service which Prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had to perform as Prussian general and governor of Breslau prevented both a regular presence in his residence at Ingelfingen and in the immediate neighbourhood of the Upper Silesian industrial works. In his nature the prince was already rather like the people of the post-industrial age. Having to be present, by his profession, at constantly changing places of deployment brought with it a flightiness of presence that had especially fatal consequences for his economic interests. In the summer of 1806 the loss of the independence of the Empire struck him, joined with the annexation of his lordship by king Friedrich I of Württemberg.[^p39-26]

Of his military capacities, despite strong short-sightedness and violent fits of gout that plagued him at times, still entirely convinced, he had once on the occasion of a banquet in the Hohenlohe heartland announced to the representative of the French military of his "greatest" political antagonist, Napoleon: "J'estimerai le jour pour le plus heureux de ma vie, ou je pourrai me trouver vis-à-vis de lui, pour le combattre." [I shall esteem the day the happiest of my life on which I may find myself face to face with him, to fight him.][^p39-27] In the following autumn of 1806 the exact opposite came to pass. By Napoleon and his troops the Prussian field commander was taught the most painful defeat in the double battle of Jena and Auerstedt, which finally put an end to his military career.

From the battle, which had been fought by the Prussian main army with 58000 soldiers and 46500 soldiers of the Saxon division as well as by Rüchel's reinforcement corps with 15260 men, the prince could send home only a remnant troop of 12000 survivors.[^p39-28] Napoleon's victory meant not only the prince's greatest personal military defeat, it was the complete collapse of Prussia. Not least because of the extremely hard terms of peace which the French emperor, out of his own grace, dictated to the Prussian king Friedrich Wilhelm III already on 25 October 1806: beside the recognition of the Confederation of the Rhine he demanded a war indemnity of 100 million francs and Prussia's renunciation of the land west of the Elbe, but also the greater part of the Polish territories occupied by Prussia with the town of Danzig. This was altogether 190,000 square kilometres with 3.8 million inhabitants.[^p39-29]
