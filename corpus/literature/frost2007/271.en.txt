## III

If the influence of the Latin civilization of Western Europe was strong in the development of the Polish nobility, the nobility of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, while increasingly open to western ideas and concepts, emerged from a more distinctly eastern tradition, in which military service lay at the heart of the system. From the thirteenth century the Grand Dukes of Lithuania ruled vast tracts of sparsely populated territory including the Lithuanian heartland round Wilno and Troki and much of the western Ruthenian lands (present-day Belarus and Ukraine) which came under Lithuanian rule after the collapse of Kievan Rus in the face of the Mongol onslaughts of the thirteenth century. Constant warfare against the Tatars, the pagan tribes of the Baltic coastlands, the Teutonic Knights who sought to subdue them, and various eastern Slavic princes meant that the transformation from a military to a landed estate took rather longer than in Poland.[^12]

The Grand Duchy before the 1386 Union with Poland was an uneasy amalgam of pagan Lithuanians and Orthodox eastern Slavs. The influence of east Slavic culture was strong: since Orthodox clergymen were probably the only literate members of society, Old Court Ruthenian – an ancestor of modern Belorussian and Ukrainian – became the official language. Western influences, however, were always important, through contacts with Poland and the Teutonic Knights. By the fourteenth century, the sources describe the élites of Lithuanian society in various ways: in Latin as nobiles, nobiliores, satrapae, potentiores, barones, meliores; an individual might be known as shliaikhtich, from the Polish szlachcic, or in eastern terms as kniaz or boiar.[^13] Quite what these terms meant in practice is not always easy to determine. East-Slavic influences remained strong: the importance of princely blood was recognized, and descendants of the houses of Gedymin, the Lithuanian ruling dynasty, and Rurik, which had ruled Kievan Rus, were entitled to use the title kniaz (prince, duke). The Grand Duke maintained a small council of high nobles; otherwise, there were various ranks of servitors, who might be bonded men, free peasants or enjoy the greater privileges of the zemianin – a member of the military service class.

It was in the context of the growing political relationship with Poland from the late fourteenth century that a noble Estate in the western sense began to develop. Lithuania, facing fierce challenges from the Teutonic Knights and from Muscovy, increasingly looked to Poland for military support. After the Lithuanian Grand Duke Jogaila (1377–1434) converted to Catholicism, taking the name Władysław Jagiełło, to become King of Poland in 1386, he wooed the pagan Lithuanian boyars into adopting the Catholic faith in 1387 by granting them certain privileges, the most important of which, for the creation of a true landed nobility, was the removal of service obligations on private estates and the introduction of rights of inheritance.
