It is true that once a family was established at the peak of noble society any decline tended to be relative. There were ways to stave off disaster, or at least to preserve one's position. The property rights enjoyed by women meant that one option was to marry, although marriage could pose threats of its own. In 1642 Stanisław Lubomirski (1583–1649), divided his eighteen towns, 316 villages and 163 estates between his three sons, although the death of the youngest in 1663 saw his share divided between his brothers. The younger surviving brother, Jerzy Sebastian, managed eventually to build up his holdings to 229 villages and towns, but that included several starosties which he lost on his impeachment in 1664. He had, however, five sons and two daughters from two marriages, which caused the subdivision of his estates, a blow from which the junior line of the Lubomirskis never really recovered. The senior line was more fortunate: Aleksander Michał, Stanisław's eldest son, had only one child, Józef Karol, who married in 1683 the fabulously wealthy Teofilia Zasław-Ostrogska, heiress to the Ostrogski entail and the vast Zasławski fortune. On the death of Józef's only son in 1720, however, the estate went not to Jerzy Sebastian's descendants, but to Paweł Sanguszko, who married Józef's daughter Marianna.[^86] A match like this could transform a family's position. In 1724 Maria Zofia Sieniawska (1699–1771), heiress to the Sieniawski fortune, married Stanisław Denhoff, Lithuanian Field Hetman. On his death in 1728, she inherited his massive estate, which made her an even greater catch in 1731 for August Czartoryski, who had lost out to Sanguszko in the race for the Lubomirski inheritance, but now succeeded in establishing the financial basis which enabled the Czartoryskis – known as 'the Family' – to launch one of the most successful attempts ever by a magnate faction to dominate central politics.

Such transactions only benefited a handful, however; since wealthy heiresses were in short supply, competition for their favours was fierce and occasionally bloody.[^87] Most had to look elsewhere to maintain their position. The important role played by royal land in sustaining magnate incomes meant that even well-established families required access to starosties to provide for their heirs and to compensate for the effects of partible inheritance. This in itself was a source of intense instability, since so much of the monarchy's influence – and many of its problems – derived from the fact that, as with office, demand far outstripped supply: the tariff of 1771 listed a mere 1400 separate royal estates and leaseholds of various kinds (excluding Lithuania). The king did not enjoy complete freedom of action.
