This trend reached its peak in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth century, as Władysław Łokietek struggled to reunite the Polish state, which he ruled as king from 1320. The death of Władysław's son, Casimir the Great (1333–70), without a male heir – although it did not lead to the break-up of the kingdom – ensured that the nobility was able to maintain and extend the privileges it had won while central authority had been weak.

By the mid-fourteenth century, any trend towards a feudal hierarchy had been decisively reversed. Immunities and privileges were granted to the szlachta as a whole: nobilitas was now taken to apply to all who possessed land iure militari and who could, according to statutes passed under Casimir the Great, demonstrate noble descent. Even those who did not own land, but were noble by descent – the szlachta nieosiadła or impossessionati – were regarded as nobles, even if they did not enjoy all the privileges of the Estate. This trend towards equality of treatment was consolidated in the great wave of privileges granted by Casimir's successors after 1370 as the price of recognition as king. The emergence of noble clans, comprising groups of noble families of differing status under a common coat-of-arms as a focus of identity, encouraged the idea that the nobility was one estate with common privileges: adoption into a clan remained a way into the nobility until the sixteenth century.[^10]

Despite this trend, the nobility remained highly diverse, reflecting differences between the kingdom's component provinces. The influence of magnates was greater in Little Poland than in Great Poland, where the middle and petty nobility was both numerous and powerful. Royal Prussia came under the Polish Crown after the Thirteen Years War against the Teutonic Knights (1454–66). It only began sending official envoys to the Sejm in 1569, and it maintained its own political structure into the eighteenth century. The continuing importance of the great trading cities of Danzig, Elbing and Thorn meant that the nobility was never in as dominant a position as elsewhere: representatives of these three cities sat in the Senate of the Prussian Landtag (the only bicameral sejmik in the Commonwealth), and the lesser cities sent envoys to the lower house until 1662. The duchy of Mazovia, part of the Polish state until 1138, was only reincorporated in 1529; it remained the heartland of the petty nobility. The dukes of Mazovia, engaged in constant battles with the pagan Prussian and Lithuanian tribes, had encouraged settlement of the forest wastelands which made up so much of their duchy by liberally distributing charters of nobility to colonists, whose numerous descendants defended their noble status with tenacity: so numerous were they that Bartłomiej Paprocki, who published his Coats of Arms of the Polish Nobility in 1584, gave up in despair when he came to Mazovia.[^11]
