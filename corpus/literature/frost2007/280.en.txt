In some areas, it was substantially higher: 89.6 per cent of the land was already held by noble smallholders by the end of the sixteenth century in the district of Nur, 79.5 per cent in the district of Wizna and 78.7 per cent in the district of Łomża.[^33] Mazovia was exceptional, but in the Commonwealth as a whole it is estimated that by the end of the eighteenth century some 125,000 nobles (16.7 per cent) were comfortably off (bene possessionati); there were some 300,000 lesser-noble property-holders (40 per cent), 150,000 who rented their property (20 per cent) and 175,000 in service and various professions (23.3 per cent).[^34]

The large number of noble smallholders and poor nobles was encouraged by the laws and customs surrounding the inheritance of land. The effects of partible inheritance were exacerbated by the protection afforded by law to the property rights of noblewomen. Normally, a quarter of an estate was allocated to any daughters to provide them with dowries, the rest was divided up amongst the sons. Often shares were allocated by the father to sons as they came of age, subject to guarantees designed to protect widows in respect of the property they had brought to the couple as dowry.[^35] Partible inheritance tended rapidly to reduce the family patrimony and income; by the late sixteenth century perhaps the majority of the Commonwealth's nobility was poor or at least impoverished. The economic condition of many was often worse than that of peasants, and was frequently commented on by foreigners: in the 1670s, Ulrich Werdum, from Frisia, expressed his surprise at seeing a Mazovian nobleman proudly wearing his sabre as he drove manure to his field himself.[^36] Surplus children were sent to serve richer nobles, or rented tiny plots of land from larger landowners, or took up a military career, or migrated to the towns, where they swelled the ranks of the indigent szlachta brukowa, the ‘pavement nobility', or dropped out of the nobility altogether.

The relative impoverishment of so many and the constant threat of poverty for a great many more ensured that many of the theoretical constraints upon the economic activities permitted to nobles were, in practice, ignored. At the end of the sixteenth century, the English traveller Fynes Moryson observed:

> In generali, a gentelman will not marry a marchants daughter, nor any ignoble woman, for any riches whatsoever, and if any should so marry, his Kinsmen would force him to be divorced, for they are so carefull not to stayne their nobility, insomuch as a gentleman will not buy or sell anything, but his owne come and catle.[^37]
