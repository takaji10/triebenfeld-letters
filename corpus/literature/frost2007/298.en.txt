Janusz Radziwiłł's abortive attempt in the 1650s to exploit separatist feelings in Lithuania and replace John Casimir with Charles X Gustav of Sweden all but destroyed the position of his branch of the family. Finally, in 1700 it was the support of Augustus II for the local szlachta which helped finally break the pernicious grip of the Sapiehas, who had themselves been built up by Sobieski to break the Radziwiłł and Pac dominance of Lithuania established under John Casimir.

## IX

The result was anarchic stalemate. By 1721, with the Commonwealth's international position in ruins, its political system apparently paralysed and its economy wrecked, the lofty ideals of the mid-sixteenth century seemed to have been destroyed. Yet as the gap between ideal and reality grew, it became increasingly difficult to sustain the pretence. The political strains of the period after 1717, when Russia virtually established a protectorate over the Commonwealth, against the background of enlightened ideas which no longer saw arguments from tradition or authority as necessarily conclusive, finally helped undermine szlachta solidarity. The result was unexpected: as noble society began to fragment under its last king, Stanisław August Poniatowski (1764–95), this apparently moribund system regenerated itself. The reforms of the Four Year Sejm (1788–92) culminating in the Constitution of 3 May 1791 may have come too late to preserve the Commonwealth – indeed they helped provoke its destruction – but they embodied a dramatic break with the Sarmatian past.

The growing appreciation of the chasm between ideal and reality stimulated not only an intense debate about the political reform which was so clearly necessary, but also a fundamental re-examination of the very assumptions on which the whole socio-political structure had rested since the mid-sixteenth century. The reign of Poniatowski – himself from the reform-minded Czartoryski 'Family' – was dominated by the problem of reform. Partly under the influence of enlightened ideas, the Commonwealth's constitution was subjected to growing criticism and unfavourable comparisons with other European states. In Court circles, the blinkered conservatism of Sarmatian traditionalists was mocked. Yet there were important barriers to reform. Poniatowski, ex-lover of Catherine II, had been elevated to the throne with Russian support; although he was determined to reform, he soon split from his uncles in the Family, who wished to direct operations.
