Gradually, the szlachta established control of the political system. The need for noble consent to taxation required the gathering of the nobility in the local sejmiki (dietines). From 1454, the king could not summon the nobility to perform its military duty in the general levy (pospolite ruszenie) without the agreement of the sejmiki, which were henceforth to scrutinize all new legislation. To facilitate the conduct of business, a bicameral Sejm (Diet) was established in the 1490s, consisting of the Senate – comprising Catholic bishops, government ministers and the palatines and castellans who were the chief provincial officials – and the Chamber of Envoys, composed of delegates (usually two) from each sejmik. By the statute of Nihil Novi (1505) all new legislation had to be passed by the Sejm. Following the parliamentary union of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania at Lublin in 1569, the Sejm became exclusively noble. On the extinction of the Jagiellonian dynasty in the male line in 1572, the right of all nobles to participate in royal elections was institutionalized; henceforward the election of the monarch viritim was regarded as the fundamental guarantee of noble liberty. Finally, the nobility took control of its own judicial affairs with the establishment of separate tribunals in Poland (1578) and Lithuania (1581) as supreme appellate courts for cases involving nobles, in which judges were elected by the nobility, not appointed by the king. These three developments extended noble control over the executive, legislative and judicial functions of the state. According to Laboureur, ‘There is no more true Nobility in the world than that of this Kingdom; there is none also which enjoys such advantageous privileges.’[^4]

By 1600 Poland-Lithuania seemed to be – and was seen to be – a state run by nobles for the nobles. Indeed, it was the extent of power and privilege won by the Polish szlachta which helped persuade Lithuanian nobles to accept political union against the opposition of a small group of magnates. From 1569, powerful forces of cultural integration operated among the noble élites of Poland and Lithuania, coalescing round a shared vision of a state based on the perfect coincidence of citizenship with noble status. This was justified by a heady mixture of classical political theory, whose principles were adapted to match a noble-dominated political system,[^5] with an elaborate justification of this monopoly through the creation of a mythical common past and identity for the new Commonwealth's ruling class. This identity-myth claimed that the szlachta, whether of Polish, Lithuanian, Ruthenian or German descent, formed one political nation, descended from the ancient Sarmatian tribe which had long resisted the Roman Empire.
