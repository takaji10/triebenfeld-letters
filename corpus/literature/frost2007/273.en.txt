The privileges of 1492 and 1506 agreed that the prince could decide nothing new without the agreement of his council, but the growing power of the magnates was matched by the political assertiveness of the middling Lithuanian servitors, who were still required to serve and who did not enjoy the full rights and privileges of the upper levels of Lithuanian society. As the practice of meeting in local deliberative bodies on the Polish pattern spread, the assertiveness of the middling Lithuanian nobles increased. When called out to fight at the start of the Livonian War in September 1562, large numbers of servitors refused to obey the commands of their leaders, demanding union and the extension to them of the privileges enjoyed by their Polish counterparts. Following the definitive establishment in the 1560s of sejmiki, which largely followed the Polish model, the Second Lithuanian Statute of 1566 formally established a bicameral Sejm on Polish lines; 3 years later the triumph of the middling nobility, in alliance with the Poles, was crowned with the Lublin Union.

There were, however, those left behind, since the very complexity of the Lithuanian system of service made it unclear who, exactly, should be included among the new class of citizens. Sigismund I (1506–48) attempted to reform and better regulate the system of service; in 1528, during the long series of wars against Muscovy, the Lithuanian Sejm fixed the amount of service required from service lands for the next 10 years; in 1529 a list of cavalry servitors was drawn up, including 19,842 men from 13,060 families, of which nearly one-third were provided by magnate families. In the 1550s, under Sigismund August (1548–72), the last of the Jagiellonian dynasty, the massive grand ducal estates were surveyed as the basis for an extensive land reform which, by tying the peasants to the land, created a substantial incentive for servitors to demonstrate their status by performing military service, and in the great muster of 1567, 27,776 cavalrymen turned out; on this occasion under a fifth (4890) were provided by the magnates. For generations, the appearance of an ancestor's name in the muster lists of 1528 and 1567 was seen as a clear proof of noble status; many petty servitors, however, were not able to afford the equipment required, and as the Union with Poland both solidified the definition of what constituted a noble and extended substantial privileges to those who were able to do so, a substantial group of boyars[^14] were effectively excluded from the Promised Land of noble privilege. Some sank into the peasantry; others managed to continue their precarious existence in service to great magnate houses.[^15]

The Union of Lublin was, however, merely one of the most important stepping-stones in the gradual – though never complete – convergence of the Commonwealth's noble élites. Cultural and political integration continued after 1569, marked by the spread of the Polish language and Catholicism among Lithuanian and Ruthenian nobles.
