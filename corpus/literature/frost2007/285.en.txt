Zamoyski built an entire Renaissance city at Zamość, complete with university and fortifications constructed according to the latest designs. Stanisław Lubomirski had a private chapel with an orchestra of fifteen and a choir of three castrati, three altos, three tenors and five bases; Władysław Dominik Zasławski's musicians cost some 12,000 zloties per annum.[^56] The 150-odd waggons taken by the Swedes from the looted Lubomirski castle of Wiśnicz in 1655 contained works of art by Raphael, Titian and Veronese, among many others; in the eighteenth century, Wincenty Potocki's collection of engravings and drawings was reputed to contain 3500 items by Rubens alone.[^57]

## VII

Wealth brought power. From the late sixteenth century, the influence of a small number of families within the political system grew enormously. The limiting after 1569 of the monarchy's financial independence meant that the royal Court was in an almost constant state of penury and could not act as the centre of social life in the way it did elsewhere in Europe. Chronic suspicion of royal intentions prevented the emergence of a substantial military establishment, and the inability of the inadequate fiscal system to pay the Commonwealth's forces during the long cycle of defensive wars between 1648 and 1721 meant that the royal government was wholly unable to provide careers compatible with noble ideas of suitable employment which impoverished nobles might have welcomed and which the developing military and administrative systems of the Commonwealth's neighbours had begun to provide: by the mid-eighteenth century, a substantial number of nobles from Great Poland were serving in the Prussian army.

Wealthy nobles partly filled this gap. It was on their estates, at their courts and in their private military retinues that poorer nobles sought employment as factors, administrators or servants. Others leased or rented lands from magnates who could not or would not farm all their lands directly, especially in the second half of the seventeenth century, when the depredations of war stimulated the widespread development of the szlachta czynszowa: landless nobles who rented their properties. In consequence, magnates commanded significant clientèles made up of nobles who depended on them directly or indirectly for their livelihood.[^58] The Commonwealth's political structure gave these clientèles great scope: the liberum veto – the extension of the principle of unanimity in debate which enabled one envoy to break the proceedings of a Sejm or sejmik, first recognized in 1652 – enabled faction leaders to block the policies of their rivals, while the fact that envoys to the Sejm were delegates from their sejmiki, furnished with instructions, not representatives with plenipotentiary powers, meant that magnates were able to use their local power bases to win significant leverage at the centre of politics.
