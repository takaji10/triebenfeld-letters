The greater part of the entail's income came from rent, which constituted on average 34.2 per cent of income between 1767 and 1798 and the propinacja – the exploitation of the nobility's practical monopoly of the production and sale of alcohol – which produced an average 29.9 per cent of income between 1767 and 1798.[^50] The propinacja, in which landlords made huge profits by selling alcohol to their own peasants, was merely one example of the way in which they sought to maximize income through a kind of mercantilist policy designed to exploit the serf-based system. By the eighteenth century, many estates were involved in manufacturing enterprises of various kinds – particularly glass and textiles – not merely the production of alcohol, and the estate's peasants were frequently (if not always effectively) forbidden to buy certain products except from their overlord. These enterprises were designed to capitalize on the raw materials in such abundant supply on the great latifundia, but the practical problems, not least of transport, meant that the vast majority, apart from the breweries and distilleries, were short-lived.[^51]

In the favourable political and economic conditions down to the mid-seventeenth century, the foundations of the staggering fortunes of such as the Radziwiłłs – one of the richest noble families in Europe – the Sieniawskis or the Zasławskis were laid. It is difficult, however, to establish just how profitable these latifundia were. Estimates of magnate income by dumbstruck foreign observers, or even by nobles themselves, should be taken with a certain degree of scepticism.[^52] It has been estimated that Janusz Ostrogski's annual income from his vast estates probably did not exceed 400,000 zloties in the early seventeenth century, about double that of Jan Zamoyski.[^53] Nevertheless, a tiny group was certainly able to lead lives of great splendour, and their courts were major centres of patronage and employment for many impoverished nobles. Stanisław Lubomirski (1583–1649) maintained a permanent court of some 270 people, excluding menial servants. Many magnates kept private military retinues, as much for display as for defence, but they could mobilize large numbers in an emergency. Lubomirski himself maintained some 600 men which, with uniforms and arms, must have cost him something like 108,000 zloties per year.[^54] Tomasz Zamoyski brought 1700 to the 1632 royal election, while that of 1669 drew substantial contingents: Private wars between these forces were not uncommon.

These men were wealthy and cosmopolitan aristocrats well able to dazzle their European contemporaries. When they travelled abroad they made a particular effort to impress: the entrance of Jerzy Ossoliński to Rome in 1633 caused a sensation, accompanied as he was by 300 retainers in Polish costume, 30 mules in scarlet, ten camels in gilded silk, a personal guard of 30 cavalrymen and six splendid Turkish horses trotting on gold horseshoes.[^55] Travel certainly broadened the mind: the courts of these magnates gave ample testimony to the sophisticated influences picked up abroad.
