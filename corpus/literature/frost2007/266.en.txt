# 9 The Nobility of Poland–Lithuania, 1569–1795

Robert I. Frost

University of Aberdeen

## I

> We live in the present, we know not the future: possessions are nothing but accidental, subject to all kinds of vagaries; but Nobility is eternal.[^1]

Even for a sophisticated Frenchman like Jean le Laboureur, visiting Poland in 1645, the noble society of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was impressive enough to stimulate philosophical awe. In a Europe dominated so completely by noble élites, the Commonwealth was the noble state par excellence. Foreigners marvelled at what the Irishman Bernard Connor termed the ‘extraordinary Grandeur of the Nobility’, which enjoyed unparalleled powers and privileges:

> the Polish Nation is divided into two sorts of People, the Gentry or Free-born Subjects, who are hardly a tenth Part of the Kingdom, and the Vassals, who are no better than Slaves to the Gentry, for they have no Benefit of the Laws, can buy no Estates, nor Enjoy any Property, no more than our Negroes in the West-Indies can.[^2]

Though this was an exaggeration, the extent of noble power certainly seemed impressive. Already by the early sixteenth century, the Polish szlachta (nobility) was a legally defined Estate, with extensive privileges at law.[^3] In 1374, it was exempted from ordinary taxation on hereditary noble land without its explicit consent. By the principle of neminem captivabimus nisi iure victum, accepted by the monarchy in 1433, no nobleman could be imprisoned unless formally convicted of a crime.
