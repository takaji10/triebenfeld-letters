Although the szlachta enjoyed, in theory at least, a greater degree of social and political power than nobles elsewhere in Europe, nobility was defined in essentially similar terms. Szymon Starowolski (1588–1653), from a poor Volhynian noble background, who rose to be a canon of Cracow Cathedral, wrote that

> The Polish people is first divided into the equestrian order, or the nobility, and the common people; secondly into the ecclesiastic and lay orders. The nobility is distinguished for the purpose of government and defence, the common people for agriculture, mercantile and mechanical pursuits: on the other hand, the priests are dedicated to the worship of GOD.[^7]

Marcin Kromer (1512–89), Bishop of Warmia, stressed the importance of virtue, of living in a noble fashion, of land ownership, and of descent:

> The Polish nobility separated itself off for the government and defence of the rest of the people and of the country. Thus those who form this estate in former times were called knights and now – because of their duty to provide service as cavalrymen – are most frequently called equites, by those who speak and write Latin; in the mother tongue, however, they are called shliahta, from the szlachetność (nobility) of their family – in which, if I am not mistaken, the word was borrowed from the German (Geschlechter) – and ziemianie (the landed ones) or, as it is commonly explained, terrigene. This name comes from the lands and fields which they till and where they have lived so long, which they inherit, buy or are granted by the prince.[^8]

The rationalization of the nobility's privileged position, and the idealizations of noble life offered by poets and political theorists were, therefore, highly conventional: nobility was essentially transmitted by descent, having originally been earned for the services rendered by a noble's forebears. Since nobility was in origin a reward for virtue and service, especially military service, nobles could be created, although nobles by birth enjoyed greater status than those who had been born commoners.[^9] A nobleman was expected to maintain a noble lifestyle and perform the functions proper to his station: he was to live as a gentleman, to be based on the land and to defend the fatherland in time of danger; indeed, the legal requirement to serve in the general levy survived into the eighteenth century.
