A series of social, economic, political and cultural developments had ensured that by 1788 it was no longer sustainable. The attachment to birth as the essential defining characteristic of a noble had been steadily eroded from the mid-seventeenth century; indeed, as early as 1568 Wawrzyniec Goślicki, who commended the laws of the Rhodians for depriving of their property those sons of virtuous fathers who lived viciously, had argued that ‘it is not a Hall painted full of proude Armes or badges, but vertue which maketh a man Noble'.[^102] Noble status in the Commonwealth had never been primarily determined by relationship to the means of production, but was a legal and cultural construct; nevertheless, the idea of the nobility as essentially a landed Estate had always lain at the heart of that ideal. Yet, despite the domination of the political system by the szlachta, the law had proved incapable of bridging the gap between ideal and reality: noble birth could not guarantee a noble lifestyle. The bonds of solidarity felt by middling nobles with the swelling band of the penurious, boorish, frequently illiterate noble proletariat were steadily weakening, especially since poor nobles and those renting or leasing land from wealthy magnates formed the vast bulk of the obedient footsoldiers in the magnate clientes which allowed magnate factions to manipulate and obstruct the political system.

From the mid-seventeenth century, in consequence, a steady process of change had begun to separate out those privileges of nobility which were attached to the person, and those enjoyed only by landholders. Some of these – exemption from taxation and customs privileges and the inviolability of property – could of their very nature only be enjoyed by property owners. Increasingly, however, a number of privileges in civil law were restricted to property owners, including personal freedom from arrest and the ability to hold public office. A number of sejmiki began to exclude non-property owners from their deliberations; a larger number began to question their right to participate. All that remained of the panoply of privilege to a growing number was the right to acquire landed property, which was usually beyond the means of the poor szlachta, and preferential treatment regarding admission to ecclesiastical office, military service or the bar.[^103]

By 1764 a growing number of middling szlachta and reform-minded members of the richer nobility were willing not only to redefine nobility, but to admit certain non-nobles openly into their ranks.
