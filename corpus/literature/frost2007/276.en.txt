As significant as the size of the nobility was its highly uneven distribution, which reflected the differing histories and traditions of the Commonwealth's component parts. In general, there were fewer nobles in Lithuania and Ukraine – less than 0.5–1 per cent in the palatinates of Kiev and Bratslav – than in Poland, although parts of western Lithuania, especially Samogitia (Żemaitija), also had a high concentration of petty nobles. Even within Poland there were substantial variations, with fewer nobles on the whole in Royal Prussia and Little Poland than in Great Poland. The tendency of the petty szlachta to group together meant that local concentrations could be high, even in Little Poland: although some parishes in the Cracow palatinate had no noble inhabitants, there were 23 where the noble population was over 5 per cent, and four where it was between 8.6 per cent and 12.8 per cent.[^18] It was, however, Mazovia which saw the greatest concentration: some estimates put the noble population as high as 20–25 per cent, and villages inhabited entirely by nobles were not uncommon.

No estimate of the size of the nobility can ever be entirely accurate, since it proved impossible in practice to establish a firm boundary between noble and commoner, despite the apparently simple definition of nobility and the control the nobility exercised in theory and at law over its own membership. The number of formal ennoblements did diminish after the king was deprived of his independent initiative in 1578: the Sejm created 334 new nobles, mostly for military service, between 1578 and 1601, and granted Polish nobility (indygenat) to six foreigners. Another 336 native ennoblements and 183 rights of indygenat were granted between 1601 and 1764.[^19] According to the Third Lithuanian Statute (1588) Jews converting to Catholicism automatically obtained noble status, although the numbers involved appear to have been small until the 1760s.[^20]

There were less formal ways of entering the nobility. In such a vast State, with such a large population of petty nobles, it was not difficult for a commoner, particularly if he had money, to adopt the hallmarks of a noble lifestyle and to pass himself off as noble. Since the bearing of arms had long been regarded as one of the central functions of the nobility, and since there was always a substantial number of commoners in the Commonwealth's armies, it was relatively easy for commoners with long military service to slip into the nobility without formal ennoblement, especially since they often served among petty, landless nobles. But military service was not essential: Many simply adopted a surname of the noble type and moved: Mazovia's numerous population of petty nobles was constantly overspilling into other regions and it was easy to claim noble status in a far-off part of the Commonwealth where it was difficult for checks to be made. Another favourite route was through service to magnates, who felt more able to rely on the loyalty of clients whose status was in some doubt.
