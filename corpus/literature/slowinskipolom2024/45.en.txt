If, after such preparation, the man summoned refused to put on the uniform, he was murdered in a bestial manner. The Protestant parishioners carried on unofficial slaughtering and trade, and many gave their Catholic neighbours various kinds of support. Yet not all were willing, and some were simply afraid, for a general terror was raging. It was not known who would report whom to the Gestapo: a Pole or a German?

The events of the war, and the division into “equals and more equals” – that is, the nationality groups – affected the worsening of relations between Protestants and Catholics. For people, for their humanity, for Christian values, there came a time of heavy trial. Not everyone emerged victorious from that trial – both among the Protestants and among the Catholics. There were examples of self-sacrifice, of risking one's life for others, but there was also persecution. During the war, some Protestants made no secret of their anti-Polish sympathies and looked down with a sense of superiority on the Polish Catholic population. Some made themselves sorely felt by the Poles and contributed to their death, their deportation to concentration camps, or other forms of persecution. As early as November 1939, there came arrests, imprisonment and secret executions of members of the Polish Military Organisation. This was retribution for the “Barka” action of 1918. A group of Polish fighters, during an attempt to sink a barge carrying grain requisitioned from the local farmers, killed one of the bargemen. Both the Poles and the mortally wounded German knew one another; the victim, named Klemsztajn, came from Wrąbczynek. The second of the boatmen, the brother of the man shot dead, escaped and filed a report at the gendarmerie post of a robbery and murder. He, too, knew well the people who had set upon them. No arrests took place at the time; Poland regained her independence and chaos reigned at the gendarmerie posts. The family of the murdered man, however, promised vengeance, which was carried out only on the night of 22 November 1939. In the forest near Grabina, members of the POW were tortured to death: Karol Rychliński, Franciszek Pluciński, Jan Kmieciak, Feliks Molski, Antoni Płócienniczak and Stanisław Budaszewski. Together with them perished men who did not belong to the POW and had nothing to do with the “Barka” action: Tadeusz Płócienniczak, Mikołaj Smorawiński, Marek Paradziński and Józef Krata[^19].



[^19]: The irony of fate was that in 1918 it was Romuald Brzeg who fired from the barge – the only one of the "Peowiacy" who managed to escape and avoid death in 1939.
