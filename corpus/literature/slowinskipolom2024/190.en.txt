Although he does not say it outright, he nevertheless tries to prepare his nearest "for the worst" – that is, for the occupation of the Wartheland by the troops advancing from the east. "The war situation gives me much to think about. We want to fight and struggle to the last – but must we not look at the dangers – precisely as realistically and soberly looking Germans? I do not wish to see the lamentation, tears, suffering with which our nation is visited, should the threatening dangers materialise! How they will then treat you, the poorest of all, the liberated Poles! May God give us the strength to endure and preserve us from misery! But perhaps God wishes already to bring all matters to an end, then this is by no means excluded. And therefore we ought to persevere in the faith; then even the greatest cares will not overtake us. There is also another thought-provoking sentence. "We wish also to forgive those and to ask (for grace) for them whom we by our conduct have hurt and in this way have threatened their souls". The letter ends with the honest confession that shivers run through him at the thought that once he urged his brother to move to Konin! "It is the grace of God that in this time moving towards destruction we have this quiet island". He wishes them "to live through in Łazińsk many sunny and happy days...".

This and other letters show to what an extent the war destroyed, and at the same time laid bare, the complicated sense of national identity, the consciousness of one's own roots, but also of simple human decency, of many German settlers. For we often read of the "wonderfully simple and therefore beautiful childhood", of the joyful days lived through in Łazińsk, while at the same time Mielke writes that before the war this was for them "the hard period of Poland". In these accounts there is a peculiar alienation, a kind of extraterritoriality of the land that fed them. The system of "values" which the Nazi ideologues had for years been instilling into the Germans and which came to reign in the conquered Wartheland shakes, or rather suppresses in these pious people the age-old moral order built on the biblical Ten Commandments.
