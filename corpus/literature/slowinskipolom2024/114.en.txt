He arrived safely in the lands lying a short distance east of Konin, in the vicinity of Kramsk in eastern Wielkopolska. In this region a new stage of his life began, a new chapter in the existence of the Beutler family. The villages of Bilczew and Klonowa, and then, a little further west of Konin, Święcia and Dąbrowica, are the places where the Beutlers would live, work and die for over two centuries.

It was the beginning of the nineteenth century. Living conditions in the new territory, though fertile and bountiful, were not easy at the time. Despite many exemptions from obligations owed to the manor or the Church — which was a privilege of the Hauländer settlers — their life was no fairy tale. Hauländer law, under which the settler villages were founded, guaranteed above all the personal freedom of the settlers, long-term and even perpetual leasehold of the land, with the possibility of buying it out — provided the manor or the Church, which was the founder of the village, agreed — as well as the passing of the land to one's heirs. Draining floodplains, bogs and waterlogged meadows, wresting from the water land that could be cultivated, clearing the forests and thickets that often covered large areas of the terrain given over to the settlers' use, building homesteads and farm buildings from scratch — these were the first and most fundamental tasks that the new inhabitants of these lands had to perform. Treacherous shifting sands and bogs, the depths of rivers and lakes, ice equally treacherous in winter, in summer swarms of mosquitoes and midges, which besides their painful stings often left their victims with various illnesses that decimated the population. But the will to fight, the will to survive, of these people who had covered hundreds of kilometres to find better conditions of existence for themselves and their families, a better world, was enormous. At the beginning of the nineteenth century Andreas Beutler built a house, put up farm buildings in the yard, cleared a stretch of forest, drained expanses of marshland, and as he finished one round of work he began the next, no less toilsome: tilling the soil, raising animals, grappling with a hard but prosperous life, ensuring the family's survival.
