# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 26
# translation by the editor's files

At that the conversation ended. They returned after a few days, only to hear the refusal once more. They left the office, threatening and intimidating.

– They heard good advice – my dad concluded – to occupy themselves with "their own job" and let me get on with my work.

The first mass after the war in the Evangelical church took place on 18 July 1954. My father directed the cooperative without interruption for 40 years.

To young people, fed on propaganda pap, the war appeared as an extraordinary adventure, in which victorious fighting came first. Of course, the blood that flowed was the blood of enemies. So naturally I very much wanted to hear the telling of some heroic deeds of my dear ones. Excited by another film or by a novel I had read, I would try to question my father or grandfather about that difficult time and about their wartime experiences. They could not be persuaded into longer reminiscences.

– God forbid a war – I heard many times…

Grandpa would dismiss various questions with the brief statement that whoever had not been there, had not survived the camp, the death march, hunger, would understand little. Nor did my father belong to the veteran storytellers. Apart from general sentences, very cautiously formulated, it was difficult to extract from him any precise description of an event, of human behaviour, especially that concerning the times directly after the liberation. He had many bad experiences in this, but he did not want to share them. The story of the harvest campaign was an exception.

In the corner house on Konińska Street, adjoining across Targowa Street the former cantorate house, there lived in the attic a woman whose dramatic fate I learned of only in adult life. Her attire – some rags, worn grey hair, a matte madness in the eyes and often a she-goat led on a string – it is a sight I cannot forget. And the hollow sound in the middle of the night – the sounds of wood being chopped on the corner stone adjoining the house in which shelter was given her. This was Janina Breede, née Stawicka, the wife of a well-known German doctor – one of the first ladies on the Volksliste. Her sad story is described by Katarzyna Połom in the text "Doktorowa.
