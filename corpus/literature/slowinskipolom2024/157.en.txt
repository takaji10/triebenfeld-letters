# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 157
# translation by the editor's files

He thinks with gratitude of his parents, who despite their weaknesses gave him what is most precious: "a healthy body and blood with the faith of our dear Evangelical Church", and finally of the wonderful siblings, from whose hearts beats only warm love. In the face of what is inexorably approaching, we read a letter like the life creed of Bruno Mielke and an attempt at an examination of conscience. It is also – or perhaps above all – an unspoken strong reaction to the news reaching them from soldiers returning from the first line.

Letters from the loved ones, such as those quoted earlier and the next one from Anna (of 22 September 1942), which I quote below, undoubtedly had their weight and significance in preserving the psychological stability and resilience of the private Mielke. "I am so proud that you are fighting in this decisive struggle. […] I believe that when a man such as you stands at the front and looks danger straight in the eye, small and insignificant things slip out of the soul, and it then cleanses itself, and afterwards becomes strengthened and proud. When you return, you will certainly be a different man. Take to yourself everything that is precious, and rid yourself of everything else. Conquer yourself; in that way you will return as a man of worth and nothing will be able to move you. I would so much like to shield you from all hardships, but such is the lot of man that he must pass through everything quite alone". Perhaps this girl, writing her letter just before dusk, intuitively felt a certain painful paradox which many soldiers experienced. They fought together, in a squad, in a company. On how they supported one another often depended their very lives, and on the other hand their equally dangerous enemy was the grim and oppressive feeling of loneliness.
