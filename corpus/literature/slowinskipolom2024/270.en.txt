Genowefa, as I have already mentioned, besides looking after the children, was a teacher. To this day she is remembered fondly by her surviving pupils. After the war the family was resettled to Niska Street, into a large house into which the Woźniak, Czarnecki and Falkenberg families were also billeted. The latter, with their sons Bronek and Adolf, left for Germany in 1956. In the summer of 1948 Reinhold Dalke was raking hay on a meadow by the Warta. Suddenly he heard the voice of a girl drowning in the river, coming from the direction of Zagórów. He was an excellent swimmer. The son recalls that his father crossed the river five times in one go. Reinhold Dalke threw himself to the rescue without a moment's thought. Unfortunately, exhaustion, the high temperature, the cold water and most probably the whirlpools brought about the tragedy. Mr Dalke's body was found after five hours. The funeral took place on 1 August 1948. After her husband's premature death Genowefa became attached to Julius Lim from Białobłoty, with whom she had another son, Zygfryd Lim (1949-2021). Genowefa and Julius never entered into marriage. In the turmoil of war Mr Lim's wife and two sons had gone missing. They were sought through the Polish Red Cross, but without result. Mrs Genowefa's family lived on Niska Street until 1964, when the house they occupied, like all the others on that street, was consumed by a great fire.

Significant from the point of view of the post-war fates of Polish Protestants is the anecdote Mr Jan told me about his aunts, his mother's sisters. Berta and Helena Zeigner left for Germany during the Second World War. After the war they remained there, trying somehow to arrange their lives for themselves. Similar recollections appear in many accounts of members of Hauländer families from Poland living in Germany. Although both ladies had by then lived in Germany for years and spoke German fluently, the Germans would often call them Polish swine.
