## Introduction


Mirosław Słowiński

Can there be a world without history? Can it be understood if one cannot tell of it? This book is an attempt to describe an ordinary world that great history destroyed. The small town of Zagórów, situated in eastern Wielkopolska, on the southern slope of the valley of the Warta, together with the surrounding settlements and villages was for centuries shaped by Poles, Jews and Germans. An emblematic reflection of this denominational and ethnic mixture were the houses of worship of Zagórów: the Catholic church of St Peter and Paul at the Mały Rynek, the synagogue on Średnia Street, and the Evangelical-Augsburg church on Konińska Street. The cataclysm of the Second World War brutally shattered this mosaic picture assembled over generations.

In the first half of the eighteenth century, after the hard years of 1708–1710, caused above all by the effects of the terrible onslaught of the Great Frost, which in 1709 gripped Europe from the Mediterranean to England and Scandinavia (one could cross the Baltic by sledge from Poland to Sweden), and by the struggle with unimaginable, total famine and the painful aftermath of successive epidemics of smallpox, cholera or typhus, Zagórów went through perhaps the heaviest period in its history. Universal looseness of morals, actions according to the law of the stronger, the helplessness of the owner of the town – that is, of the monastery in Ląd. From the books of the Zagórów parish preserved in the State Archive in Poznań we learn that "this sudden death was more merciless towards men than towards women, which is why after every epidemic in Zagórów and its surroundings quite a few children were born out of wedlock – as if to balance the state of the population thrown into disorder by the pestilence[^1].
