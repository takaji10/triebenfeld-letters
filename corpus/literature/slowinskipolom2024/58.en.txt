#### The Hauländers' promised land

In the 18th century the landowners possessing forests and wastelands in the Puszcza Pyzdrska – such as the nobility, monastic orders, the state treasury, the Bishop of Gniezno and the towns of Pyzdry and Zagórów – gradually came to the conclusion that for these lands to begin to yield income, they had to be put to agricultural use in a form other than that practised hitherto. Farming based on corvée labour did not meet the needs of transforming difficult, unprofitable land (poor, dry, marshy) into fertile and profitable land. Newer technologies and hands to do the work were lacking. The best way of developing these areas was to establish Hauländer settlement on them. This settlement was one of the important forms of rural rent-paying settlement in early modern Europe.

Poland, within both its historical and its present-day borders, was one of the several areas where this settlement was developed broadly and intensively in the years 1547-1864, at first with the participation of the Dutch, and then of their descendants, though mainly by Germans and Poles. The name “Hauländer settlement” derives from Old Polish terms: its pioneers, the Dutch, were called “Olędrzy” (“Olendrzy”), and above all from the Dutch law they brought with them, known as “olęderski” (Hauländer), which in the 17th–18th centuries was commonly used by the settlers of other nationalities who by then already predominated numerically.

In accordance with the rules of Hauländer law (the law of settlers of Dutch/Flemish origin), as applied in the feudal charters concluded by the founders of settlements with entire communes, the Hauländers were people who were personally free and held their land on the terms of emphyteusis[^26], at first in the sense of a multi-year, long-term, renewable lease, and later for the most part already in perpetuity, after payment (as a lump sum or in instalments) of the so-called okupne (*grundgeld*), which gave them the possibility of selling the entire farm together with its buildings,
