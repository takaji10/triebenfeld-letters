It may be an interesting fact that in the last of the settlements located by the heirs of Trąbczyn there was also a group of Polish peasants, Catholics. In the years 1746–1789 the heirs of Łukom create successive settlements – these are today's villages: Bukowe, Imielno, Huta Łukomska (called at that time Zagórowskie Olędry), Myszakówek.

The name Olędrzy, used for a long time in the Zagórów land, was not connected with the nationality of the settlers, but with the law on the basis of which the villages were located and relations with the landowners regulated. This law was brought into the Commonwealth by Mennonites arriving from the Netherlands, who were fleeing persecution in their own homeland. The Warsaw Confederation adopted by the Polish Sejm in January 1573 was, for its times, an exceptional act of religious tolerance, which guaranteed the nobility freedom of worship and ensured peace between the dissenters. The Mennonites were not a numerous group that reached the Vistula and the Żuławy, but the law proved very useful in organizing the settlement campaign and was applied for a long time – until the mid-nineteenth century. Zbigniew Chodyła, a meritorious researcher and expert on Hauländer settlement, estimates that Holendrzy and their descendants constituted barely less than one percent among the settlers located on Hauländer law. The others were, in the great majority, Germans – Evangelicals, and also Poles – Catholics and Protestants. "As a result of the second partition of Poland (the year 1793), Zagórów found itself, together with the entire Słupca Land, under Prussian rule. A year later it was engulfed by the Kościuszko Uprising. In the vicinity of Zagórów, Ląd, Lądek, Słupca and Gniezno, the corps of General Jan Henryk Dąbrowski, supported by local insurgents, fought bloody battles with the Prussians. The fall of the Commonwealth after the defeat at Maciejowice restored Prussian rule in the little town. Zagórów, Słupca and the adjacent areas entered the so-called South Prussia"[^2].
