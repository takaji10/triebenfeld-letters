 of a division of the Church into a German part, linked with one of the Lutheran Churches in the German Reich, and a Polish part with its authorities in Warsaw. General Superintendent Juliusz Bursche – the head of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church, whose supreme authority was in Warsaw – took the side of the newly created Polish state. This was connected with the striving to liberate evangelicalism from the isolation conditioned by the Counter-Reformation and the German-speaking immigration, through an opening of the Church toward Polish culture and society. This, however, meant violating the existing stereotypes and the immutable boundaries of the relations between nation and confession. From the end of the nineteenth century, both in Poland divided by the partitions and in Bismarckian-Wilhelmine Germany, the nation was traditionally identified with religion, which produced a way of thinking about Polishness – Catholicism, and Germanness – evangelicalism. Other options aroused suspicions as to their national authenticity and required proofs of loyalty. Bishop Bursche, presenting an evangelical model of the Polish cause, balanced between dubious alliances with the Polish national-nationalist forces, thereby giving sustenance to the Germans, who considered him a traitor to German cultural dominance, from which the fruits of centuries of activity in the east had been taken away. On the other hand, Bishop Bursche's vision ran up against the image, common to the political camps, of an idealized reborn Poland – united linguistically, culturally and denominationally, and therefore able to exist only as a Polish national Catholic state. This meant that the national and religious minorities could at most count on limited rights, until they were assimilated, while the Roman Catholic confession acquired the rank of a state religion. Bishop Bursche advocated a model of relations on the nation–confession line that would accept diversity – that is, a true coexistence of confessions and nationalities. The tragedy of Bishop Bursche lay in the fact that, in his strivings to secure a future for evangelicalism within Polish society, he was forced to cooperate with political forces that were prepared to grant the minorities at most limited rights.
