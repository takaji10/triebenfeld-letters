These mutual good relations helped us overcome some of the difficulties in the last months of the war. We were able to keep with them, among other things, clothes or food, because at that time there was always the danger that our house, as the house of Germans, would be plundered. Our whole cultural life, apart from school, centred on the church.

Almost every Sunday morning we went to the service at the House of Prayer, which stood next to our school. The sermon was usually given by the teacher, and during the war also by my uncle Rytthammer.

Grandfather devoted one hour every Sunday afternoon to reading the Bible. He knew the Bible by heart. It was important that my parents and their forebears preserved the German language and their faith. And so for a long time the Bible, the hymn book and the prayer book served as the textbook for their children, before German teachers began to teach them and the later generations in German schools. The proper holidays were in summer. They lasted from mid-June to the beginning of September, so about ten weeks. Even if many did not travel anywhere then, it was still a wonderful time for us. There was no radio or television, and we made our toys ourselves. We only had to set our imagination going. Even if we had to help our parents in the house, in the field or in the meadow, we still had time for games and pranks. During the harvest we played hide-and-seek in the stacks. When the grain had ripened, the men cut it with scythes, and the women bound it into sheaves. In the evening the sheaves were set up in stacks. In this way the straw, the ears and the grain were protected from dew or rain. The wind blowing through the empty spaces in the stacks hastened the drying of the sheaves. Winter played a special role for us. The winters were frosty and very long. I still remember exactly how, on 7 October – it was my mother's birthday – we were digging the last potatoes and sowing the winter grain when the first frost appeared, heralding the approaching winter.
