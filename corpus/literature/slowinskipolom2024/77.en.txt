# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 77
# translation by the editor's files

In the intra-church discussion, Bishop Bursche repeatedly returned to the principle adopted at the general synod of pastors as early as 1906: *suum cuique*. Adopted unanimously, it concerned the various nationalities and language groups in the Evangelical-Augsburg Church. It read: The synod (of pastors) takes its stand on *suum cuique*: the Jew for the Jew, the Greek for the Greek. The pastor should stand above the nationalities; he is not a politician, but a pastor. His task is neither Germanization nor Polonization, but the faithful proclamation of the Gospel, whereby, as far as possible, he must prove just toward every nationality. However, during the occupation new political constellations arose, changing the coexistence of the Polish- and German-speaking population and making the application of this principle difficult. Clergy and laypeople who came under German-national influence placed the question of maintaining Germanness above the goals of the Church. On the other hand, the conception of a missionary Lutheranism, open to the Polish surroundings, promoted by Bishop Bursche, with the end of the war became a programmatic political option in favor of the new state – arousing distrust from the Polish as well as the German nationalist forces.

The maintenance of the Church's unity with strict observance of the principle of supranationality collided with the process of national awakening of the German-speaking members of the Church, which intensified in proportion to the growing tensions in German-Polish relations and the sharpening internal political situation in Poland. Bishop Juliusz Bursche said: "Bilingual is our Church, the Evangelical-Augsburg, in our country. There are German evangelicals, and there is also a considerable number of evangelicals of Polish speech. For above all, quite a few authentically Polish families sacredly preserve the faith handed down to them from their fathers and grandfathers from the times of the Reformation, but also among us, who bear German surnames, there are many who have grown into the local society." And how could it be otherwise? Having dwelt here for several generations, we have ceased to be alien newcomers; we have grown into the country, which has become our homeland, for whose good we live and work.
