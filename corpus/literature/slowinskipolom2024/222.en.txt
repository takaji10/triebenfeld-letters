# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 222
# translation by the editor's files

I was friends with his son Abram when I went to school on Łazińsk. Abram was very thin and very tall. It was the only Jewish family in our village.

#### After the war

We became homeless. A Polish family moved back into the farmstead in Łazy. Mama and I went to our grandma Loisa on Łazińsk. From the farmstead in Łazy we were not allowed to take all our things and our animals. We could take with us what we could carry in our hands. There were seven of us then. Father was no longer with us, for in 1945 the Russians had taken him away for forced labour in Russia. The youngest sister was still a baby. Secretly, at night, we still carried over some small things. Everything else stayed behind. At Grandma's there was not much either. In winter it was sometimes so cold that our bread froze. In Łazy there were people who helped us as much as they could.

#### The Nowicki family

Then, right after the war, somehow one had to live… We depended on what other people were willing to give us. We had no rights at all. Everyone looked out for themselves, for how they would survive. As children we had to look for people who would help us. Like our mother, up to her illness and death in 1949. There was a family called Nowicki, farmers from Łazy. They were very good people. When we were thrown out of our house, our farmstead, they kept me for two years. They had five children of their own, but they took me in as well. I lived and worked with them. I was thirteen. I worked for lodgings and food. I also received bread, milk, potatoes, so that my siblings and Mama would have something to eat… It is hard for me to talk about it… If you had something to eat, you had it, but if not, you were condemned to begging or hunger. To this day I remember the smell and the taste of dry, smoked salt bacon. Once I got such a piece straight from the chimney from Mr Nowicki. My siblings went to work for farmers in Szetlew.
