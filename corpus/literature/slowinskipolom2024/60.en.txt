After the expiry of the period free of dues (usually lasting from six to seven years), intended for developing the holding, the settlers owed the lord only rents (in money and in kind) and labour services to a very limited extent. They possessed a limited freedom of land tenure, answered solidarily, as an entire commune, for the punctual fulfilment of their obligations towards the lord, and had an elected village self-government (with a village headman (sołtys) confirmed by the manor), which among other things supervised the fulfilment of these obligations, and they retained a limited judicial freedom, being subject to their own village court and only partially (in criminal matters and in appeal proceedings) to dominial jurisdiction, patrimonial in the royal and church domains. They also paid dues to the Catholic Church (until 1768 this included Protestants) and taxes to the state, as a rule possessed freedom of trade, and enjoyed freedom of religion (until 1768 partly restricted where public worship was concerned) and the right to establish their own schools, most often serving at the same time as houses of prayer. These rules of Dutch law were applied by the Dutch mainly on flood-prone and waterlogged areas, and subsequently by their descendants and continuators, mainly Germans and Poles, not only on such terrain.
