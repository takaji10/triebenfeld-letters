In the localities where it was impossible to guarantee the German settlers steady pastoral care, it therefore became necessary to appoint a lay functionary who could replace a clergyman in certain duties. Such a person was precisely the cantor, obliged to hold services in the house of prayer every Sunday and feast day, to sing hymns, to read out the letters and to read the Bible. The lay functionary could also perform weddings and funerals, but he had no ability to administer baptisms, and he was not allowed to deliver any speeches while administering the sacraments[^32]. His duties also included work as a teacher, that is, teaching children writing, reading, arithmetic and – above all – religion. For an important obligation of the cantor was the preparation of the farmers' children for confirmation[^33].

#### Work neither easy nor lucrative

Initially cantors did not have to have pedagogical preparation. Often it sufficed merely that a candidate for this office could read and write. The cantor was chosen by the pastor or the parish college, paying for his services from parish funds. These were all too often modest, which explains the commonplace absence of a professional teacher in the settlements. In that period (just as earlier in Germany) functionaries for the post of cantor were recruited from among educated farmers, former soldiers or craftsmen. It happened that a weaver, a shoemaker or an innkeeper became a teacher.
