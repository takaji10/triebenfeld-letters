Until 1918, 45 pastors from Germany conducted pastoral work in the Evangelical-Augsburg Church. This caused shifts in the internal balance of forces of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church in favor of the pro-German orientation. Nor did the draft of the new church order leave any illusions as to the direction in which the church reform planned by the pro-German camp was heading, together with the conception of separating the Polish and German parts of the Church and attaching the German part to one of the Lutheran Churches in the Reich. With the Wielkopolska Uprising, the creation of the People's Council in Poznań and the transfer of power in Warsaw to Józef Piłsudski, the German occupation came to an end. Together with the German troops, clergy of Reich origin and members of the church boards also left the country in part. Yet, as Governor Bruno Geissler wrote after returning to Germany: the activity of the German clergy from the Reich in occupied Congress Poland did not remain without national-political consequences. The emergence of a new, independent Polish state in 1918 required of the evangelical Churches – previously located on the territories of the three partitions – a regulation of their relationship to the state and to the emerging legal order, and an adaptation of church life to the new territorial and social conditions. This process was hindered by the varied confessional, linguistic and national traditions of the individual Churches – and it must be remembered that in the interwar period seven evangelical Churches functioned on the territories of the Second Polish Republic. The situation was further complicated by the fact that some of the evangelical Churches had their supreme authorities in Berlin or in Vienna. The political forces in the German Reich constantly questioned the existence of the new Poland – remaining in the hitherto legal union therefore meant for the Churches a questioning of Polish sovereignty.
