# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 10
# translation by the editor's files

 situation. As early as the year 1808 (that is, in the times of the short life of the Duchy of Warsaw), 28 Jews permanently settled in Zagórów were recorded. Both the Ashkenazi Jews arriving in Zagórów and the settlers from Silesia, Prussia and Pomerania lived for long years in the world of their own language (Yiddish and German) as well as of religion. These were communities with their own self-government, deep autonomy and religious separateness. That was so above all in the times of the first Commonwealth. The partitioning powers regulated the social order according to their own jurisdiction – yet the cultural distinctness and the special legal status remained.


Until the mid-nineteenth century the contacts of Poles, Jews and Germans had an almost exclusively economic, commercial character. Also in later years one cannot really record (apart from incidental cases) mixed marriages: Polish-Jewish, Polish-German or Jewish-German. There are also no cases of conversion. Russian rule lasted more than a hundred years, but in none of the communities – Polish, Jewish or German – are there testimonies of conversion to the faith of the rulers – Orthodoxy. In the region, apart from officials, there are small groups of Rusins, and the nearest cathedral church (later Orthodox church) is in Kalisz. The first Jewish settlers were engaged in the production and trade of linen, in tailoring, hat-making, and later they in fact came to dominate commerce. Poles and Germans worked on the land and ran numerous craft workshops. It is worth mentioning an important communication undertaking. From the eighteen-twenties of the nineteenth century, along the main (Poznań) highway, a mail coach regularly travelled twice a week on the route Warsaw – Poznań – Berlin. It carried letters and parcels, but also passengers. It opened a window onto the world for the local people. But the possibility of contacts and of learning what others were doing was also created by another circumstance. After the defeat of the January Uprising, the Liquidation Commission for the Kalisz governorate, appointed in March 1864, singled out Zagórów, among 32 other towns, to be deprived of its town rights. On the basis of the act of 11 February 1869 this fact took place. It was never reconciled with this act, and successive boards managed for over 40 years to maintain the urban character of the proud town.
