The next day I drove to Skulsk, where after the war Mrs Gertruda and her husband Reinhard Schwemmer had bought a house. On a foundation of blue and red granite boulders, roofed with pantiles, several dozen metres from the gate, deep in an almost magical garden, rises a house of red brick. Mrs Gertruda was waiting for me behind the house, seated at a white table on an equally white garden chair. The red of the brick, the green of the trees and lawn, the magic of the garden — all of this made her seem even more ethereal than she was in reality. I sat down on the other side of the table and we began to talk about the past, about the history of the Beutler family.

Who was Mrs Gertruda Schwemmer of the Beutlers? Born in independent Poland, she came of age in the times of the Second Republic, married during the Second World War, already in the Warthegau, and bore her first children in that period as well, the later ones already in the People's Republic, in which it fell to her to raise and educate all of them. She spent the last twenty-five years of her life in Berlin, to which she moved on account of her age, to be closer to her daughters, who surrounded their 'Mutti' with solicitous care. A European, a Pole, a German, but above all a Hauländer — for that would probably be the most fitting description of the entire social group from which the Beutler family grew, a group that in the present day, as before the Second World War, was diverse, both in origin and in views and political opinions, as well as in identification with a particular nationality.

From Gertruda Schwemmer née Beutler, as she was leaving this world, back to her first ancestor known by name on Polish lands, there stretched 240 years. Andreas Beutler — for that was the name of the first Beutler, who began the family's history in Poland — was born in 1778 and was one of the many settlers whom Polish kings, church hierarchs and the Polish nobility had earlier invited.
