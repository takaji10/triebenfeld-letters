# Kneifel (1964), p. 45
# translation by Claude

1731 it was said: "It shall be permitted them for all time to build and maintain a school, and for this school two Morgen of land are given them, so that from it they may maintain a schoolmaster." Further schools arose: in 1765 in Witoszyn-Lengden, in 1770 in Bögpomöz, and in 1780 in Rybitwy.

In the Dobrzyner Land German schools organized themselves: in 1710 in Michalki near Rypin, in 1730 in Klosno, in 1745 in Tomaszewo, in 1747 in the Wolschen Buden; then in 1765 in Gaj, Iwane, Kierz, Kotowo, Kleszczyn, Klein-Kretki, Seedorf, and Zduny. Besides, in 1769 in Bocheniec, in 1774 in Ryszewek and Bialowiezyn, and in 1776 in Witkowo.

In Kujawien about 25 German schools were in existence up to the takeover of the land by Prussia, whereas in the Warthebruch their number came to ten. In the Kalisch land German schools formed: in 1740 in Wladyslawow-Rosterschütz, then in Prazuchy, Zbiersk, Danowiec, Zamenty, Zakrzyn, Przespolew, Czachulec, Grodziec, Mycielin, Obory, Bialobloty, Gadowskie Hol., Orlina, Wierzby, Lazinsk. In all, 16 schools were counted in the Kalisch land and about ten in the Gostynin district. Furthermore, schools took shape in the Lodz area, among others: in Dombrowa Hol., Mileszki, Gross-Bruzyca, Klein-Bruzyca, Radogoszcz, Kaly, Slowik, Swendow, Glogowiec, Domaradzyn, Paprotnia Hol. Together with the schools in the towns there existed here, until 1793/95, i. e. until the incorporation of central Poland into the Prussian state, approximately 125 German Lutheran schools.

With this base, so to speak, the foundation was laid for our school system. While the Polish Catholic peasant at that time stood in a relation of serfdom to his lord of the manor and culturally on a low level, the German Lutheran colonist was a free man and, thanks to his schooling and his more advanced methods of husbandry, far superior to him. Only in the towns, and by no means sufficiently, did the Polish population have schools.

In the German village schools, generally called cantorate schools (religion schools), the teachers — almost down into the first decades of the 19th century — taught only during the winter half of the year, i. e. from late autumn to spring. But even then the instruction as well as school attendance left much to be desired. The subjects of instruction — religion, German (writing and reading), and arithmetic — were exhausted in imparting what was most necessary and for the most part did not go beyond the rudiments of a very modest store of knowledge. Naturally there were also among the teachers such as, despite their low level of education, filled the gaps in their knowledge and honestly strove to raise their schools in every respect and to give the children not only knowledge but also to form and strengthen their character. In instruction the greatest emphasis was laid precisely on religion (Bible, catechism, hymnal). Naturally every school session began and ended with song and prayer.

Apart from their service as teachers, the "school-holders" were also cantors, i. e. readers in the divine service and rural representatives of the pastors. On Sundays and feast days they held reading services, performed acts of office (baptisms and burials), and represented the pastor also otherwise, for example in confirmation instruction. Under the cantor stood the religious care often of several localities, which were joined together into the so-called cantorate. According to its size, a rural parish consisted of a number of cantorates. These lay on average five to twenty, and even more, kilometers from the parish seat. Because of the great distances, especially given the bad conditions of the roads, the pastors visited them only two or three times a year. In the last century, more regularly and more frequently.
