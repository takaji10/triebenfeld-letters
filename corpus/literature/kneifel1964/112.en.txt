### 3. Schools and Cantorates

School affairs in the country were directed and supervised, in place of the Chamber of Education, by the Commission for Popular Enlightenment in the Kingdom of Poland. The school ordinance of the year 1808 and the new school law issued in 1834 obligated Roman Catholic and also evangelical pastors and estate owners to supervise the schools in the communities and to report on them. The school law of 1840, drafted by the Russian minister Uwarow and confirmed by tsar Nicholas I, was unfavorable to the Lutherans insofar as, through its unclear provisions, it opened the door wide to the interference of Polish-Catholic authorities in the German-evangelical school system. Thus, among other things, the curator of the Warsaw school district, in the rescript of 23 February 1843 to the General Consistory, took a position against the sending of Roman Catholic books and writings to evangelical schools. Likewise the school curator declared in the decree of 28 August 1847 that the viceroy had ordered, for the sake of fostering a religious spirit and preserving the German language, not only the separation of the German-evangelical schools from the Polish-Catholic ones, but also the founding of new schools in the German-Lutheran congregations. By this order the confessionally and nationally mixed schools were for the most part separated according to the language and confessional principle. The Polish-Catholic authorities attempted to obstruct the implementation of the order by making difficulties in the conversion of the simultaneous schools or in the founding of new evangelical schools. After 1830 the endeavor of the government was directed toward converting the cantorate schools into elementary schools wherever possible. It thereby wanted to raise their level. For in many places they were in a lamentable state. After 1840 there were in the Vistula lowlands, besides numerous cantorate schools, the following elementary schools: in Bögpomöz, Osieker Lengden, Witoszyn, Rybitwy, Wolfswinkel, Lentzen, Modzerowo, Groß-Domb, Troszyn, Borki, Sady, Wymysle, Wionczemin, Kladow. The elementary schools were also to be accessible to Polish children. But this intention, both here and elsewhere, was thwarted at Russian intervention. In the Gostynin land the number of German schools in the fourth decade of the 19th century totaled 22. In the Kalisch land the number was considerably higher. In the congregation of Stawiszyn alone there were eight schools, in that of Wladyslawow seven, Grodziec about ten, in the district of Zagörow seven, Turek six, Prazuchy ten. With the three schools in the parish of Kalisch, the Kalisch land counted around 55 evangelical schools. Individual ones of them, such as, for example, Orlina Wielka, Lazinsk, Obory, were endowed with 15, 18 and 22 morgen of land respectively.

Among the teachers of the Kalisch land Karl Kittner, born in Schönwald/Silesia, distinguished himself. For over 50 years, from 1813 to 1869, he labored at the school in Wladyslawow. "In the memory of the parishioners teacher and cantor Kittner lives on indeed as a strict, but also as a hard-working educator." In the Lodz area, likewise, numerous new schools were called into being.
