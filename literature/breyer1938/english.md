## I. Landscape

From the former border town of Peisern to below the archbishop's little town of Uniejow — a stretch of 130 km — some hundred German villages line the valley of the Warthe, some of them on the very edge of its banks. The Berlin-Warsaw glacial valley, at times up to 5 km wide, in which the Warthe rolls its waters sluggishly along its winding course, is varied in its scenery. By the towns of Zagórow and Konin lush green cattle pastures spread out, broken here and there by gleaming sand hills. In general, however, the river meadows form a half-open landscape in which picturesque clumps of alder, willow and poplar prevail, and in places wind-torn pines.

Into the tranquil quiet of the marsh landscape comes no noise of raftsmen, no pounding of steamers. Silently the mirroring waters glide by. Gulls and lapwings now and then bring some life into the dreamy seclusion of these river meadows. It also happens that here and there fishermen glide downstream in a rocking boat, laying out their nets. Mostly hidden in the thickets of alder and willow, as cattle breeders and farmers, the German people of this region lead their quiet yet busy life far from the bustle of the world, but faithful to the ways and manner of life of their fathers and bound to their traditions.

A hundred and eighty years ago the marsh was an impassable region, truly "broken" ground. Between primeval undergrowth and huge oaks and maples lay, by turns, still backwaters thick with marsh and water plants, which in places were beginning to break up into smaller and larger, eerily overgrown pools. Swamp and mire everywhere fought for mastery, where today profitable peat cuttings and lush meadowland spread out, but where even today there are enough "mouldering meadows" ("Moderwiesen"). Then as now, at the spring thaw, turbid floods poured over the glacial valley and made every unevenness of the ground disappear. An all-embracing, boundless realm of water formed, out of which the bare crowns of the huge marsh trees broke helplessly. Gradually the turbid masses of water fell, leaving behind a rich silt, the fertile "Letten", as the present German inhabitant, the "Brücher" (marsh-dweller), or in mockery the "Wasserpatscher" (water-splasher), calls it. For the meadows the spring flood is a welcome manuring. If the spring water is low, the marsh farmer is dissatisfied: his meadows and fields lack the strength to grow in summer. It is the raging floods, too, that are to blame for the large

[p. 2]

sand hills and the wide sand fields, and that now bury valuable farmland under sand for many years. For months and years the evil floods give the marsh farmer work of clearing. Cartload after cartload of drift sand is carried off to the swampy spots of the meadows, so that new bread-land may arise. Tough and untiring, the German goes about this honourable work of cultivation. In proud awareness he treads here in the footsteps of his forefathers, who often, in far harder conditions, set foot in this swamp wilderness with courage and resolve to fight.

> *"They knew but toil and meagre bread,*
> *Mire and wasteland breathed fever's death,*
> *The foul waters welling at the spade*
> *Their will forced sudden into ditch and drain,*
> *And ever deeper into the wild drove*
> *The hoe; slowly the sown field rose..."*
> *(Maria Kahle)*

These proud memories the sight of the neat villages of the Warthe marshes calls up in the ethnic German (volksdeutsch) wanderer. And confident hopes that the strength of the people will last fill him as he comes to know better the splendid German stock of this still, dreamy Warthe landscape.

## II. The German Settlements

Like links of a long chain the German villages of the Warthe marshes follow one another. Only by the towns of Konin, Koło and Uniejów are there, strangely, gaps in the settlement, which might be explained by early settlement near the towns. In four large language islands, the *Wenglew*, *Paprot*, *Politz* and *Leśnik* islands, live some 10,000 Germans, all of them members of the Evangelical Church of the Augsburg Confession, belonging to the parishes of Zagórow, Konin, Koło, Władysławow-Rosterschütz and Turek. Living conditions in the German marsh villages may be called satisfactory, it being noted that the villages around Zagórow and Konin are more prosperous than the others. This is caused by the quality of the soil in each place. The Wenglew and in part the Paprot language island have in some places fertile silt soil, here simply called "Lett", on which wheat and clover can be grown. There are also meadows with a good growth of grass. Certainly the fields have their fair share of waste land and sand, or of mouldering, sour meadows, since the quality of the soil is spread like a mosaic over the marsh. The other marsh villages, further up the Warthe, mostly have very sandy soil, which is especially striking in the settlements on the edge of the valley. In the Leśnik language island most meadows bear only a poor growth because their soil is shallow. In dry years this shows strikingly. Everywhere *cattle breeding* and *dairy farming* stand first in farming, and in the lower part of the Warthe marshes *goose breeding*. The cattle graze here in "Koppeln", meadows fenced with willow hurdles. Some holdings consist mostly of meadow, which gives the owner a good income. German farmers from the neighbouring villages of the upland rent sizeable meadows in the marsh every year. The "Brücher" also brings hay to the town market. In some marsh villages *bee-keeping* is practised too, and here we find without exception "bee skeps" woven of straw, a special form that only German bee-keepers prefer. Among the most widespread side occupations, which for poorer farmers even becomes the main one, is *basket-weaving*. The basket rods, called "Wieten", grow abundantly on the sandy river islands and on the edges of the "Altwasser" (backwaters) and "Laken" (pools). With the products of basket-weaving, white wickerwork and green-woven baskets, the markets of the marsh towns are

[p. 4]

supplied; indeed, large wagonloads are sent even to Łódź and Kalisz. In some German villages live *professional fishermen*, who fish the Warthe with all sorts of gear and sell their catch in the small towns and the cities. That the "Brücher" are skilful boatmen and boat-builders goes without saying. Most of them, especially the less well-off, "went on the barge" in their young years or took up "Flötterei", that is, timber rafting, travelling long stretches of river, as far as Küstrin and Stettin, and indeed by the Oder to Breslau. Up to the World War the timber trade and the timber industry flourished in the Warthe region. Many found paying work sawing boards and cutting stave wood, just as since the beginnings of the immigration many German beam-hewers and "wood-cutters" were to be found in the forests of the Warthe meadows. In the church books we find signs that around 1830 German boat-builders lived in the Warthe village of Piersk, building Berlin barges and similar craft.

[Picture: Former German school house in Drożynek-Holl., Konin county. It now serves as a prayer house.]

The yield of grain and potatoes is mostly enough for the household's own use. The rich stands of alder and pollard willow give the marsh farmer the fuel he needs, the more so as peat is also cut in many places. In building houses, wood used to prevail, and in places bog iron ore, the "ironstone". Today most new buildings are put up of fired or sand bricks. For the woodwork of houses and barns the abundant poplar is used. Characteristic of the marsh dwellers are the wagon baskets woven of willow rods,

[p. 5]

called "Wasung". The well-kept horses are likewise the marsh farmer's pride. Working the loamy soil needs a strong team, and for the farmer himself ploughing is hard work that costs much sweat. So it is said in mockery that a "Brücher" ploughing on the sandy fields of the upland can be known by the loud cursing and swearing with which he means to urge his horses on. Orchards are found only in the rarest cases. The only exception are the sour-cherry gardens, which thrive on the light, shallow soil. In recent years the local authorities have set about regulating the Warthe. In places dikes have been built which keep the neighbouring villages from flooding, so that the "drowning" of the grain fields can now be counted as remedied. On the other hand, some of the "Brücher" are not quite content with the new state of things. The yearly floods, by leaving silt and mud, gave the fields, and the meadows in particular, an abundant manuring.

The most widespread form of settlement in the Warthe marshes is the *scattered settlement*, in a particularly marked form of its own. Every new settler sought to build his farmstead on the edge of a meadow, from where he could most easily keep watch over the grazing cattle, the "Geziefer". Since on any given area of settlement the pieces of meadow lay jumbled together without order, the holdings had of necessity to stand on the edges of the meadows. In the marsh the edges of the meadows mostly run in a winding line; so a kind of zigzag came about, along which the single holdings were strung. In the marsh villages a village street, mostly straight, serves as the common road, and an access way leads to it from each farmstead. This manner of settlement, which takes account of the natural conditions of the marsh, might be called the *marsh-edge village* (Bruchranddorf), since it is found only in the swampy ground of the Warthe marshes and in part in the Kalisz country. Besides this predominant form of village we also come upon peculiar line villages lying along the boundary between the river valley and the valley slope. Here the farmsteads turn the house side toward the meadows and pastures, while the barns stretch along the village street. Finally it should be pointed out that, except for the villages of the Wenglew language island and in part of the Paprot language island, the settlements lie out of danger of flooding. So there is no need here to build the houses on

[p. 6]

artificially raised mounds (Wurten). The German village of Neu-Czarkow alone is an exception. It happens that in a very high flood the whole field area is covered by the waters, or as the marsh farmer says, "by the bare water", and traffic can be kept up only by boat. The members of the cantorate then come to the service by boat; funerals likewise go by water. All this vividly recalls the roads of the Spreewald. In some respects economic life in the Warthe marshes resembles that of the Vistula lowland. On the whole, however, the Warthe marshes are a landscape rich in peculiarities of their own, which stands out markedly from the area of settlement of central Poland. The "Brücher" are loath to marry up to the "Höhe" (the height), the Silesian or Pomeranian villages on the upland. They cling with all their heart to their green home. It should be stressed that the marsh dwellers of Brandenburg (Mark) stock in particular are distinguished by a sober and straightforward mind. Here we have never found sects, which cannot be said of the Pomeranians in the Leśnik language island. The Pomeranian farmer is more open to religious enthusiasm.

## III. The History of Settlement

### Early Times and the Middle Ages

The valley of the Warthe has always by its nature been hostile to settlement. Even today the Warthe marshes deter a settler "unskilled in water works" from settling there. The floods at the spring thaw, and sometimes at St John's (24 June), do their damage year in, year out, and rob the marsh farmer of his harvest.

In the grey past the river was an important route of communication for the neighbouring regions. The river kept this importance, of course, only in the formerly Prussian territory. In the former Russian partition the wild, untamed river is still, as before, closed to shipping. The fisherman and hunter has since time immemorial pushed into the swampy thicket and found his living there after a fashion. On steep banks he would put up his hut if need be; he also liked to take possession of single islands of higher ground (Werder; ostrowy) rising above the swamp, from which he could go about his hunting. Such dwelling places might be the islands of Spicimierz, Koło, Ochle, Swięte, Ostrów near Sławsk and Ratyń. That these "Kämpen" may have been well settled in prehistoric times appears from the many finds of urn sherds that one comes upon everywhere on these sand hills. In 1855, not far from the village of Powiercie south of Koło, two statues of heathen idols were found in the bed of the Warthe[^1]. The names of some islands, such as Swięte, Swięciec, and likewise Modlica and Modla, also speak of heathen places of worship. The place names Grójec and Grodzisko point to refuge forts.

In the following centuries the fertile banks of the valley may have been chosen for villages because of the good meadows beside them. This is true especially of the Warthe villages on the right bank.

Fishing held its place then, as now, as an occupation of its own, as may be seen from the grant of the fishing village of Rybaki west of

Konin to the Cistercian monastery at Lond in 1145.[^2] The charters of that time speak of fishermen further, as in the villages of Dobrow and Czarkow, where they say "cum piscatoribus" (with the fishermen)[^3]. In 1232 a charter of the village of Dobrow noted that fishing and the taking of beaver in the rivers Warthe and Ner were released to the inhabitants ("piscatio et castores in fluviis Wartha et Ner"[^4]). Moreover, the riverside villages were obliged to the monastery at Lond "to draw the net in fishing on the Warthe". In the same charter we also read of the wealth of grass of the Warthe marshes; at one place it says: "the rent-paying villages are obliged, at the time of the hay harvest, to bring three cartloads of hay each into the monastery's barns".[^5]

In the 12th and 13th centuries Polish provincial princes began to give single Warthe villages to church dignitaries. Thus around 1136 the Archbishop of Gniezno owned the villages of Chełmno, Uniejów, Kwiatków, Dombie and Jankowice, to which Rzuchów, Leszno, Grabina and Szadow were added in 1284. Towards the end of the 13th century the archbishop acquired, in the land between the Warthe and the Ner, the villages of Cechmiany, Grodziska, Orzeszków, Ostrowsko and Zbylczyce. The archbishop's property grew considerably south of the town of Uniejów, where the following villages were acquired or founded: Spicimierz, Wiejszcze, Kościelnica, Zielenin, Ubysław, Lubiszewice, Niemysłów, Boleszczyn, Smólsko and Popów. Downstream he acquired the villages of Skobielice and Trzęśniew, which bordered on his property. All the archbishop's villages together made up a wide property stretching a good 50 km along the course of the Warthe.

The Bishop of Poznań received from the ruling prince the village of Ciążeń in 1251 and Ratyń in 1261. West of the town of Koło lay the only property of the ruling prince, the village of Gozdów, since in 1362 the prince's villages of Bliżna and Nagórna Wieś were incorporated into the newly founded town of Koło. Among the archbishop's possessions a few noble villages are scattered, such as Balin, Białków, Powiercie, Grójec, Wola Podłężna, Daniszew and others.

The floor of the valley was wholly unsettled in the Middle Ages, except for the island of Ochle, inhabited around 1300, and the raised strip of land near Krzymow, taken into possession some decades later.[^6] The swamp forest of that time had no attraction for people ready to settle; the spring floods deterred them.

### The Cistercian Monastery of Lond

The Polish duke Mieszko the Old, whose mother was the German count's daughter Salome of Berg, visited the city of Cologne, his mother's home, around 1135. There he came to know the monks of the Cistercian order at Altenberg. The strict rules of the order, above all the careful tending of the fields, from whose yield the monks lived, made a good impression on the prince. He resolved to invite the brothers of the order to settle in his principality. As the place for the order's work he chose the district of the castellany of Lond, which stretched along the right bank of the Warthe.[^7] In 1145 the deed of gift for the monastery of Lond to be founded was drawn up. It listed 13 villages given to the monastery, which lay mostly on the edge of the Warthe valley or a little way from it. They were Dolany, Szetlewo, Rzgów, Chorzeń, Głowiew, Rybaki, Grabieniec, Sławsko and Kwiatkowo, which still exist today, and some villages that have vanished in the course of time, such as Mokre, Stara Łąka, Świniarowo and Wyramowo, which in our opinion could be the still existing Wilamowo. The monastery's property grew very considerably in the 13th century by purchase, gift and exchange, mostly in the near neighbourhood of its villages. In this way were added Radolin, Sługocin, Kopojno, Skokom, Oleśnica, Osiecza, Zagórów, Bronniki, Wrąbczyn, Jaroszyn, Kowalewo, Policko, Tury and others. Instructive for the history of settlement are also the service villages, partly taken over and partly laid out by the monastery itself for particular purposes, whose names point directly to a particular service. The monastery's possessions also grew by the gifts of single church dignitaries. At the end of the 12th century the Archbishop of Gniezno lived as a hermit in the village of Dobrow on the Warthe. He was the last descendant of the powerful noble family of the Poraj. His father's inheritance, the villages of Dobrow, Rzuchów and Leszno, he left to the monastery of Lond for all time.[^9]

Among the finest cultural deeds of the German monks were without doubt the many new villages they founded, the more so as they also called German colonists into the country. In 1250, as the charters show, the monastery, with the consent of Duke Bolesław, founded under German law the village of Wola Londska, also called Freidorf, and settled it with Germans and free Poles. The abbots did the same in founding the villages of Biskupice or Bischofsdorf, Kotlin or Kesselberg, Wola Koszutska, Kuchary Kościelne and others. In 1296 we find in the village of Kossut a German village headman (Schulz), Christian, who two years later sold his headman's holding and moved to Prussia.[^10] It should be said here that of the German monks' services to the cultivation of the land we know only scattered fragments. Deep research is needed here.

From what is offered here only in short extract, it is easy to see how many-sided and varied the blessed cultural work of the German monastery at Lond was over the centuries. The historian Hockenbeck describes this work of the brothers, exemplary in every respect, as follows:

> *"The monks, familiar with the tending of the field, knew how to make their estates as useful as possible; they turned wild, waste land into fruitful soil, they cleared the dense forest, drained the swamps, regulated the water in the lakes, made roads and bridges, planted orchards and vineyards, and so turned the wilderness into smiling fields. If one considers further that the monks were a model to their neighbours not only in industry and in the sensible tilling of the soil, but were distinguished just as much by sincere piety, modesty, strict self-control and self-sacrificing love of their neighbour, and by zealous pastoral care in their villages, it is no wonder that their contemporaries gave them undivided praise, and that Archbishop Henry of Gniezno compared the order to the morning star that had risen over Poland with new splendour."*[^12]

For barely four hundred years the monastery of Lond was in German hands. Then came an abrupt change. In 1511 the Polish Sejm resolved that the monasteries which could not show their bishops chartered privileges for admitting German monks only should admit Poles to the order as well as Germans. This resolution of the Sejm ended the hitherto wholly German character of the monastery of Lond. In 1539 the archbishop appointed as the first Polish abbot the monk J. Wysocki, who began to treat the German inmates of the monastery none too gently. The last German abbot, Heinrich, died in 1551. The monks, left without a leader, left Lond and

By a turn of fate, after a good two centuries, German colonists appeared again within sight of the monastery of Lond, to take up afresh the dormant work of cultivation of their fellow Germans. But of that in a later section. Looking back, let us now sum up the state of settlement of the Warthe marshes from the town of Peisern down the Warthe to south of Uniejów at the end of the Middle Ages. Around the middle of the 14th century single riverside villages followed one another almost without a gap on the right bank of the Warthe from Peisern up to Dombie. Only the 18 km strip of bank from Kramsk to Groß-Osiek was unsettled, because it was sandy. We find similar gaps on the left bank too, with the same causes. This is so south of Peisern and east of Konin. The land between the Warthe and the Ner, and its edges too, were already fairly densely inhabited in the Middle Ages. The soils here are middling, but there are ample meadows, which must have been of particularly great importance for the cattle farming then practised; moreover, the land between the rivers was free of floods. South of the town of Uniejów the Warthe villages stand with strikingly large gaps between them, which again is to be put down to the flat valley slopes covered with river sand; the river meadows here are also shallow and so yield little. The densely settled banks between Peisern and Uniejów owe this to the fertile loam soils of the land beside them; here farming paid well. The forest behind the riverside villages was only sparsely settled at the end of the Middle Ages. The soil hardly tempted anyone to clear the forest; moreover, the lack of roads and the wildness of the immense primeval forests deterred even the boldest clearer. Yet the population, rising over the decades and centuries, sought new means of living. New settlements slowly pushed in between the old riverside villages; some dared to lay out their farmsteads in the marsh itself, on raised spots. It is noteworthy that it was the marsh south of Koło that fell to this heavier settlement. Into the marsh between Koło and Konin the settler ventured only around the middle of the 18th century, and likewise into the marsh west of Konin. Here as there it was German marsh farmers who opened up this swamp wilderness.

The conditions of settlement in the Middle Ages are also partly reflected in the old place names. In the village of Bobrowo the duke's beaver hunters may have lived. In Barcie were the bee-keepers, a calling held in honour in the Middle Ages. For the worship of the Catholic church vineyards were laid out on the high north bank near Podgórz, because it lay in the sun; hence the place name Winnica. Names such as Podbiel

[p. 13]

and Bielczew, the later Bilczew, point to a swampy position. Likewise the raised places in the flood area are marked several times by the name "Ostrowy" (islands, Werder) or "Grądy". The meadows visited by floods the marsh dwellers here call łęgi, in German Lengden, as in other river regions. So in the Warthe marshes south of Koło we find Groß-Łęg, Lęg Radczyński, Kościelski, Baliński, Dominikowski, Piekarski and Popowski. East of Konin lies Wola Podłężna. Purely topographical notions are given by place names such as Brod (ford), Białobrzegi, Piaseczno, Podgórz, Wysokie, Chełmno, Barłogi, Czyste, Kozubów and Brzeźno. Service villages were Skotniki, in the land between the Warthe and the Ner, which may have been known for its good meadows, and Rudniki (bog-ore smelters). The later Majdany points to potash burners, who plied their trade in the archbishop's wide forests. On the forest land they cleared, German clearing farmers settled afterwards.

### What did the Warthe marshes look like before the last German immigration?

Old German maps tell us: that of Quartermaster von Pfau of 1773 and that of the architect (Baurat) von Gilly of 1796. If we compare the state of settlement on these maps with that at the end of the 14th century, considerable progress is to be seen. By our count, in a period of 250 years some 36 riverside villages were added, in the sandy edge strips east of Konin, where lively settlement began after 1450,[^13] and on both sides south of Uniejów. In the marsh itself, east of Zagórów and Konin, small villages were laid out, in the typical scattered pattern. Some of these new settlements, feeling their way into the marsh, later vanished, such as Skrzynka, Kocianin, Osowce, Babia Góra and others. Settlement had grown quite considerably in the strip of marsh south of the town of Koło. On the edge of the banks 8 new villages arose here, and in the marsh twice that number. We should not leave unmentioned the iron hammers that appeared in the Warthe marshes at the beginning of the 18th century, called "Kuźnice" in Polish. These works indirectly favoured the rise of single marsh villages, since the great consumption of charcoal did much to fell the surrounding primeval forests and so made way for new villages. In the Warthe marshes at the end of the 18th century we come upon six iron hammers: at Mariantów near Zagórów, at Kużnica near Wysokie, at Tury and at Wakowy; south of Koło, near Koźmin and Januszew, there were two. That the iron won there was worked into swords on the spot is suggested by the neighbouring place name "Kordownia", since kord means sword in Polish. The iron hammers arose because the marsh was rich in bog iron ore. If we count all the new villages founded in the marsh up to the 18th century, we come to 34, which makes 70 new settlements in all, without the German foundations. In sum: the true marsh landscape of the valley floor was still wholly unopened around the middle of the 18th century. It needed the superior drainage skill of the German marsh farmer, who in the following decades brought it about that lush meadows and smiling grain fields began to spread where swamp and primeval thicket had once ruled without limit.

### The Second German Immigration into the Warthe Marshes

The first news attested in documents of German Holländereien (villages settled on Dutch-law terms) in the marsh dates from 1767. The owner of the estate of Sławsk, Leszczyc Zielonawski, handed over to the "honourable" headman (Schulz) Johann Keim a recently felled piece of forest, which he and some farmers were to clear and grub. For this clearing the settlers so drawn in received ten free years, after which they were bound to pay a set rent. Further, apart from their payments to the Catholic provost in Sławsk for his services, they were to deliver every year from each Hufe a quarter (Viertel) of rye and a [quarter, addition by J.D.] of oats. Beyond this the settlers were bound, after thirty years, to pay 100 Tinfe[^14] once for the renewal of the contract of settlement. The privilege concluded for the village called Slausker Holland was "roborated" (entered and confirmed) in the court books (Grodbücher) of Konin. At about the same time, or perhaps some years later, the neighbouring German marsh villages of the Holländereien *Wenglew*, *Rumin* and *Kolno* may have been founded. The Holländerei of *Ciążeń*, west of the town of Zagórów, and the *Altpeisernsche Holl.*, opposite Peisern, may be some years older than those near Konin. On the map of the Warthe marshes surveyed by Pfau in 1773 this village appears well built up.

After 1770 the German marsh farmer pushed into the empty swamp east of Konin. Our first news of this comes from the church books of the Lutheran parish of Władysławow-Rosterschütz, founded in 1776. That before 1773 there were no German marsh villages east of Konin is shown by Quartermaster von Pfau's map, which conscientiously marks the Holländereien that did exist then. We find the first entry from the Warthe marshes in 1777. The "owner" of the newly laid out village of *Borowo*, which arose in the marsh forests of the estate of Krzymowo, Hans Maass, aged 66, is first recorded in the burial book.

Around 1780 the village of Borowo had a sizeable German population, since at that time a court headman (Gerichtsschulze) and a schoolmaster held office there. The founding of Borowo and of other German settlements goes back to the then owner of the Kramsk estates, the judge of Łęczyca, I. Byszewski. He had his extensive marsh forests at Krzymow felled by German forest workers. German stave-cutters and plank-sawyers worked the usable timber for shipment by water to Stettin. In the church books of Rosterschütz we find many entries of stave and timber workers from the Krzymower Buden (the huts of Krzymow), as in general the German forest workers played an important part in the Warthe marshes up to the World War. Most of them came into the Krzymow forests from the forests around Boruj and Tomischel. It is characteristic that many of these wood-cutters settled in the forests they had felled. So a baptism note from the Krzymower Buden says, for instance: "Grüger Gottlieb, stave and plank worker and owner in the same place". Instructive for the history of settlement is also the gradual disappearance of the place name "Krzymower Buden", which was replaced by the doubtless better-sounding *Krzymower Holland*. In 1778 the new village of "Briesener Buden", the later *Briesener Holland*, appears for the first time in the church books of Rosterschütz. The "Briesner Buden", a hamlet of no importance on the highroad from Koło to Konin, arose on infertile soil after 1830 from the estate of Brzezno and is settled by Polish peasants. The rise of the village of Tury, or Turow as it was called at first in the books of souls, deserves notice. Around the middle of the 18th century there was an iron hammer in the old village of Tury. In 1779 its owner, the lord of the manor of Wyszyn, laid out the Holländer settlement of *Neu-Tury* near the old village. The stretches of forest felled to run the iron hammer gave the room for the new settlement. In 1780 the village name "Drunżyner Holl." appears for the first time in the notes of the pastor's acts. This is the present *Dronżynek*. The church-book entry is confirmed by our having found the year "1782" carved into the ceiling beam of an old house in that village. Of the neighbouring German settlement, *Alt-Paprotholland*, the first church-book news dates from 1782; ten years later the neighbouring Neu-Paprotholland arose. Close by the left bank of the Warthe, German marsh farmers laid out *Holland Drensen* around 1790. All the new villages named so far were marked above all by their wealth of meadows, which allowed a rich dairy farming.

[p. 18]

Downstream, German farmers settled south of Koło in *Leszcze*, in German Lesche, around 1783, as can be seen from the Rosterschütz books. In the marsh opposite Lesche, German settlers arrived around 1787 and laid out with success the typical marsh Holländerei of *Groß-Politz*. A school was founded here before 1800. The settlers in these German villages arising in old Polish times were mostly full-Hufe farmers (Vollhüfner). In terms of the law of property the first marsh settlements are to be called Holländer villages, since nowhere is there talk of an overriding importance of a village headman or of a special grant of land to him. The Pomeranian villages of the Leśnik language island, on the other hand, are to be regarded as headman's villages (Schulzendörfer).

The settling of German farmers by private lords set an example. On 4 September 1781 Ostrowski, Archbishop of Gniezno, had King Stanislaus Augustus confirm the privilege he had granted "to the honourable Hauländer newly establishing themselves and named in the contract, and to their heirs". The taking of the land took place in 1779, and *"the useless pieces of ground of the forests in the Chełmsk key, lying within the bounds of the village of Rzuchow, belonging to the archbishop's estates"* served for it; and as it goes on, *"since we have therefore found within the bounds of the village of Rzuchow such a forest from which one can never hope to have any use, we have*

[p. 19]

*left the same to the Holländer ... for clearing. They are however bound, during the free years, to clear the forests left to them entirely and to put up proper buildings."* Among other things, the founding charter of this Holländerei, called Rzuchow at first but Maidany from 1790, contains the right freely to sell the lands taken up, and also, which sounds strange for church property, complete freedom "from all dues and payments to the provost at Chełmsk". To the village headman and to the schoolmaster the archbishop gave half a Hufe of land each, "freed for ever from all rents and labour services". The charter names the following first settlers: Adam Kramer as village headman, [and as] his aldermen (Schöppen) Gottlob Klingbeil, Andreas Schindel, Michael Schindel, Christoph Ratay, Michael Ratay, Christian Ratay, Jacob Pinno, Jacob Schultz and Mathias Rautke. As entry money the new settlers bound themselves to pay 18 Gulden for each Hufe. The later yearly rent came to 60 Gulden for each Hufe. For the complete clearing of the pieces of forest left to them the Holländer received nine free years; in that time the farm buildings were also to be put up. According to a survey of the property made by the cathedral chapter of Gniezno in 1784, 11 full-Hufe and 2 half-Hufe farmers lived in the Holländerei of Maidany, owning 6 horses, 20 oxen and 30 cows.[^15]

The German villages of *Sobótka* and *Groß-Grabin* were settled on the archbishop's land under similar conditions. Sobótka arose in 1781; at first it was among the Holländereien richest in livestock, having 25 horses, 46 oxen and 51 cows, though we find in the village only 6 half-Hufe and 8 full-Hufe farmers. In the village of Groß-Grabin sat 7 full-Hufe and 13 half-Hufe farmers; this settlement was founded in 1779, at the same time as Maidany.

From the estate of Budzisław the marsh-edge village of *Budki Holland* was laid out in 1786. Around 1790 German settlers established themselves in the middle of the marsh of the estate of Koźmin and founded the new village of *Koźminer Buden*, the later Neu-Kwiatków. Around 1790 the lord of Brudzew had German farmers of Pomeranian stock settle beside the old villages of *Tarnowa* and *Neudorf*, who in this way built two new street villages of the same names. As appears from the papers of the Kalisz tribunal in the matter of the colonists of the estates of Piekary of 1851, the German villages of *Czyste*,

*Kaczka* and *Mechnówka* arose in 1792. The land given out was covered with swamp forest and brushwood. These were the first settlements of the Leśnik language island. In 1794 we come upon the village name *Myśliborzer Holland* for the first time in the church books of Rosterschütz.

If we sum up the German villages founded in old Polish times by private and church colonisation, the result is this. In the marsh there were 20 Holländereien and on the banks 20, a considerable number all the same. Some settlements, such as Lubin, Dzierzin, Sokołek, Lyszyce, Romonek, Tarnowo and Krzymowo, have since vanished from the marsh. They were mostly small scattered settlements.

We would not leave unmentioned the relatively numerous water millers of the Warthe marshes of that time. In the church book already mentioned we find in 1778 a German miller at Szadow, in 1779 at Powiercie, in 1781 at Posoka beyond Koło, and in 1783 at the Glinka mill near Konin. At Powiercie in 1781 there were a farrier and a farmer.

### The South Prussian Period

In the first years of the Prussian taking of the Warthe marshes, German farmers of Pomeranian stock settled on the Borzewiska estates of the landowner Milewski. The settlement charter, drawn up in German in 1796, gives us an insight into the terms of settlement. On taking up the land the colonists had to pay 189 Taler of entry money. The manor kept the right to sell drink for itself. The settlers received six free years. Of the nature of the "piece of forest of 10 Hufen [given] to the aforesaid colonists for clearing and building" Art. 5 of the contract gives a good idea; it says:

[p. 21]

> *"Considering that the land measured out to the colonists, and to be more closely defined, is much run over with water, the takers of the hereditary lease bind themselves to drain it by the proper digging of ditches and the throwing up of a dike on the Warta river, for which the hereditary lord promises to give a hundred men by degrees in help. And so that the closer survey of the land may be brought about the sooner the better, the takers of the hereditary lease bind themselves to bring about the draining of the land as soon as possible."*

From this it follows that the piece of forest given over for settlement was so swampy that it first had to be properly drained; otherwise not even a survey of the ground could be carried out.

In 1796 the governor "of the city and fortress of Breslau", the Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, received from King Frederick William II, as a gift estate (Gratialgut), the church estates of the lordship of Zagórowo and the starosty estates of the lordship of Trombczyn, made up of the manor farms (Vorwerke) of Drzewce, Kopojno, Swiątniki, Swięcia, Bronniki, Nowa Wieś, Przybyłow, Alt-Hütte and Trombczyn and the villages of Oleśnica and Szetlewek. Extensive forest and meadow land in the Warthe valley likewise took up a considerable part of the gift estates. Working these complexes of estates of some 250 Hufen may have brought in little, since as early as 18 May 1805 Prince Hohenlohe's general agent, the notorious war and forest councillor (Kriegs- und Forstrat) P. F. von Triebenfeld, had concluded with eighteen German colonists a contract of hereditary lease (Erbpacht) for the "dismembration" (division) of 100 Hufen of forest and, in small part, of farmland. The Royal South Prussian War and Domains Chamber at Kalisz gave its consent to this afterwards, on 28 January 1806. In this way the following German villages arose in the Warthe marshes near Zagórow: *Sophiental* - Olchowo, *Ludwigslust* - Mały Las, *Friedrichsfelde* - Tarzewo, *Adelshof* - Chróściki; the manor farm of *Adolfsberg*; on the neighbouring upland of the Kalisz country the villages of *Ingelfingen* - Anielewo, *Emilienheim* - Michalinow, *Augustinhof* - Augustynow, *Schwentz* - Święcia, and the colony of *Wrombczyn*. Of the fate of the German inhabitants of the hamlet of *Klein-Öring*, or Groździkowo, we have no sure news at all. An official Polish list of 1827 gives 4 farms with 39 inhabitants. The census of 1921 records only Polish owners. The settlement contract, written in German and well kept in the headman's office (Schulzenamt) at Emilienheim, lets us gain a good insight into the first terms of settlement. It would lead too far to set out all the details of the history of settlement in full. But one fact of importance, new in the practice of settlement until then, may not go unmentioned: *the complete freeing of the colonists from*

[p. 22]

*all services to the manor, with the duty instead to pay a money rent*. Or as paragraph 13 of the contract of sale in question reads:

> *"The hereditary lessees and their descendants remain for ever wholly free of all hereditary subjection, and may not do the lordship the least services at the manor nor deliver any produce whatever."*

For the schoolteacher, the headman and the cemetery 21 Morgen were allotted. What is remarkable in this taking of land is that the 100 Hufen of forest ground were divided among 18 takers, who in their turn passed the pieces of forest on to other, smaller applicants keen to clear. All the new settlers had the right of pasture and of wood in the Prince's heath. The colonists bound themselves to pay the rent, "the hereditary canon" (Erbkanon), after the four free years ran out, at Christmas and at St John's, namely thirty Reichsthaler a year for each cleared Magdeburg Hufe. After the South Prussian authorities left in the autumn of 1806, certain changes came about in the holdings of the newly laid-out marsh villages, although the estate's administration had in good time provided the settlers with legally valid hereditary deeds. The withdrawal of the Prussian government brought the colonists many troubles, as will be told in the section "Migrations". And for all that, these villages have held up well to the present, both economically and in their national character (in völkischer Hinsicht). From 1828 the Zagórow estates were owned by K. G. von Weigel, a great landowner from Hennersdorf in Silesia. After the Polish rising of 1863 the Polish Count A. Taczanowski acquired the Zagórow properties. The German administration of the marsh villages, lasting a good 35 years, did much for their strength. Legal independence and freedom from rent payments to the Zagórow manor came at last with the Russian emancipation of the peasants of 1864. To conclude, it should be stated that in the time of the South Prussian government 10 new villages arose in the Warthe marshes.

### The Period from 1806 to 1863

Around 1807 the marsh village of Daniszew was laid out from the estate of Leszczyn beside the old village of Daniszew. The meadows of this village were small in extent, and so were its first pieces of field. For this reason the newcomers were forced to set about clearing the forest on the sandy valley slope. In this way, besides the "meadow pieces" with the "building plots" on them, there arose in the present field area the "forest strips" (Waldkaweln), the "mud strips" (Blottkaweln) and the "highway strips" (Chausseekaweln). As a result of the emigration to Volhynia around 1875 some of the farms got

[p. 23]

Polish owners. Life together in this remaining village is quite peaceful, however.

At the same time the lord of Piorunow laid out in swampy ground the German village of *Genowefa Pior.*, in which mostly poor settlers established themselves, so-called "Büdner" (cottagers). Near the old village of Groß-Politz the new village of Klein-Politz arose around 1810, which like the old one was taken up mainly by well-to-do farmers.

[Picture: German farm girl from Drożynek-Holl., Konin county]

From what has been set out it follows that most of the German settlements were laid out at the end of the old Polish period. The private interests of the Polish lords were the cause of the German colonisation. The German settlers took over either felled stretches of forest or mouldering meadows grown with alder bushes. The quality of the soil was in most cases poor, mainly sandy and swampy, a so-called "Schliefboden" or "alder ground", as the colonists called it. In single places there was also very fertile silt soil, the "Letten".

After the lively activity of settlement in the first decade of the 19th century there came a pause of a good twenty years. The industrialisation of Congress Poland, still carried out in 1820,

[p. 24]

which brought with it the shooting up of grain prices and the rise of agricultural industries (distilleries and sugar factories), made the price of land rise markedly. At the same time the landowners, besides tilling their fields better, also thought of enlarging them. Deserted land, marsh ground and wastes came under the plough again. In some regions of Congress Poland new manor land was won by clearing out peasants (Bauernlegen). Not least, the landowners took up a new settling of their waste lands by German colonists. In 1818 the Holländerei of Budki grew in area by the founding of *Neu-Budki*. Around 1820, 18 German marsh farmers laid out the village of *Konstantynow* and *Wysoker Holl.* in the forests of the estate of Podgórz. The settlers received from the lord rights of wood and pasture, but no school land. At the same time *Rostoka-Holl.* arose close by the bank of the Warthe, which received the right of settlement from the estate of Krzymow. The lord of the manor of Wola Laszczowa, K. Potworowski, in March 1836 let out the "Great and Little Forest Ground" of 400 Morgen, bought from the government domain of Stare Miasto, Konin county, to German takers, who founded the village of *Neu-Czarkow*. As purchase money the new settlers bound themselves to pay a hundred złoty for each Morgen. For the pieces of land with timber on them a separate payment for the timber was asked, by the valuation of trusted men named by both sides. There were two or three free years, according to the quality of the forest land. Near the village of Alt-Budki the villages of *Neudorf* and *Rosenberg* were founded around 1835. The latter small settlement vanished after some decades. The German farmers gradually vanished from Neudorf too, as in general German landholding has much declined in this region.

In 1837 the German farmers of the village of Lesnik enlarged their field area by buying pieces of land from the neighbouring estate of Borzewisko. For the newly acquired land they received five free years.

In March 1841 a contract of sale was drawn up at the manor of Jabłkow, Konin county, between the lord, Karl Osterloff, and 27 German and 10 Polish farmers. On the estate's lands, partly swampy and partly overgrown with brush, together with the pieces of field farmed until then by the estate's peasants, arose the villages of *Bilczew* and *Strumyk*. In the latter new village mostly Polish takers settled. The surveyor R. Sydow measured the land. To drain the swampy meadows the landowner bound himself to dig two

[p. 25]

broad ditches. The timber standing on the Hufen allotted was given to the single buyers free of charge. For the village schoolteacher, who was bound to teach German and Polish, Osterloff gave ten Morgen of land. The house of the estate's steward, after its transfer and due rebuilding, was to serve as the village school house. The single colonists received one to three free years. The lord Osterloff had bought the estate of Jabłkow, which fell to be divided, a year before at a public auction before the court tribunal in Kalisz. From the estate of Głowy the villages of *Janow* and *Praksedow* were founded in 1840. The lord bequeathed three Morgen of land to the village school. The takers Kaus and Schneider conducted the negotiations with the manor of Głowy. The first settlers, 20 in number, mostly received pieces of a Hufe, half of them meadow.

The owner of the estate of Piekary, Count Grabowski, on 13 November 1841 concluded with German, and in part Polish, colonists a contract for an "everlasting rent lease" (ewige Zinspacht), by which he had forest land measured out to them for laying out the villages of *Józefow*, *Młyny*, *Lengden*, *Neu-Kaczka* and *Rosengarten*. In a complaint against the later administrator of the estate of Piekary the lawyer points out, among other things:

> *"The colonies named above are laid out in forests, on pastures or on waste places; and if some arose on former village lands,* then *the arable lands of those villages were joined to the manor farms; or else all the pastures and meadows in any way fit [angengigen] for tillage that had fallen to the colonies by allotment were likewise separated off."*

After three years the colonies of *Zagaj* and *Stawki* arose within the estate of Piekary. The field areas of the old villages of Mechniówka, Rzechta and Czyste were considerably enlarged by new cleared land and some worthless pieces of meadow. The surveyor J. Bernauer divided the land both times. Each new village received from the lord two Morgen of school land and half a Hufe of headman's land. Around 1850 the villages of *Człopy*, *Dombrowica* and *Paulina* were founded.

Striking is the rise of 10 German villages in the Leśnik language island in the fifth decade of the 19th century. The soil here is the poorest imaginable, and there is also a lack of meadows. The founding of these daughter settlements goes back to the strong growth of population in the old villages around Leśnik. No influx of settlers from the area of other German

[p. 26]

villages could be found. Of the takers of the marsh villages of Neu-Czarkow and Bilczew we have sufficient knowledge. The farmers of the first village all came from the Warthe marshes, namely from Briesener Holl., from Ranna, Piersk, Młynek, Konstantynow, Slauske, Dembina and Schwentz; those of the village of Bilczew likewise from Lesche, Sobótka, Rostoka, Kramsk, Drensen, Daniszew, Politz, Kuny and Helenowo; one came, by exception, from Lipiny near Babiak. The chief taker of the village of Bilczew was Gottlieb Wapner, who had drawn up the settlement contracts with the lord in German.

### The Time from 1863 to the Present

By about 1860 the room for settlement in the marsh was almost wholly taken up by German and Polish farmers. From year to year the pressure of overpopulation made itself felt more strongly. After 1875 German marsh farmers divided up the manor farm of Dombrowica near Konin. Otherwise settlement rested, apart from single settlers moving here and there into Polish villages. The growing drainage of swampland brought some marsh villages the new land they wanted, but this could by no means still the land hunger there was. When, after 1875, word reached the German marsh villages of the good chances of settlement in the forests of Volhynia, the young people lent it a willing ear. The share in emigration was especially great in the Paprot and Leśnik language islands, since overpopulation there was great and the soil poor. Many villages suffered a great loss of inhabitants; indeed single places broke up altogether and the village land passed into strange hands. That was so with the villages of Piersk, Budki and Strumyk. Single settlements, such as Borowo, Drensen Holl., Konstantynow, Młynek, Józefina, Alexandrow, Mechniówka, Zagaj, Dombrowa Holl., Zbylczyce Holl., Genowefa and so on, lost almost half their German inhabitants. Single villages succeeded in time in making good the losses of land to Volhynia. Most of the remaining villages have unfortunately kept [beibehalte] their mixed-language character to the present. After 1890 the German surplus of population found an outlet in seasonal work in Germany (Sachsengängerei). The rise of the village of Neu-Krzymow on the lands of the abandoned estate of Krzymow goes back to German seasonal workers who laid out this scattered village around 1906.

At various times there were *settlements by single families* from the German places into neighbouring Polish villages, as to Posoka, Ksawerowo, Kendziorki, Ochle, Lichnowo, Rzechta, Isabelin, Wionsowa and Trzyborki. Experience shows that these

[p. 27]

settlements by single families do not last long. The dangers of depopulation in the mixed-language villages are especially great for the German part.

After the World War no notable shift in the holdings of the German marsh villages has taken place. Partible inheritance (Realteilung) is gradually beginning to take root in single villages, since there are no other chances of settlement. Against this it should be stressed that modern ideas of farming are also finding their way into the marsh villages and so help to ease the struggle for existence. There is thus no ground for special worries.

[Picture: Warthe marshes. A Koppel (fenced pasture) in the German village of Leśnik-Holland - Photo: Rode]

And to close this section, let this telling observation be made: the German settler of this region took over, without exception, a swamp wilderness of backwaters and sandbanks. The marsh soil was mostly sandy or leached, shot through with sour humus. Wide stretches of the river valley were exposed to the danger of the yearly floods of the Warthe. The bad harvests and the losses of livestock they caused are among the ever-recurring events in the life of the marsh farmer. Dry years likewise rob the farmer of the hoped-for fruits of his work. The church books of the Evangelical parishes, and on the other hand the oral tradition from

[p. 28]

the marsh, tell us abundantly of the hard struggle of these German people for their daily bread. And despite the unfavourable conditions in many places, the tough breed of the Brücher has held its own victoriously to the present. Its German sentiment showed itself especially clearly at the time of the first Polish census (1921). At least 95 per cent of the marsh farmers had themselves entered by the enumerators as people of German nationality (deutschen Volkstums). We have met an equally upright national (völkisch) bearing only among the German farmers of the Vistula lowland (Niederung).

## IV. The Origin of the German Settlers

For the present, the only source for research into origins is the church books of the Evangelical parishes of Turek, Władysławow, Koło, Sompolno, Konin and Zagórów. On the ground of the inquiries made in these church archives over the years, the following findings on origins could be made, and these were further supplemented by personal talks in the German places themselves. Among the inhabitants of the marsh around Zagórów and Konin the traditions of origin are still quite alive, as these marsh farmers are in general well distinguished by a good national (völkisch) bearing. In the Leśnik language island the memory of the land of origin is as good as extinct.

Seen by stock (stammlich), the Germans of the Warthe marshes fall into two parts: the *Märker* (of the Mark of Brandenburg) and the *Pomeranians*. The stretch of river between Konin and Uniejów became a zone where the two stocks mixed, which may be connected with the direction in which they migrated and with the nature of the soil of the land settled. The river valley between Peisern and Koło is mostly a typical marsh landscape with periodic floods and much swampland. Only a settler familiar with the ways of farming in a marsh could decide to settle in this region. At any rate the Pomeranian farmers did not; they preferred the Warthe valley south of Uniejów, seldom visited by floods, or settled on the flat sandy slopes of the valley. In the mixed area near Krzymow they chose the places in the valley safe from floods.

The Märker, or "Braneborger" as they are commonly called, are made up of two groups of immigrants from separate regions: the inhabitants of the *Warthe marshes* (Warthebruch) around the towns of Driesen and Landsberg, and the farmers from the *Priegnitz*, chiefly from the districts on the Oder and its tributary the Stepenitz. One and all they are farmer-burghers (Ackerbürger) well familiar with farming in a marsh. The single immigrants came from the following villages of the Netze marshes around Driesen: Lubatz, Lupsbruch, Alt-Beelitz, Friedrichshorst, Neu-Ansbach, Hammer, Eschbruch, Drewitz, Vordamm, Neu-Erbach, Schöneberg, Netzbruch, Alt-Haberwiese. The following marsh villages around Landsberg gave up emigrants: Landsberger Holländer, Birkbruch, Marienwiese, Liebental, Güscht, Christophswalde, Pollichen, Zantoch, Gratow, Dexel, Wilhelmstal, Liebental, Schönewald, Morrn, Blockwinkel, Raumerswalde, Scheiblersberg, Neu-Dresden, Kriescht, Woxfelde, Woxholland, Neu Lietze-Göricke, Neu-Küstrinchen near Wriezen, Moldau near Sonnenburg. From the areas north of the marsh came single ones, as from Fahlenwerder, Woldenberg,

[p. 30]

Neu-Falkenberg, Kartzig, Merenthin, Stüdnitz, Glashütte; from south of the marsh single ones came from Radgosch, Alt-Merine Buchholz, Reichenwalde and Langendamm. Further, the Oder marshes (Oderbruch) likewise gave up some people eager to emigrate, as from around Frankfurt and Wriezen, from the villages of Letschin, Zirpe, Oderbruch. From the province of Priegnitz people came from around Prietzwalk, Wittenberg, Perleberg, Meienberg, Wittstock and Neu-Ruppin. The district of Havelberg gave up emigrants from the villages of Breddlin, Bendelin, Damelack, Neuberg, Kümmernitz and Saldernhorst. In the mixed zone we also come upon single immigrants from places in Pomerania and Mecklenburg-Schwerin: from the former from Berlinchen and Neu-Marien, from the latter from Schupin. Some colonists come from the province of Posen, from Lubatz near Filehne, from Storchnest, from Wysokotowo, Jarotschin district; the village of Neu-Marya not far from Birnbaum is given as the place of origin of three colonists. In number, of course, the farmers coming directly from the marshes prevail.

[Map: Places of origin of the German rural settlers of the Warthe marshes in central Poland]

The settlers of Pomeranian stock immigrated from the Netze district into the Warthe country south of the town of Koło. In the settlement charter of Leśnik Holl. we find the direct reference to *"colonists come here from the Netze District"*. The church books contain places from the districts of Samter, Obornik, Kolmar and Filehne. Single places of origin lay in the border districts of Wreschen, Witkowo and Mogilno. For completeness, the arrival of settlers from the Lower Silesian districts of Krotoschin, Ostrowo, Koschmin and Gostyń should also be mentioned. There were, however, only a few Silesians in the

[p. 31]

Pomeranian villages. In their new surroundings the Silesians lost the peculiarities of their stock in time.

Characteristic of the Pomeranian settler is his tenacious clinging to his inherited dialect, especially in the area uniform in stock. In the villages of mixed stock the Pomeranian dialect has had to give way to a popular German. The Märker marsh settlements have been untrue to their old home speech for many decades. The lively timber-rafting on the Warthe, in which the Märker marsh farmers took a lively part, may have contributed much to this. These differences of dialect mean that the Germans of the Warthe marshes have not yet become a uniformly aligned national body (Volkskörper). The true "Brücher" looks down on the "Kashubian", which has its ground in part in the economic inequality of the two stocks. As a rule marriages are made only between partners of the same stock. Of course it is not only differences of stock that play a part here, but rather questions of the size of the dowry. The German countryman is, like every other of his station, a merciless reckoner, since the farm to be taken over may not be burdened with excessive debts.

The places of origin given here by no means give us a full picture. The gaps come for the most part from the lack of places named over whole decades. So for the period from 1805 to 1825 almost no places of origin at all are available to us. The want of these is the fault of the Evangelical pastors and cantors of the time, who, to save time and from convenience, made only very short entries of civil record. With the founding of the parishes of Konin, Koło, Turek, Sompolno and Zagorów, which took place between 1826 and 1858, a proper drawing up of suitably full records began. It should further be pointed out that in many notes of the deaths of retired farmers the places of origin [Herkunfsangaben] are missing. Moreover, when some of the record notes were drawn up, the place of origin may simply have slipped the

[p. 36]

memory of those concerned, which is often the case even today, especially with older country people. Lastly, it is quite possible that false birthplaces were often given without ill will, simply from embarrassment. This happens especially often, since the clerk only in the rarest cases asks to be shown a paper.

The places of origin given here are not to be taken as complete. The intention is to close all the searches at a common year, about 1870. For guidance we give the years of the marriage and burial books searched so far, since the baptism books only in the rarest case give places of origin: Władysławow 1778 - 1833, Grodziec 1798 - 1842, Konin 1826 - 1845, Turek 1837 - 1851, Sompolno 1840 - 1865, Koło 1843 - 1862, Zagórow 1843 - 1867. Immigrants from the Mark also streamed in great numbers into the forest region south of the Warthe. There are many records of this from the parishes of Stawiszyn, Turek and Prażuchy. A summary article on the immigration from the Mark is soon to appear.

## V. Migrations

The village people of the marsh, like those of other regions, are subject to a constant flowing out and in. Scarcely settled, some of them turn over thoughts of moving away to the nearer or wider neighbourhood. At times outward causes give the motive for moving on. This happened, for example, in November 1806, when the South Prussian authorities were forced to leave the country and a flood of persecutions poured over the colonists, who had scarcely settled, as was the case, for instance, in the marsh villages of the Hohenlohe estates near Zagórow. Two colonists from Ludwigslust report on this in a petition of August 1816 to the Prussian government in Königsberg. In the name of 10 families the two made a submission to the East Prussian authorities asking leave to move back to Prussia, in which they paint in dark colours that very evil time of November 1806, as follows:

> *"Right and justice had ceased for them, and the violence of the Poles had begun to rule over them, orphans left behind. Through the many terrible revolts they had been plundered and robbed on several occasions — and also some of us Germans were killed outright, many beaten so badly that there was nothing left between death and life. On top of this, the enormous circumstances of war, marches through, quarterings, were shifted as far as possible onto us Germans by the neighbouring places. — To this came heavy, unpaid deliveries, carting services and other burdens of war, so that whole communities, in which there are good, honest, industrious farmers, must lose their property and leave it, go to Russia and Bessarabia, take refuge on waste grounds... Now they are totally — ruined, so that nothing is left for our keep."*[^17]

The petition of the Ludwigslust petitioners unfortunately found no hearing with the government offices in Königsberg, since they were not "real colonists", settled on state land and with state funds. The Hohenlohe possessions were only formerly Polish gift estates (Gratialgüter) given away by the Prussian king.

[^17]: Pokrandt, A., Dt. Rückwanderung aus Mittelpolen nach 1815 (German return migration from central Poland after 1815), DMP., year 1936, no. 3/4, p. 126

[p. 39]

After the refusal there was probably nothing left for the sorely tried colonists but to take up the "unwanted wanderer's staff", as 41 families from around the town of Konin had in fact done before them in the spring of 1815, led by the Evangelical governor of the town of Kalisz: they emigrated to Bessarabia and founded the village of Alt-Arzis on steppe ground.[^18] Unfortunately we do not know the closer circumstances of the departure of this second group. That German marsh farmers from around Konin also took part in the colony of Paris, founded in Bessarabia in 1816, is quite likely; the family names point to it.[^19]

As late as October 1818, 40 relatively well-off Hohenlohe colonists from the village of Święcia turned to the government in Königsberg with a petition for leave to move back, after the government offices at Posen had previously given them a refusal.[^20] In Königsberg the Święcia farmers likewise had no success.

In the thirties single marsh dwellers found their way into the rising Łódź region, as appears from the church books of Pabianitz, Alexander and so on, although there were still enough chances of settlement in the Warthe marshes of the time.

Around 1830 the Kowalewken Holländerei near Konin vanishes, two decades later the Rumin Holl., which belonged to the crown estate of Rumin, and Klein-Öring near Zagórow. In the marsh east of Konin, where a lively German settlement had developed between 1820 and 1840, the emigration to Volhynia tore considerable gaps. It began shortly after 1872 and ended around 1890. Some figures may show us the scale of this ebbing away of German farmers. In 1882 the German share of the population of the commune (Gemeinde) of Krzymow, later Brzeźno, was 45 per cent, since of 4,596 inhabitants 2,158 were Germans. By the Polish census of 1921, 739 Evangelicals lived in the commune of Brzeźno, which is only 15 per cent. A picture not quite so dark is given by the neighbouring commune of Wysokie, formerly Kramsk. In 1882, among 3,716 inhabitants, 433 Germans lived there, which is barely 12 per cent.

[^18]: Leibbrandt, G., Die Dt. Kolonien in Cherson und Bessarabien (The German colonies in Kherson and Bessarabia), Stuttgart 1926, p. 134

[^19]: Leibbrandt 1926, p. 166

[^20]: Pokrandt 1936, p. 127

[p. 40]

The census of 1921 records only 208 Germans, which gives 6 per cent. In this commune the village of Piersk lost all its German inhabitants, and in the neighbouring commune likewise the villages of Górka and Grabczyna. Quite considerable too is the number of remaining villages (Restdörfer) that owe their state to the fever of emigration to Volhynia. Most of the marsh villages in the Paprot, Politz and Leśnik islands had to undergo a heavy depopulation, from whose effects they will hardly ever be able to recover. In most cases the floods returning every year may have had a deciding influence here on the resolve to emigrate. If one walks through the fine Warthe villages in summer and looks at the land and the people, one finds no striking signs of want in the single places. Nor is the quality of the soil by any means poor. Most marsh farms draw quite considerable earnings from their meadows in particular. The exception is the Leśnik language island, which has very sandy soil of little yield, especially in the settlements on the edge of the banks. That the soils here could be called hostile to settlement also appears from the following. In the north of the Leśnik language island a broad strip of pine forest runs for almost 10 km parallel to the edge of the banks, planted on infertile estate land that it certainly did not pay to take under cultivation. Extensive pine forests are likewise found in the west of the Leśnik language island, where again they are owed to sandy soil. The leached alluvial soils around Leśnik and Koło may share the blame for the Volhynian travel fever. If we look at the emigration to Volhynia from the point of view of stock, the great share of the Pomeranian stock strikes us. For it was just the villages strongly mixed with Pomeranians, or wholly inhabited by them, that gave up most emigrants, which, as already said, again stands in close connection with the poor quality of the soils. In the Paprot language island the Russia fever left the following remaining villages: Borowo, Kędziorki, Lichnow, Ranna, Zabłocie, Drensen Holl., Alt- and Neu Paprot Holl., Konstantynów, Budki, Młynek. In the Politz language island farmers from the villages of Politz, Groß Lengden, Maidan Holl., Praxedow, Kwiatkow and Sarbice went to Volhynia. South of Uniejow many moved from the places [Ortsaften] of Czyste, Zagaj, Stawki, Alt-Dombrowa and Leśnik into the Volhynian forests. Happily the emigration here did not destroy the village in most settlements. The land mostly passed into German hands. The sandy soil did not tempt the Polish countryman either to buy in the German village. Unfortunately in some remaining villages south of Koło a danger of de-Germanisation (Entdeutschung) is beginning to loom, which may have sad consequences. We see the grounds of this national danger in the absence of German schools and in the want of German religious instruction. The isolation of these villages from other German settlements is also partly to blame. In the purely Märker villages only

[p. 41]

a few took part in the search for land in Volhynia. The good soils with grassy meadows made distant parts little tempting.

After 1895 emigration to North America appeared, which was, however, far outstripped by seasonal work in Germany (Sachsengängerei). In the last years before the World War seasonal work grew so much in scale that in summer some marsh villages could be counted almost bare of people able to work. Women and old men did the field work with the help of Polish servants. Many of the seasonal workers found lasting employment in the old homeland and in time acquired citizenship there. In this way the family ties arose that are still alive today. There were also single farm owners who bought land from the Prussian Settlement Commission, as around Jarotschin and in the southern part of the province of Posen.

[Picture: The German village of Leśnik-Holl., lying on the edge of the bank of the Warthe]

Only a vanishingly small number took part in the emigration to Canada. At present the surplus of people is building up in the single marsh villages, since difficulties are put in the way of seasonal work and there are no other chances of settlement. This leads here and there to partible inheritance and to the move to the two-child system.

For completeness, the *Mennonite* settlers of the Warthe marshes should also be remembered. By oral tradition, Mennonite marsh farmers still sat in the village of Adelhof around 1890, called "Ortsleute" by the Evangelical Germans.

[p. 42]

They felt wronged by the introduction of compulsory military service and all emigrated to North America. A note in the baptism book of the Evangelical parish at Władysławow of 1821 says that in the marsh village of Daniszew three Mennonite children of the Goerz family, aged 15, 9 and 6, were baptised Lutheran. The local teacher Wegner stood godfather. The eldest daughter was also confirmed at the same time.

From the last list of owners it can be seen that a certain splitting of the single farms has taken place in the village. On the whole, however, the economic conditions are to be called healthy. A certain prosperity can even be found in this village. Single large farmers may put their wealth at at least 50,000 zł, a Morgen of land at 500 zł being reckoned not too high, since Schwenz has good grain soil. Dr. Reiser has shown these healthy peasant conditions vividly, by marking on a map of landholding the size of each single farmstead with coloured circles to scale. Schwenz looks well

[p. 44]

on this map[^21]. It should be pointed out that the village's landholding has grown somewhat by purchases in the neighbouring Polish villages.

If over 110 years the size of the holdings underwent no great swings, the same cannot be said of the holders. Of the farming families of about 1818, the direct descendants have stayed in the village in only five cases, which makes 12.5 in a hundred. Changes of ownership appear quite often in the German villages in the course of time, which likewise, on a small scale, helps to make the conditions of stock changeable. In the example of Schwenz we see a constant up and down of the villagers. Most marsh villages can count themselves lucky, since whenever ownership changed, apart from the mass emigration to Volhynia, German buyers from the nearer or wider neighbourhood always stepped in, and in this way the uniform character of the village community has been kept.

## VII. Appendix

### Founding Charter of the Village of Leśnik on the Warthe

> *Done at the District Justice Commission at Lask, the 22nd of July 1796. Before the said District Justice Commission appeared 1. Constantin von Milewski, owner in his own right of the estate of Borszewice lying in the Lutomiersk district, who is well known in person to the court, as grantor of the hereditary lease (Erbzinsgeber) on the one part; 2. the colonists staying at Borschewice and come here from the Netze District, 1. Johann Gottfreid Peglow, 2. Caspar Hoffmann, 3. Johann George Fosberg, 4. David Dohm, 5. Gottfried Kant, 6. Friedrich Dickow, for themselves and their absent fellow takers, 7. Christian Chayn, 8. Gottlieb Hayn, 9. Martin Buffal, 10. Andreas Thomek, in whose names they also contracted with the first contracting party, and for whose approval they therefore also gave surety de rato et grato [casirten], as takers of the hereditary lease (Erbzinsnehmer) on the other part, who were known to the court in person only slightly [nährbekannt], but whose identity was attested by the gaoler here, Zingel, who had come to know them on his journeys through the district. First the contracting party v. Milewski stated that he was the true owner of the estate of Borszewice, had already declared it to the Mortgage Commission at Peterkau and become known in that quality. Next, that he was minded [gesonen] to let out from his estate of Borszewice a piece of forest land of 10 Hufen to the aforesaid colonists for clearing and building; that they had already drawn up a Polish contract about it, and so for the completeness of the matter only the drawing up of the contract before the court was needed, which they asked to have executed. The aforesaid colonists were wholly versed in the German language. The contracting party v. Milewski was indeed not versed in the German language, but had with him his leaseholder Joseph Brzechwa, who was likewise equal to the German language. They therefore declared that the taking of a Polish protocol of declaration was not needed; rather they would be content if it were issued only with the Polish translation. The Polish contract handed in was put to the file, and on its ground the following contract was declared to the protocol.*

[p. 50]

> *§ 1*

> *Constantin v. Milewski, owner in his own right of the noble estate of Borszewice lying in the Lutomiersk district under the Royal South Prussian Government at Peterkau, for himself, his heirs and descendants, grants, deeds and hands over, from the estate of Borszewice belonging to him and the forest land lying by it, to the aforesaid colonists, their heirs and descendants, ten Hufen of forest land, say 10 Hufen of land, with the meadows lying in it, the so-called Lesznik lying in it, and the so-called Borzewice Zazpak belonging to it, as this was agreed in general by the chief takers Gottlob Kanka and Friedrich Dickow, and has already been assigned and measured out to the aforesaid colonists, for clearing, building and free use under hereditary lease (Erbzins) rights, now and for ever, at their own knowledge and pleasure, without harm to any third party.*

> *§ 2*

> *Of these ten agreed Hufen of hereditary lease, by agreement of both contracting parties, a quarter Hufe is reckoned for the headman (Schulz), a quarter Hufe allotted to the schoolmaster, a quarter Hufe must be deeded to the colonists' inn (Krug) to be built, a half Hufe assigned to the first chief taker Gottfried Kanka, his heirs and successors, a half Hufe handed over to the other chief taker, Friedrich Dickow; which together make seven quarter Hufen, and which remain now and for ever not only free of the present purchase money, but also now and in future free of all the lord's rents and dues; as for the dues belonging to the community, however, their holders are bound to bear them like the others. The other eight and a quarter Hufen are divided so that of them Gottfried Peglow receives three Hufen, Caspar Hoffmann one Hufe, George Fasberg one Hufe, Christian Hayn a quarter Hufe, Gottfried Hayn a quarter Hufe, Martin Bufall a half Hufe, David Dohm two Hufen, Andreas Thomek one quarter, as their property and for their building and setting up.*

[p. 51]

> *§3*

> *Although the land has already been measured, and each taker of the hereditary lease has already been assigned his set land, yet, considering that the measuring of this land took place without the owner of the estate being present, this measuring shall be done again, the owner of the estate alone bearing its costs; and it is laid down that, should it be found at this measuring that the land marked out and measured comes to more than 10 Hufen, the excess is to be given back. But should it be found to come to less than ten Hufen, what is lacking shall be allotted.*

> *§ 4*

> *The Hufen are measured by Rhineland measure. Each Hufe shall contain thirty Morgen, each Morgen three hundred rods (Ruthen), each rod seven and a half Rhineland ells, and the ten [Hufen] including the meadows and fields lying in them.*

> *§ 5*

> *Considering that the land measured out to the colonists, and to be more closely defined, is much run over with water, the takers of the hereditary lease bind themselves to drain it by the proper digging of ditches and the throwing up of a dike on the Warta river, for which the hereditary lord promises to give a hundred men by degrees in help. And so that the closer survey of the land may be brought about the sooner the better, the takers of the hereditary lease bind themselves to bring about the draining of the land as soon as possible.*

> *§ 6*

> *Of this number of Hufen taken over, as already laid down, seven quarter Hufen remain free of all purchase money; for the other eight Hufen the takers of the hereditary lease pay to the hereditary lord Constantin von Milewski for each Hufe a purchase money of 1150 fl., say one thousand one hundred and fifty Polish Gulden, and also a glove money (Handschuh Geld) of three Polish Gulden on every hundred Gulden; both purchase and glove money are settled and paid at once on the signing of the contract.*

[p. 52]

> *§ 7*

> *Besides the purchase and glove money, the takers of the hereditary lease undertake to pay the hereditary lord for each Hufe a yearly ground rent of 138 fl., say one hundred and thirty-eight Gulden, and also from these Hufen the tenth groschen at ten Polish Gulden a year; both the ground rent and the tenth groschen, which however is to be paid on all ten Hufen, to be settled at Martinmas each year.*

> *§ 8*

> *Further, the takers of the hereditary lease undertake to deliver to the lord every year for each Hufe two geese, three capons and forty eggs in kind, of which dues in kind the geese and capons must be handed in at Martinmas, but the eggs at Easter each year.*

> *§ 9*

> *As hearth money (Rauchfangs Geldern) the takers of the hereditary lease undertake to pay ten Polish Gulden, namely those who have until now leased the cottages (Kathen) lying on Lesznik and sold with it separately; this is likewise to be paid to the manor at Martinmas each year.*

> *§ 10*

> *As days of labour service (Scharwerk) the takers of the hereditary lease undertake to do the lordship six days a year for each Hufe, namely two days with the scythe, two days carting dung and two days with the plough, in which last two days two Morgen must be ploughed for each Hufe, each Morgen ten rods wide and thirty rods long.*

> *§ 11*

> *Besides these days of labour service the takers of the hereditary lease are also bound to give the lord help at harvest, which is set so that each holder of a quarter Hufe gives one day, of a half Hufe two days and of a whole Hufe three days with the sickle.*

[p. 53]

> *§ 12*

> *For building the houses and the farm buildings belonging to them, the holders of the hereditary lease have already been assigned the places needed. Chiefly they shall build in the lowland towards the church mill, on the right side of Borszewice, on the Warta river, or on the Lesznik, or on their shares, as they think fit; but the building in the lowland shall extend only to the part of the river where one crosses, and to the so-called old Rzeczyska beside the little ponds on the side of Lesznik as far as towards Spicimierz. The inn itself, however, shall be built on the road by the church mill.*

> *§ 13*

> *For building the houses and farm buildings, free wood as much as they need, be it building timber, saw logs, rafter wood, lath poles and the like, is given to the hereditary takers from the lord's forest, namely from the Krempki Brod, as the wood stands in it. From the patch assigned they are entitled to take the wood of that kind, but also bound to cut it down one after the other. From other pieces of the lord's forest, however, they are not entitled to cut building timber.*

> *§ 14*

> *The hereditary holders are likewise allowed to take firewood from the lord's forests belonging to Borszewice. As long as they have firewood from their own Hufen they are bound to take it from these. After these are cleared, the forest by Spicimierz, from the boundary mounds to the Borzewice peasants' meadows, is assigned to them for their need of firewood. Should there later be no firewood in it, the hereditary lord binds himself to assign another piece of forest in its place; for this free cutting of firewood each holder is bound to pay the lord ten Gulden cull. a year at Martinmas, it being understood of course that if there is no firewood at all in the lord's forests the due of ten Gulden also ceases.*

> *§ 15*

> *Free pasture is allowed to the hereditary takers and the holders for the time being, both for their cattle and for sheep, in the lord's forest on the church boundary as far as the road from Borzewice to Niemysłów, in length and breadth as the Hufen run; but the hereditary takers are not to presume*

[p. 54]

> *to sell any wood from this or from any other piece of the lord's forest, on pain of five Thaler for each case of contravention. As for the wood on the Hufen allotted to them, however, it remains theirs as their property, free to sell.*

> *§ 16*

> *In the waters lying within the bounds of the takers of the hereditary lease, fishing and the taking of crayfish are also allowed them; but they must not presume to fish or take crayfish outside their bounds; rather the lordship reserves this to itself, indeed even within the boundary towards Spicimierz and the old Rzeczysko; and should it suit the manor to build a mill or lay out a fishery, the manor reserves to itself the place at the heath mill as far as the boundary of the church mill by the Warta river at the bare hill; and should the takers of the hereditary lease presume to fish or take crayfish outside their bounds, a fine of fifteen Thaler shall be paid to the lordship for each case of contravention.*

> *§ 17*

> *The innkeeper is indeed allowed to keep his own oats and roughage in the inn. On the other hand, no drink other than the lord's Borzewice drink, beer as well as brandy, shall be served in it or in the colony at all, on pain of confiscation of the drink and ten Thaler in money for each case of contravention; whereas if, against expectation, there should be no drink of the lord's, an order shall be given by the manor for procuring other drink.*

> *§ 18*

> *The takers of the hereditary lease are bound to keep the bounds of the ten Hufen given in hereditary lease, and to secure them they shall be allowed to dam them, mark their bounds, and also furnish them with water bridges; but they must take care not to do harm to the lord's meadows, or even to graze them off.*

> *§ 19*

> *As the ten Hufen assigned to the hereditary takers are deeded to them as true property, so they are also entitled to sell them and to dispose of them in any other lawful way. In case of a sale they are bound to notify the lordship of it and to obtain the due*

[p. 55]

> *consent and approval of the contract, and after the consent is received to pay the tenth groschen of the purchase money to the lordship.*

> *§ 20*

> *The takers of the hereditary lease promise to show the lordship due obedience and owed respect at all times, and also to conduct themselves towards it so that no well-founded complaint against them arises.*

> *§ 21*

> *Considering that in the ten Hufen deeded some patches of meadow are already arable, all dues are paid during the first three years only in proportion to the lands cleared, and all services and dues in kind are performed in the same proportion; but after three years the hereditary takers are bound to pay us the whole rents and dues, and during these three years to clear the land deeded.*

> *§ 22*

> *The takers of the hereditary lease alone are to pay the costs of this contract, and*

> *§ 23*

> *to hold to the contract both contracting parties set a pledge (vadium) of 5000 fl. cull., which the party not holding to it is bound to pay to the other, and also to keep the contract.*

> *§ 24*

> *The Hufen deeded are handed over to the hereditary takers for use at once, and in respect of them the hereditary lord undertakes the warranty (eviction) against all claims and demands of anyone. Herewith*

[p. 56]

> *§ 25*

> *both contracting parties renounce all exceptions and excuses that may arise in this contract, chiefly of injury, of overhaste, of persuasion and whatever other names they may have; rather they approve the contract and [ask that it be] issued to them in forma probandi [probandis]. They signed the present protocol of agreement after it was read out and ratified [Retihabilierung].*
