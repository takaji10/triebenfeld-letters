A NEW ANALYSIS OF THE "BLACK REGISTER".
A SOURCE STUDY

The "Black Register" is a commented list of the estates in Great Poland that the Prussian occupier gave away in the years 1796-1797 to Prussian officers, officials and junkers. The aim of the present analysis is to assess its value and significance. Sources that throw new light on the "Black Register" justify its use. The list of the estates given away also has a wider significance for assessing Prussian policy in South Prussia and Polish-German relations in this period. We shall therefore present the aims, the course and the effects of the giveaway of estates, taking into account a wider context than has been done so far.

The state of research

After 1945 Jan Wąsicki was the only student of the "Black Register", devoting to it his article Rozdawnictwo dóbr ziemskich w Prusach Południowych w latach 1796-1797 (The giveaway of landed estates in South Prussia in the years 1796-1797)[^1]. He set as the limit of his analysis the presentation of the giveaway of Polish estates by the Prussian authorities, which had earlier not been a separate subject of study in Polish historiography. Jan Wąsicki's article was a pioneering work — like a series of his other studies probing the history of South Prussia. It was also thanks to this historian that the subject was introduced into the history of Great Poland and into the synthesis of the history of Poland[^2].

While in the works of Polish historians the stressing of the rapacious and Germanising aspect of the giveaway of estates dominated, German historiography showed interest chiefly in the economic significance of the operation, interpreting the giveaway of estates as the drawing of Prussian capital into economically neglected Polish lands. The justification for selling off the domainal estates to private owners was the miserable state of the Prussian state's finances. It was therefore more profitable to hand the neglected estates to good husbanders, who would not only pay for them but also invest in them, and then pay taxes to the state treasury. Yet there were also many historians (Mayer, Philippson, Stadelmann, Bissing, Simsch, Beheim-Schwarzbach) who noticed that in practice the giveaway was very far from the ideal presented above. They pointed to corruption, the depriving of the state treasury of its due revenues, speculation in landed estates[^6].

Then, from these doubtful gifts — as Grünhagen would have it — the unhappy Prussian generals were to pay 74 or 100% tax, and then still invest in the estates thus paid for as tribute, spending money on Polish peasants unfit, in the author's opinion, for proper husbandry. Meanwhile, as if in defiance of these circumstances, the Prussian donataries not only did not receive these estates with disgust, but sold them on afterwards at great profit. This aspect of the unfortunate end of the giveaway the author passed over entirely. Grünhagen's error also lay in adopting the superficial assessment that the owners of the former starosties paid 24% contribution and 50% Kompetenzgeld[^9], that is 74% tax of the net revenue; and the owners of church estates 50% contribution and 50% Kompetenzgeld, that is 100% of the net revenue. This was supposed to have been possible because everyone knew anyway of the under-declaration of tax returns. In fact the donataries in the department of Poznań paid in every case only 24% contribution and 50% Kompetenzgeld. This by no means amounts to 74% of revenue. The contribution was reckoned on the net revenue (reine Ertrag), in Poland of the time — the intrata, that is the net income, after the deduction of the costs of husbandry (sowing). Then the 50% Kompetenzgeld likewise was not reckoned on the income, but on the net revenue, after deducting 5% for the costs of administration and 10% for the upkeep of buildings and farming stock[^10]. Only now can one say that by the Prussian standards of the time this was an extraordinarily burdensome taxation, and one must ask why in spite of it the donataries made enormous sums out of the undertaking.

The "Black Register" and new completions

We have reached the manuscript of the "Black Register"[^11], probably one of the copies. It is a small quire of 12 leaves, written in a legible hand, after the scheme repeated in the printed versions of the list. Likewise — as C. Grünhagen supposed — in 1849 J. Löwenberg[^12] used a copy in the possession of Tytus Działyński, and the anonymous publisher of the work Materialien zur Geschichte polnischer Landestheile unter preußischer Verwaltung (Leipzig 1861, H. 1, s. 140-171) used Löwenberg's edition. There should also be recorded the Polish edition of 1878 — anonymous and without commentary. In 1886, on the basis of the "Neue Feuerbrände", Władysław Smoleński published the "Black Register" as an appendix to his work Rządy pruskie na ziemiach polskich 1793-1807 (Warsaw 1886), Gdzie się podziały nasze królewszczyzny (Poznań 1878). Besides, Janusz Iwaszkiewicz published a list of 5 private estates confiscated by the Prussian authorities, Wykaz dóbr ziemskich skonfiskowanych przez rządy zaborcze w latach 1773-1867 (Warsaw 1929).

The register was first published by Joseph Zerboni di Sposetti in the periodical "Annalen der leidenden Menschheit"[^13] in 1801. The manuscript he extorted from Hans von Held; it was Friedrich von Cölln, a councillor of the Poznań Chamber, who completed Zerboni's compilation. He then published it in the periodical he edited in Amsterdam, the "Neue Feuerbände"[^14]. Zerboni's compilation bore the name General-Tableau von sämmtlichen in Südpreußen von des Königs Majestät Friderich Wilhelm II verschenkten königlichen und geistlichen Gütern. H. von Held gave it the title aimed against Hoym and preserved by history: Das schwarze Register oder General Tableau der von Friedrich Wilhelm II in Südpreußen verschenkten Güter während der Minister von Hoym diese Provinz verwaltet hat in den Jahren 1794-1798. It was of this version of the manuscript that we made use. The numerous interesting completions in the appendices to the monograph mentioned above were presented by C. Grünhagen[^15]. Jan Wąsicki published only incomplete data for 40 donataries. Omitted by him were councillor Unruh (Ritterschaftsrath), the president of Berlin Eisenberg[^16], chamberlain St. Patern, Prince Antoni Czetwertyński, Count Łuba, Dzierzbicki, director Goldbeck, minister Hoym[^17]. Before the Second World War — in 1936 — sample data for 22 donataries was published by Kazimiera Jeżowa[^18].

The basic problem has arisen so far from the discrepancy between the information declared on the first page, of both the manuscript and the printed editions of the source, that the real value of the 241[^19] estates sold to 52 donataries was 20 million thalers, the nominal one however 3.5 million thalers[^20], and the content of the register itself, where much lower figures figured. A doubt therefore arose whether von Held had not left yet other indications about the remaining estates, not listed in the register. Meanwhile as it turns out, the material printed by Zerboni-Held, then by Friedrich von Cölln[^21] in 1807 and further in the years 1849, 1861 and 1878 does not differ from the manuscript in the Berlin archive. Besides, we managed to find three other lists of estates, not connected with Held's register[^22]. The first two are official lists of the contribution of the donations for the department of Poznań, the third is a Polish list from the period of the Duchy of Warsaw for the whole of South Prussia, differing slightly from Held's list. These compilations are marked by small though interesting differences against the "Black Register" — thus Massów figures there (probably not Julius Anton the minister, partisan of Karl August Struensee, but the court marshal, who was to have received Kopanica in 1796[^23] and resell it to the Bloch brothers). Likewise absent from the "Black Register" are Colonel Massenbach[^24] and Tschammer, which shows on the one hand the incompleteness of Held's list, on the other — and this is more important — that these gaps are not considerable. Martin Philippson as early as 1882 mentioned another list, coming from Bucholtz-Alvensleben, which was to have been far more compromising for Prussia than the "Black Register". The same compilation Grünhagen then recognised as the basis for Zerboni and Held. We used the compilations of Grünhagen and Philippson supplementarily, to verify the data[^25].

The archival tax returns that we confronted with the "Black Register" come from 1797/8 and give a contribution the same as or very close to the sample data printed by Grünhagen. We set these returns together in the table that crowns our work. Lists I-II have a tax character, and hence it was necessary to compute the value of the estate from the height of the tax. That means that the net revenue of an estate, after the deduction of the costs of husbandry, makes 5% of the value of that estate. Thus Held assessed the value of councillor Beyer's estate at 160 000 thalers on the basis of an income of 8 000 thalers; General Blücher's estates yielded 6 000 thalers a year and were therefore to be worth 120 000 thalers. In a similar way the donations were assessed of Major Hünerbein, Prince Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, Marshal Keyserlingk and the Master of the Horse Krackwitz[^26]. This assumption was received both in the Commonwealth and in Prussia, and then also in the Duchy of Warsaw and still in the Congress Kingdom. It made an essential premise for tax settlements, and then for assessments of the mortgaged value of estates[^27]. The tax sources have however this undeniable advantage, that they are the result of the fiscal endeavours of the state, so there can be no question here of a deliberate (on the part of the state itself) deflation of the value of the estate, as was the case with the nominal price of the donation. We possess the most diversified material for the department of Poznań, where the nominal value of the estates was 1 025 250 thalers, while from the tax returns there follows a value of 1 638 000 thalers, that is a sum almost twice as high. Nevertheless it is at the same time a magnitude twice as low as the one given by Held on the title page of the "Black Register" — the "true" value of these estates, that is 3 million thalers[^28]. That true value of the estates was either the price of their sale, the sum of the lease, or the income from which Held in the way we have described computed the value of the estate. In this way, after counting the estates of all the departments, we obtain the sum of 7 036 160 thalers. It is an approximate magnitude, because in the case of some estates the author of the list entered the description "insignificant" or "value unknown". Such items are however barely four and cannot have a significant influence on the magnitude of the sum of the values of the estates given away. Somewhat greater is the sum of the values of the estates that follows from the Polish list, for it comes to 7 243 675 thalers. It should further be added that the "Black Register" has not 52 donataries, as its title page declares and as is usually said, but only 48; there are, however, 52 donations, because Treskow, Hohenlohe, Lüttichau and Wendessen appear twice — in different departments. The weak points of the "Black Register"

As can be seen, none of the items contained in the register comes anywhere near the value of 20 million thalers declared by Held. What is worse, the sum of the nominal values of the estates that follows from the compilation of Zerboni-Held likewise does not come to the declared 3.5 million thalers, but only 2 069 550 thalers. Held gives us yet one more indication, perhaps a decisive one, writing that the tax losses of the state from the estates given away come to 50 000 thalers[^29]. Converted into estate value this comes to over 4 million thalers — that is, as much as the difference between the nominal and the real value that follows from the "Black Register". This means that beyond the estates figuring in the register Held knew of no others. In the report of 25 November 1797, at the close therefore of the giveaway action, Karl Georg Hoym reported to the king that the estates given away yield 111 199 thalers of income, from which likewise (after conversion) the 20 million of value is infinitely far off[^30].

Our doubts however took their beginning from the setting together by Adelheid Simsch of two magnitudes: the value of the noble estates in South Prussia, which the mortgage register published by August Karl Holsche allowed to be assessed at 80 million thalers[^31], and the domainal estates sold off by the Prussian authorities, whose real value in Held's opinion was to have come to 20 million thalers[^32]. Such proportions are utterly improbable, because the area of all the domainal estates did not exceed 10% of the surface of the province[^33]. Meanwhile Hoym in his report to the king announced that the donations did not exceed 1/14 of the domains[^34], and yet as late as 1805 there were estates not included in them[^35]. The "conjuncture" of agriculture and speculation in estates

To answer this question it is necessary to take into account several phenomena occurring then not only in South Prussia. The period of the close of the eighteenth and the beginning of the nineteenth century, up to 1806, was marked by violent speculation in estates, being on the one hand a cause and on the other an effect of the rise in the prices of landed estates. That rise in prices however also followed from other premises. For South Prussia there had beyond doubt significance the renewed connection with the port of Gdańsk and the relatively favourable customs policy of the Prussian state[^44], whose promoter was in particular Minister of Finance Karl August Struensee (especially after 1797). Accompanying it was the generally favourable conjuncture in the sale of farm produce and a considerable rise in grain prices — which contemporaries already perceived as a tendency and precisely as a conjuncture, Frederick William III among them[^45]. Of essential significance was also the creation of the Prussian mortgage. It strengthened the value of estates, whose registers were put in order, and the buyer of an estate had sight of its encumbrance and real value. Finally, the violent revival in the trade in estates was brought about by the enormous credit granted by the Prussian public banks to the Polish nobility in South Prussia on the mortgage of landed estates[^46]. This credit, assessed at 27 million thalers, answered the annual budgetary receipts of the Prussian state, and could come into being only thanks to the fact that it came from the private deposits of Prussian citizens. The Prussian state was after all in a state of deep financial crisis[^47].

The failure of the giveaway of estates

Such a state of things was as far as possible from the assumptions of the whole operation. It had several aims: on the one hand the procuring of money for the stripped bare Prussian treasury[^49], on the other the economic elevation of the domains, which were in a bad state, by means of the private money of the Prussian nobility[^50].

No less essential was the introduction into Great Poland of the German national element. It made — as was often stressed — an outpost and support for the Prussian state among a Polish population hostile to Prussia. If the Prussian authorities complained of the low economic level of the seized lands, it must be remembered that they themselves laid hands to it in the mass trials of the insurgents after 1794. Confiscations of estates were admittedly a rarity, but the basic form of punishment — the money fine — was onerous. As many as 2000 persons were brought before the court. Janina Muszyńska gathered information on 567. As follows from the list she presents, the punishments were extraordinarily high — on average 200-1000 thalers[^51]. Already on 16 May 1794, in the edict on the ius incolatus[^52], a loophole was left which gave the king, in accord with the law, the possibility of granting landed estates to nobles from outside the province. As it says in the edict — "the concession of particular estates honoured by Us (that is, by the king — p. m.)"[^53]. In fact however Frederick William II waged to an equal degree a struggle against the unlawful buying-up of estates by Prussian speculators, encouraged by the favourable prices of estates in Poland — for in Prussia complaints were already being made in the eighties of speculation and the rise in the prices of estates. In connection with this there appeared on 12 VIII 1795 a "Publicandum" threatening confiscations against speculators who come to South Prussia and, contrary to the law on the ius incolatus, buy up landed estates, thereby winding up a speculative conjuncture, artificially driving up prices on the local market and so diminishing the purchasing power of the towns[^54]. The corrupt aspect of the giveaway of estates

Likewise the giveaway of estates itself could in no way answer the intended aims. It quickly became obvious that events had gone in quite the opposite direction, and the new owners — chosen not according to economic reason but according to Hoym's comradely preferences — treated the estates acquired at half price as a source of easy enrichment. Likewise Wilhelm Anton von Klewitz arrived in South Prussia together with Voss's taking of office, and was dismissed in 1795 by Hoym. Then his return came about after Hoym's fall, in 1798[^65].

The disclosure of the abuses connected with the giveaway of estates came at the highest levels of power. The assessment of the value of the estates given away

Ultimately, with reference to the estates given away the most cautious assessment would say that their value was from 4 to 7 million thalers, which lies between the value that would follow from the tax estimates and the maximum value — given by Held. Nearer the truth however is the sum of 4 million. For while we are aware of the deflation of the value of income for tax purposes, this value is nevertheless comparable with the assessments of the noble estates and with other data[^80]. That the noble estates however did not in their mass fall so far to speculation was pointed out for South Prussia already by Holsche[^81], which Danuta Rzepniewska confirmed. If Holsche warned against the new valuations of estates, which give 2-, 3-fold their real value, this did not relate to that degree to the mortgage valuations of a few years before[^82]. The above assessments are also confirmed by the "Black Register" and the structure of indebtedness from Prussian credits. Admittedly the indebtedness of the noble estates reached over 33% of their value, but at the same time credit covered only 14% of the owners. Then of 600 estates covered by credit the owner changed in about 70, and that often twice. Finally, if 10% of the debtors were Germans, the height of the loans granted them was 20% of the average indebtedness of an estate by way of Prussian credits[^83]. There now remained only the selling-off by persons put up to it. With the ordinances characterised above one must also connect the letter of Frederick William III to Minister von der Recke. Frederick William III regarded the trade in landed estates as an unfavourable phenomenon, but he regarded secret cessions of the leases of domainal estates as particularly harmful[^114]. The affair of the "Black Register" influenced negatively the attitude of the Polish nobility to the Prussian administration and notably undermined the authority of the Prussian state, the more so as it was not an isolated case of irregularity in the functioning of the Prussian administration in South Prussia.

Summary

As we have shown, the value of the estates given away can be assessed at 4 million reichstalers, whereas in consequence of the artificial raising of the prices of estates they were assessed at the time (1801) at 7 million reichstalers. The sum of 20 million reichstalers given on the title page of the "Black Register" must be recognised as wholly false. We then established that in the light of the "Black Register" itself the number of donataries was not 52, as had been given so far, but 48 persons; there were however 52 donations. From the tax returns there follows however a somewhat greater — though not by much — number of donations.

For the carrying out of the analysis of the list of estates given away and of the action of their giveaway it was necessary to take into account the wider context of the phenomena occurring: the so-called agricultural conjuncture, the rise in the prices of landed estates, speculation in estates, the consequences of the founding of the Prussian mortgage, land credit, the state of the Prussian state's finances, the state of the Prussian corps of officials in South Prussia at the turn of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and the clique struggles at the Prussian court.

In the light of our findings the aims of the giveaway action must be considered in a broad context. There appear here above all two aspects. Different were the official aims of the Prussian authorities, different those of the team of Hoym conducting the giveaway action directly. The official assumption of the giveaway action was the modernisation of the domainal estates with the private money of the Prussian junkers. The state gave up the domains, not possessing the means for their putting into husbandry. Thanks to such a decision it obtained, moreover, additional revenues from their sale, which, with the debt of the Prussian treasury at about 50 million thalers, was not without significance. Sincere too were the declarations that the Prussian nobility is to serve the Polish neighbours as a model of modern husbandry. Besides, the action was also to serve the rewarding of the officers and officials who had deserved well of the Prussian monarchy in a situation when the state possessed no other means.

Onto these premises there overlaid themselves clear political advantages, consisting in the creation in Great Poland of a group of German landowners, making up a support for Prussian rule in the seized Polish lands. The giveaway of estates was an undoubted manifestation of the policy of "subordination". It must however also be clearly marked that Prussian policy towards the Poles had also a clear aspect of "winning over" (at the time one spoke of "adoption"), of which the land credit for the Polish nobility was one manifestation at least. Both these ways of proceeding served, on the one hand, the assuring of the obedience of the nobility and control over it, on the other the formation among it of a group of partisans of Prussia, who were to consolidate Prussian rule in Great Poland. The successive partitions of the Polish lands named West Prussia, South Prussia and New East Prussia set the Prussian state before the necessity of absorbing new considerable areas. The taking of the decision on the staffing of the administration with German officials brought in from the Prussian provinces weighed fatally on the course of this process. In comparison with Poland before the partitions the Prussian corps was expanded, modern and educated. Yet for the realisation of the task set before it too few in number and subjected to negative selection. In consequence in the Prussian administration in South Prussia there came about various degenerations, unfavourable for the Prussian state itself.

[p. 54]

Prussian. One of the essential manifestations of this was the complete distortion of the intended aims in the course of the giveaway action. It changed into a corrupt affair, joined with a speculation in estates serving no one beyond the donataries themselves, in consequence of which the estates often returned into Polish hands. It is worth marking that — as the researches of Danuta Rzepniewska showed — the Prussian credit action in South Prussia ended in an analogous fiasco, its consequences being far more unfavourable for the Polish lands than the effects of the giveaway action. On the other hand the history of the Duchy of Warsaw shows that from the positive, that is effective, patterns of the Prussian organisation of the state the Poles were able to profit without prejudice towards the occupier.

Summary

The „Black Register" is a list, with accompanying comments, of property (primarily demesne) in Great Poland, granted by the Prussian authorities in 1796-1797 to Prussian officers, civil servants and landowners. An analysis of heretofore unexamined sources and an interprétation of the problem in a much wider context than was the custom up to now, enabled the author to demonstrate that the estâtes were worth not 20 million rtl., as was claimed in heretofore studies, but 4-7 million rtl., as a result of the sudden rise in land prices in Prussia. It was also found that 48 persons received 52 landed estâtes.

The intention of the campaign (the estâtes were sold but at prices considerably below their true worth) was the modernisation of state property by resorting to the private funds of Prussian Citizens. The Hohenzollern state resolved to take this step owing to great debts produced by the war against France and the 1794 insurrection; this décision created sources for additional funds received from the sale of the landed estâtes. It was declared that the Prussian nobility would serve as a model for management; another significant role was played by the création in Great Poland of a group of German landowners who would support Prussian rule. The implementation of the thus devised opération led to its total déformation. A group of officials working for Karl Georg Hoym, supervising South Prussia, became engaged in a corruption scandal, connected with spéculative trade in the estâtes by their new owners and sizable crédits in return for mortgaged land. Another detrimental factor was the low level of the Prussian civil service in Great Poland. Ultimately, despite the legal protecti ve measures created by the central authorities, and attempts at disclosing the misuse, the estâtes frequently returned to Polish owners and the Prussian campaign failed.

(transl. A. Rodzińska-Chojnowska)
