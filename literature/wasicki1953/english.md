JAN WĄSICKI

GERMAN COLONISATION IN THE PERIOD OF SOUTH PRUSSIA 1793 – 1806

1. Rural colonisation.

The question of the German colonisation of the Polish lands of the second and third Prussian partitions has not, to this day, been exhaustively studied. The works of bourgeois German historians, and in particular the work of Max Beheim-Schwarzbach[^1], which treats the whole of the Hohenzollerns' colonising activity and is full of praise and adulation for their undertakings in this direction, rest only in small part on archival material and mainly use earlier and fragmentary studies. In this thoroughly tendentious work, moreover, all those studies were rejected that treated critically the colonising action carried out by the Prussian kings. To colonisation in the period of South Prussia Beheim-Schwarzbach devoted only a very short chapter. Its incompleteness and lack of criticism may be shown by the fact that the author relied mainly on the work of Holsche[^2], without presenting the question as a whole. A critical assessment of Schwarzbach's work was already given by Johannes Ziekursch, who showed, on the example of the colonisation of Silesia carried out in the time of Frederick the Great, that even the scanty archival materials the author used uncritically are not only fragmentary but also unreliable[^3].

Of the more recent works of German historians that deal only incidentally with the problem of colonisation in South Prussia, mention should be made of the works of August Müller[^4] and Antoni Pytlak[^5]. Unlike Schwarzbach's work, both authors used archival materials, coming mainly from the Secret State Archive in Berlin and from the Archive of the Treasury of the Kingdom of Poland in Warsaw. The materials contained in these works make it possible, in broad approximation, to establish the number of colonies founded and the places where they were founded, which is especially valuable when there is no possibility of using the collections of those archives directly. Of the source publications for this period, the publications from the secret state archive edited by Stadelmann[^6] and Prümers[^7] deserve attention.

Although this last author deals in principle with Frederician colonisation, he also presents very briefly its course in the years 1793—1806 in South Prussia. Finally, the most recent study of the question is the book of Z. Kaczmarczyk[^10]. I do not list here other, smaller contributory works scattered through various periodicals, in particular in the Deutsche Wissenschaftliche Zeitschrift f. Polen.

The source basis of the present article is the materials in the Voivodeship State Archive in Poznań, in the section S. P. Z. Although on their basis an exhaustive picture of the principles and course of colonisation in the whole province of South Prussia cannot be given, because of a certain fragmentariness of the material, the action carried on in the years 1793—1806 can nevertheless be reconstructed approximately, especially with regard to the department of the Poznań chamber.

Before proceeding to discuss the colonisation carried on in South Prussia by the absolute Prussian monarchs, it must be stressed that it was nothing new, unknown until then in this former province of the nobles' Commonwealth; on the contrary, it was only a further prolongation of the action carried on for a long time by the Polish feudal lords. The "Olęder" settlements that arose in large numbers in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, founded mostly on uncultivated land that needed clearing and draining, were dictated by considerations of an economic nature. The private initiative of the Polish feudal lords until then was, after the taking of Greater Poland, taken over and carried on, however, by the Prussian authorities.

Immediately after the taking of the Polish lands in the second partition, Frederick William II thought of colonising his new acquisitions with settlers brought from the Reich. He wrote on this matter to the president of the Poznań chamber, Buggenhagen, that he was very anxious to increase the population of the newly taken province and to raise the standard of agriculture in South Prussia[^11]. It would follow from these royal plans that colonisation was to be undertaken mainly for economic and population reasons. Undoubtedly it is from these two aspects that the colonising aims of the Hohenzollerns in the period of South Prussia should be viewed. For the colonisation carried out cannot be identified — as was done until recently — with an action of Germanisation, all the more so since in the times of the nobles' Commonwealth settlers, mostly German, were drawn to these lands. T. Ladenberger also drew attention to this in his work "On Frederician colonisation in Silesia", discussing both the opinions of Polish and German historians on the matter and the course and results of the colonisation of that period. Bourgeois German historians presented the colonising action of the Prussian kings, both in Silesia and in Pomerania, as a "work of culture", and regarded the colonists as "bearers of culture". T. Ladenberger, showing the real aim of colonisation, states at the same time, and rightly, that "colonisation served above all the royal treasury and the great estates"[^12], and that its cultural effects were downright lamentable.

The situation in South Prussia took a similar shape, although in this period an attempt was made, at least to some degree, to avoid the mistakes of Frederician colonisation. Colonies continued to be founded mainly on uncultivated land requiring clearing or draining. A considerable part of the settlers who arrived, as I shall show below, were settled on the royal domains, on the manor farms of private feudal lords and in the forests as hired labourers. Often two or three families were settled among the Polish population. The motive justifying colonisation was thus the wish to bring into use land not cultivated until then and to increase the number of hands for work on the domains and the manor farms. In these cases it is therefore very doubtful whether this poorest stratum of the rural population, employed as hired labourers on the manor farms, could fulfil that "cultural mission" so loudly proclaimed by German historians. Nor was there a better opinion of the other categories of colonists and of their knowledge of farming, even among contemporaries. Holsche himself, who had the best knowledge of contemporary conditions, states that the Württembergers, who settled in the greatest numbers in South Prussia, were bad farmers and that the whole action did not pay at all[^12a]. The settlers who arrived, and especially those who had been given small pieces of land, farming in hard conditions, left South Prussia when the prospect opened before them of obtaining better conditions of settlement in another country. It must be stated, however, that those colonies,

where a larger number of settlers were settled, and on land suitable for immediate cultivation, being given moreover considerable areas of land from 1 to 3 łany (Hufen), with hired labourers settled beside them, had assured prospects of development and therefore lasted long. For colonisation in the period of South Prussia was carried on on principles different from those of Frederician colonisation. Large farms, relying on the work of hired labourers, gave a guarantee of survival and development, all the more so since, as we shall see, the legal position of the colonist, who paid only a rent and was not bound by any other ties of feudal dependence, differed from the conditions prevailing at that time both on the nobles' manor farms and on the royal domains.

The Germanising factor was not the most important in colonisation in this period. For not only colonists from the Reich were settled here, but also colonists from Bohemia, inhabitants of South Prussia, and in New East Prussia even Tatars and Lithuanian peasants. This does not mean, however, that the Hohenzollerns did not try at this time to Germanise the Polish nobility. They did so through the administrative and judicial offices, the schools, and by drawing the nobility into military and civil service.

Frederick William II in his first instruction expressly warned that the colonising action should not cause a loss of population from the provinces of the Prussian state. It followed clearly from the royal order, therefore, that above all newcomers from the German Reich were to be recruited and settled as colonists. To encourage colonists to settle in the new lands certain concessions and financial help from the state were usually provided, and in addition exemption from military service. This last point was especially important, since the newcomers were well aware that in the militaristic Prussian state the duty of military service would be their heaviest burden. Had permission been given for colonists from the provinces in the hereditary possession of the Hohenzollerns to settle in South Prussia, there would undoubtedly have been too many willing to improve their conditions of life and to obtain the benefits due in such cases. This outflow of people, however, mainly peasants, on whom rested the whole burden of tax obligations and military service, would for some time have been a great loss for the Prussian state, and so their settling was forbidden from the very start.

From the exchange of letters between minister Voss and the Prussian envoy in Warsaw, Buchholz, it can be established that Buchholz put forward a proposal to appoint a special official to whom colonisation matters would be entrusted. These tasks were in the end entrusted, however, to the war and domains chambers, and minister Voss for his part issued an order that settlers arriving in future in the administrative districts of the chambers be given proper care and suitable help in settling[^13].

It might seem from the minister's first orders that Voss would at once set about putting the royal commands into effect; this, however, was not so. As we shall see further, Voss and the other ministers took an unwilling attitude to the whole action and set about putting it into effect only on the king's express order.

At the very outset Voss rejected the offer of the notary Hassold of Hamburg, made less than a year after the taking of the province, who wished to undertake the recruiting of colonists for South Prussia[^14]. The minister gave as the ground for his position the fact that no preparations had been made in this province to begin a colonising action. Such preparations would necessarily take a long time, to make suitable land ready for settling the arriving settlers. Besides, he considered the method of recruiting unsuitable in this case, because already now, and certainly in future, there would be many willing, chiefly from Mecklenburg, Lüneburg and other provinces of the Reich, who would wish to settle in the new province. In this particular case, then, Voss wished to depart from the method of obtaining colonists by recruiting, as practised by Frederick II.

Minister Voss's unfavourable attitude in this matter must also have been affected by the fact that in none of the royal commands was there the slightest mention of the amount of the fund provided for the colonising action, and without a fairly large sum of money set aside for the purpose the whole action could not have the slightest chance of success. Wishing to begin the colonising action, Frederick William approved a project laid before him in July 1793, under which the nobility receiving the South Prussian indigenat, necessary for acquiring estates in this province, was obliged, in proportion to the value of the estates acquired, to settle a certain number of colonists (one family for every 2000 thalers), but it is not known whether this project was put into effect[^15]. The royal edict issued on the indigenat[^16] says nothing of this condition. Since, however, the grant of the indigenat depended solely on the king, the project of settling colonists on the occasion of the acquisition of estates in South Prussia by the Prussian nobility might have had a chance of success, and the king would still make attempts to carry it out in 1804. Until then, judging from the king's enquiries addressed to minister Voss, the royal commands were not strictly observed.

From these initial plans it is clear that according to the king's intentions the colonising action was to go two ways: one carried on by the state, the other by the private purchasers of estates in the new province, which in fact happened later.

Minister Voss's successor, the Silesian minister Hoym, also did not begin the colonising action, although after the suppression of the Kościuszko Rising the starosties and church estates were confiscated and incorporated into the royal domains. Hoym did not comply at all with the earlier royal directions on settling colonists on private estates and leased domains. For under his rule, as K. Marx writes: "... Knights, adventurers, favourites of the royal mistresses, creatures of the ministers, accomplices whose mouths it was desired to shut, were endowed in great numbers with the larger and richer estates in the plundered country... It may therefore be said that those endowed with these estates must have taken an unwilling and even downright hostile attitude to the royal orders requiring colonists to be settled on private estates, and this even where the royal order did not concern estates given away for "services".

For those who received these estates treated them only as an object of further trade, naturally at a considerable profit[^18]. It must have mattered to them in such a situation that those buying estates from them should not be burdened with additional colonisation obligations towards the state, since these rather onerous and costly conditions might, if not deter buyers, at any rate cause considerable difficulty in selling them.

Besides, the Silesian minister Hoym had already formed an unfavourable judgement of the results of this action as carried on in Silesia in the time of Frederick II. As K. Zimmermann reports, in a memorandum presented to Frederick William II Hoym clearly stressed that bringing settlers from foreign lands does not give the expected results. In his opinion,

> "it is not the first duty of the steward of the state to see that the population increases as far as possible, but to see to its permanent maintenance and to settle it so that it brings the state the greatest benefit. Neither of these aims has been fully attained by settling foreign colonists in compact villages. They usually take advantage of the state's benefits, then move away, and they cannot be blamed for this, since with a disproportionately large number of foreigners they often had to be assigned places where earning a living was very difficult for them. In other places they would have taken from the natives their permanent and secure livelihood and the fruits of their thrift. Encouraging and settling new trades by granting them certain concessions is a salutary thing, and for this there will be plenty ready to settle among the Silesians"[^19].

The Silesian minister therefore tried to persuade Frederick William II that the costs of bringing in foreign settlers were too great, both for the state and for the owners of manor farms. Settling the local population could give better results. Many contemporaries, too, shared minister Hoym's view on this matter. Minister Schulenburg-Kehnert, stressing in his remarks on South Prussia the growing number of the population, expressed the view that the colonising action ought to be limited. Efforts should rather be made to improve the condition of the local population and to give it the possibility of acquiring land. This referred mainly to the "Olęders", since the minister had come into contact with this category of the population on his journey through the new Prussian province. They had a little capital, which by their own account they were eating up, because they had no possibility of buying a suitable farm. These people deserve — in the minister's opinion — special support, because they need either no help or very little help from the state. Moreover they understand farming well in local conditions and can serve as a model for the Polish population[^20].

Minister Struensee also spoke out against bringing in colonists from other lands, maintaining moreover that daily experience teaches that colonists brought from abroad disappoint, while the treasury is burdened with great costs[^21]. For these reasons, therefore, minister Hoym observed especially carefully the royal prohibition forbidding the settlement in South Prussia of serf peasants from the provinces of the Hohenzollern state. For from the reports of the chambers it appeared that on some nobles' estates in the New Mark there were serf peasants who wished themselves, and persuaded others, to settle in South Prussia as colonists, without obtaining the consent of their lord. The minister drew the chambers' attention to such cases of undesirable and forbidden inflow of settlers from the Prussian provinces. He therefore ordered them to check carefully whether the newcomers were not "in nexu subditela", and whether they had a release from the authorities of the canton[^22].

It seems, however, that such cases must have occurred more often, since a year after the incidents mentioned the order previously issued to the chambers was repeated[^23]. It may be supposed, however, from the later measures of the chambers and the General Directory, that the principle that the king's subjects could not settle in the new Prussian province was applied rigorously only to serf peasants.

Thus during the reign of Frederick William II and in the period of minister Hoym's administration in principle no steps were taken to begin a colonising action on a larger scale. This was also affected by the financial situation of the Prussian state, which, exhausted by the expenditure of the war then being waged with revolutionary France, could not set aside for settlement

The fact that the Tatar population and also Lithuanian peasants were settled in this province may best show that Germanising factors did not come into play here, but, as we have already stressed, population and fiscal factors, and in the settling of the Tatars also military ones.

Besides the Tatars and Lithuanians, colonists coming mainly from the Prussian provinces were settled here at this time, which in principle did not agree with Frederick William's intentions.

A certain revival of the colonising action can be observed only with the accession of Frederick William III and the end of the war with France. At this time, too, there was a change in the post of the administrator of the affairs of the province of South Prussia. In place of minister Hoym, one of its first co-organisers, minister Voss, was appointed again.

At the very outset of his reign Frederick William III ordered the General Directory to draw up a plan of colonisation for the individual provinces and to state what had been done in this regard until then. Unfortunately we have no information on what the plan drawn up by Voss for South Prussia looked like. Presumably the minister, while awaiting the king's remarks on and acceptance of the colonisation plan, issued an instruction to the chambers under him in which he declared expressly that this action had not yet begun[^25]. For these reasons he ordered those applying and expressing the wish to settle in South Prussia, both from the Prussian provinces and from other parts of the Reich, to wait until the announcement of the date when the admission of new settlers would begin.

Soon, however, minister Voss together with the provincial authorities was placed in a position of compulsion. In the summer of 1798 the first reports reached Berlin of a larger number of applications from colonists, mainly from Württemberg and Baden[^26]. Württemberg had already, during Frederician colonisation, been a region from which a large number of settlers were recruited. The reason for this was the considerable density of population of this part of Germany; besides, strong propaganda was carried on there, as will be discussed below. In Berlin, however, an increased inflow of colonists was expected at this time mainly from the lands lying on the left bank of the Rhine, because of the occupation of this part of Germany by French troops. Everything therefore pointed to the possibility of beginning the colonising action on a broad scale, otherwise, that is, than had been the case under the previous ruler[^27]. A great hindrance in carrying on the whole action was the lack of any guidelines on the principles of admitting colonists and the manner of carrying out settlement. Frederick William III in a letter to minister Voss ordered that in proceedings on colonisation the older models from the time of Frederick II be followed[^35]. In a letter of March 1799 minister Voss, informing the chambers of the arrival in their administrative districts of 19 families of colonists, sent at the same time, for the first time, more precise instructions on the manner of settling them[^37]. The chambers were to try to have suitable pastures granted to the colonists, or the right to graze cattle in the forests for a certain fee. Colonists were released for two years from the obligation of supplying forage. They were to be supplied with timber for building their dwelling houses and farm buildings. The colonists and their sons, and the persons who came with them, were exempted from the duty of military service. The settlers were personally free; they received the land in perpetual lease. In 1799, carrying out the royal command to conduct the colonising action on a broader scale, by recruiting settlers from the Reich more vigorously than until then, ministers Voss and Schrötter, and especially the latter, set about acting more energetically. Minister Schrötter thought it fitting to send a special commissioner to southern Germany to carry on recruiting among the population there. He intended, after some time, to use the settlers thus won for further recruiting in the Reich. Commissioner Weiss, chosen by minister Schrötter for this task, set out in May of that year for Württemberg, Baden, Hesse-Nassau and the Palatinate, in order to attract "very sturdy and well-to-do colonists". In the principality of Nassau Weiss found a fairly favourable reception from the authorities there, and many willing to settle in the new provinces, who did not, however, possess the means required for this[^39]. He did not fare so well in Württemberg, because of the hostile attitude at that time of the authorities there to Frederick William III's colonising undertakings. Besides, the imperial armies withdrawing from Switzerland did not allow the action begun by Weiss to be carried on further. On his second stay in Nassau he could already see that the unfavourable reception of the first settlers from this province in South Prussia, who had returned to Nassau, had discouraged others from leaving. In view of the king's position, aiming at an extension of the colonising action, the General Directory issued an instruction to the chambers explaining what land was to be set aside for colonisation. First among these were reckoned: fallow, waste land, pastures, forest land[^44], small manor farms unsuitable for larger-scale farming, abandoned peasant farms, and finally unused land near the towns, which could be set aside without harm to the manor farms for settling cottagers and gardeners.

As to the first point, the instruction explained that for purposes of colonisation those lands could be taken about which there were no disputed questions. Where there were boundary disputes, colonies could not be founded only in those places that were in dispute, with the proviso that if the chambers lost the case the rent paid by the colonists would be handed over to the owner of the land on which the colony had been founded. It may give us pause that small manor farms, that is cultivated land, were also set aside for founding colonies, which really did not agree with the fundamental intentions of the colonisation carried on in South Prussia. It was stressed clearly, however, that only those manor farms could be set aside for this purpose which, because of their excessive distance from larger economic units, were unsuitable for growing grain or rearing cattle on a suitable scale.

Minister Voss was well aware that the settlers arriving from the Reich would be reluctant to settle on abandoned peasant farms among a population foreign to them. Besides, there might arise the danger that the colonists, having received a farm of this kind, would have to perform the manorial services to which the rest of the population living in the village was bound. For the earlier instructions stressed clearly that the colonists received the land in perpetual lease, for which they paid only a rent. Seeing this state of affairs, the General Directory gave an instruction that abandoned peasant farms were to be occupied by the local population, or the abandoned land joined to other peasant farms to enlarge them. It was also possible — according to the instruction — to move peasants from farms lying next to the manor farms to the abandoned farms, and to fill their places with arriving colonists classed as cottagers and smallholders, or else to incorporate the land into the manor farms. In this way an attempt was made on the one hand to create larger peasant farms and on the other to assure the royal domains of a labour force for which the main, indeed the only, means of support would be work on the manor farm.

Similar principles were to be applied in founding colonies on land near the towns. The chambers were to see to the building of rented houses in which artisans, day labourers and Lassbauer (lassyci) would find lodging.

This care to settle free hired labourers on the one hand, and then to create large farms for colonists, bore witness to the far-reaching changes already under way in rural conditions. The feudal economy had at this time begun to outlive itself and slowly, first on the royal estates and then on the nobles' manor farms, to take on the forms of a capitalist economy. The transition to new forms of cultivation, the clearing of forests and the draining of marshes required the settling of a large number of free cottagers, gardeners and smallholders, compelled to sell their labour to the owners of manor farms. And so, although in principle there was constant talk of settling colonist-farmers, at the same time especially many hired labourers were settled, so needed on the manor farms. Colonists were settled mainly on the royal domains, where progressive forms of farming were being introduced, but the Prussian government also influenced the South Prussian nobility to introduce new forms of farming on their manor farms, increasing the areas

[p. 150]

of the manor farms by sowing the fallows, laying out meadows, growing industrial crops. They were also encouraged to abolish common pastures, increase sheep-farming and industrialise the manor farms. Rather generally and cautiously, but at any rate it was stressed, that such changes ought to bring with them "a change in the relations between lords and peasants". These principles were to be put into practice, for example, by the Economic Society of Międzyrzecz, founded in 1802, and the Prussian authorities reckoned with the possibility of extending the network of similar societies over the whole area of South Prussia[^45]. Beside the towns, colonies of artisans and day labourers were to arise, needed for work in the productive enterprises developing in this period and relying on free hired labour. It cannot be said that particularly numerous industrial enterprises arose in South Prussia in this period; on the contrary, a gradual decline of those existing until then can be observed, as a result of the competition of other Prussian provinces where manufacture was highly developed, as for example in Silesia; the guidelines of the General Directory, however, amounted only to general principles of colonisation, without taking account in such cases of particular local considerations.

Unlike the earlier instructions issued to the chambers, this one ordered the officials to give the arriving settlers proper care[^46]. When, however, it happened that arriving colonists did not wish to settle in the place set aside for them, as was the case with 12 Württemberg families, minister Voss ordered the chambers to leave them to their own fate, without troubling about them further[^47].

They received 5 łany of land, and besides timber for building, 3 to 6 free years, an interest-free loan of 1400 thal., repayable, however, during the free years, and finally exemption from the cantons.

The sixth and last category of settlers was formed by those for whom an allotment of land of 6 łany was provided. The required means amounted to from 4000 Reichsgeld to 5000 Reichsgeld, or 2166 thal. 16 gr. to 2708 thal. 8 gr. The help provided for them by the state was of the same amount and kind as for the previous group of settlers.

As follows from this, then, the instruction of 1802 differed fairly considerably from the one issued earlier. Financial help was given in principle according to the category of settlers, which again was closely bound up with the amount of land received. It must be admitted that the first category, to which a fairly considerable number of newcomers was assigned — judging from the chamber's reports and the General Directory's interventions in these matters — nevertheless received fairly substantial help. Colonists assigned to the second and even the third group did not, it is true, receive a non-repayable loan for a fixed period, which was in any case closely bound up with the areas of land received, but for them too the benefits provided were fairly considerable.

From the above instruction it follows fairly clearly that the General Directory was concerned on the one hand with creating relatively large rural farms, of 1 to 6 łany, and on the other with supplying hired labour for work on the manor farms.

After the occupation of the left bank of the Rhine by the revolutionary troops of France the inflow of colonists from those lands increased fairly considerably. There were relatively many colonists from the Hohenzollern provinces, despite the difficulties made at first over settling them. The fundamental aim of Frederician colonisation was to increase the population of the country; the question of the newcomers' usefulness in the province did not play the most important part here. For these reasons the element arriving here had the worst possible reputation, both professionally and morally. In point of farming technique the newcomers stood no higher than the Polish population. Hence the opinions of bourgeois German historians "on the cultural mission of the colonists" remain empty phrases. In the period of colonisation in South Prussia, as we have been able to establish, newcomers wishing to settle on the land were required to have professional training in farming and to possess suitable stock. The colonists were divided into categories according to property criteria, and the state's help was made to depend on the amount of the means brought in.

The colonists of the period of Frederick II's colonising action were in principle allotted small areas of land, so that after the expiry of the free years, unable to live on it under relatively heavy tax obligations, they fell into poverty. The Büdner could earn a living in West Prussia in the Netze district only while fortification works, the building of canals and the like lasted; after these works were finished they no longer had any possibility of supporting themselves by wage work. In South Prussia an attempt was made to create fairly large farms, from at least 20 morgen to 6 łany, which had a chance of surviving and developing further. Day labourers, on the other hand, were settled beside the royal domains, forests, nobles' manor farms and larger colonies or near towns, in order to assure them steady wage work, which was to be the basis of their livelihood. The changes in agriculture, the growing industrialisation of the manor farm, required an increase in the number of hands for work, and that is why the largest number of colonists of this category was settled in South Prussia.

The costs of setting up colonies in the period of South Prussia rose by half compared with the costs of Frederician times, for an attempt was made to assure the colonists better conditions of life. Yet the colonisation of the years 1793—1803 too was a failure. The newcomers were allotted waste land or land requiring clearing or draining, which undoubtedly put off many of them, who had expected to receive in this province land already prepared for cultivation, and more of it. For this reason a large number of colonists either returned to the Reich or left for Russia, where the conditions of settlement were more favourable.

The colonising action was also carried on by private landowners. On the possessions of the Prince of Orange preparations for settling colonists were already being made in 1799, and Frederick William III assigned 50,000 thalers for this purpose[^81].

It is necessary, however briefly, to look more closely at the colonising activity of the Prince of Orange, since this will let us see how this action was carried on on private estates. William Frederick (later King of the Netherlands), Prince of Orange, married to the eldest daughter of Frederick William II, King of Prussia, Frederica Louisa Wilhelmina, acquired in South Prussia the Wydzim estates near Wolsztyn, then the Stęszew estates, consisting of the town of Stęszew and the villages and manor farms of Chmielniki, Witobel, Dembno, Kremplewo, Tomice and Mirosławki. In the same year he also acquired the Racot estates near Kościan with the villages and manor farms of Witkówki, Spytkówki, Darnowo, Lubosk, Stonin and Wyskoc, and the Czeszewo estates in the district of Pyzdry with the villages and manor farms of Mikieszewo, Chlebowo, Radzitówko and Orzechowo. His estates thus stretched from Wolsztyn to the neighbourhood of Kościan and further eastward along the Warta to the district of Pyzdry, grouping themselves finally around Stęszew.

The negotiations for the purchase of the estates were still going on when the prince had the conditions announced in the principality of Nassau that colonists settling on the prince's estates in South Prussia were to receive. Farmers, artisans and day labourers alike were to be accepted.

The political situation at the time favoured the recruiting of colonists, and so many were found willing to go to the new Prussian province, although, as we shall see, the conditions of settlement on the Prince of Orange's estates proved later to be harder than on the royal estates. On 24 January 1799 the number of volunteers for leaving had already reached 150 families, that is about 600 persons[^83]. The prince obtained from Frederick William the sum of money needed for the colonising action and the exemption of the colonists from state burdens, as had been provided for the settlers on the royal domains. 2. Urban colonisation

Parallel with the conduct of rural colonisation, efforts were made to settle colonists in the towns. From the first instruction, however, it appeared clearly that what was meant was not to attract specialists for the further development of the manufacturing industry existing in Greater Poland, but only specialists in building, such as bricklayers, carpenters and the like. This by no means means that the settling of other arriving artisans was abandoned, but this came about when a fairly considerable number of newcomers from the left bank of the Rhine flowed into South Prussia.

Already in 1793, immediately after the entry of the Prussian troops, when the action of organising the new new province was in full swing, the chamber in Piotrków came forward with a proposal to settle in the towns colonists from the branch of crafts mentioned above, the lack of whom was particularly felt at that time[^91].

In the order issued on 16 January 1794[^92] the General Directory promised help and financial support for colonists settled in the towns. This help depended on whether the colonist came from outside the frontiers of the Prussian kingdom or from the Hohenzollern provinces, and finally on whether he settled in royal or private towns. In principle colonists could settle only in those towns where there was a lack of specialists in the branch of craft the colonist practised. According to the instruction, newcomers from outside the Prussian state received: 1) free citizenship, 2) a free licence to carry on a craft, or the right of mastership, 3) a three-year rebate of excise, 4) six years' exemption from the service, that is from payments for the upkeep of the night watchman and the fire-fighting equipment and other town payments and personal burdens such as watch duty and the like, 5) the colonist, together with his sons and the journeymen coming with him, was exempted from the duty of military service. Like the colonists settled in the villages, the town colonists too received travel money at the rate of 2 groschen a head for each mile. Besides, they were granted a special allowance for the transport of their movable property. The state's financial help also took the form of paying the yearly rent of their lodging and partly reimbursing the costs of setting up the workshop and buying tools that could not be brought with them. The order stresses once again that this help is intended exclusively for colonists settling in towns where there is a lack of this category of artisans. They were not forbidden to settle in other towns, but in such cases the state's financial help fell away. They were granted as a benefit only free citizenship, a licence or the right of mastership, and exemption from the cantons. Still greater restrictions were applied to artisans coming from within the Prussian state. They received, it is true, the concessions listed above, but were not exempt from the cantons.

The General Directory, agreeing to the proposals of the South Prussian chambers, recommended that settlers be brought above all to the seats of the provincial colleges, that is Poznań and Piotrków. Newcomers were promised greater financial help in the form of a special payment of 10—15 thalers a year. This applied only to masters and journeymen practising their trade independently.

As to private towns, the General Directory considered that their owners ought in their own interest to strive to settle artisans in them and to build on the empty plots. Colonists in these towns were to be granted the right to build freely on a plot, three years' exemption from all land taxes and obligations to the lord of the town, and finally a year's exemption from paying for lodging. Special credits were to be set aside for building houses.

Such were the first instructions on settling colonists in the towns sent by the General Directory to the chambers in Poznań and Piotrków.

The inflow of settlers to the towns was relatively small compared with the inflow of rural colonists. Like the rural settlers, newcomers to the towns met with many obstacles from the chambers. There were also cases where,

The question was settled in such a way that until separate regulations for the guilds were issued on this matter, they would have to grant the right of mastership to those of the newcomers who had held it before. If the applicant did not hold such a right, then all the costs connected with it were to be covered by the chamber from the general fund set aside for colonisation. From the numerous enquiries addressed by the chambers to the General Directory we may conclude that among the newcomers there were some who had left this province still in the time of the nobles' Commonwealth and now wished to return to it. In such cases all the concessions provided for colonists were due to them. If, however, they had left these lands after the partition and now wished to return again, they lost all right to the benefit[^94].

A greater intensity of urban colonisation came during the reign of Frederick William III. More and more often the conditions and concessions for colonists provided by the General Directory's order of January 1794 were made public. In the Gazeta Południowo-Pruska the full text issued by the chamber in Kalisz was published. It would follow from this that the aim now was to attract colonists to the towns of that department. For earlier Poznań and Piotrków had been the object of the central authorities' special care. This did not exclude, however, the fact that colonists settled in other towns, as for example the weavers in Babimost[^95].

Urban colonisation did not make very great progress, the more so since under minister Voss's administration, in accordance with his recommendations, an attempt was made to administer the fund set aside for colonisation very economically. For it was thought that an artisan would find good earnings in his new place. For this reason the time during which the chambers were to pay for the colonist's lodging was shortened[^96]. The principle was strictly observed that in the South Prussian towns only those artisans should be settled, and in such numbers, as were indispensably needed by the inhabitants.

When a larger number of artisans than needed wished to settle in them, the help due to them was naturally reduced[^97].

In August 1799 Voss informed the South Prussian chambers that many artisans from the Prussian provinces would try to settle in the new province to improve their condition of life. This did not agree with the real settlement intentions of the General Directory, the more so since these artisans did not possess sufficient means to set up a workshop. The minister therefore considered that the number of this category of colonists should be limited, since the partial financial help granted them by the state would in any case not give the expected results. Those colonists, however, deserved special support who possessed some means, thereby giving a guarantee that after settling they would be able to maintain the workshop they founded by themselves without the state's help. At first the amount of means a colonist should possess to receive the state's financial help was not exactly fixed. Only in 1802 was the amount of means a colonist must possess to obtain permission to settle fixed at 200 thalers. Only this category of persons, then, was entitled to the state's financial help needed for setting up a workshop. All the other colonists were entitled to the benefits listed in the order of 1794, but without financial help. They received a free licence and the right of mastership only after passing the appropriate examination.

As follows from the facts cited above, then, the Prussian authorities did not attach great importance to urban colonisation. This was undoubtedly bound up with the whole policy of the Prussian authorities towards the towns, industry and crafts in the period of South Prussia. The strong development of manufacturing industry in the other Prussian provinces, and especially in Silesia, did not allow the Prussian authorities to settle specialists of this branch in South Prussia, so as not to create dangerous competition for the Prussian provinces. For these reasons, too, urban colonisation was confined to only a small number of building craftsmen. Only the increased inflow of settlers from the lands on the left bank of the Rhine brought about the settlement here of a certain number of weavers, chiefly in Dąbie on the Ner[^101]. Unfortunately we cannot give the exact total number of town colonists who arrived and settled in South Prussia. In Voss's reports, tables and lists there was not the slightest mention of it. It must therefore be thought that their number was negligible, since the main stress was laid on rural colonisation.
