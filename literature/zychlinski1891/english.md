Count **Stanisław** Adam (11th generation), *colonel of the former Polish army, adjutant of Prince Józef Poniatowski, knight of the cross Virtuti militari,* heir of Trąbczyn, Wieniec, Brzeź, Dobielewo, Jaronówek, Pawłowo, Dzwonowo, Kolno, Berenbusz and Kamionna, born 24 December 1780, died in Berlin on 22 April 1845. From there his body was taken to the burial chapel of the Miączyńskis at Trąbczyn and buried there. As a young man he joined the ranks of Napoleon's armies, in the guard of the Grand Duke of Berg and Cleves, later king of the Two

[p. 220]

Sicilies; after which, "in view of his ability", he was appointed by Prince Józef Poniatowski on 2 September supernumerary captain and transferred to the general staff of the army of the Duchy of Warsaw. Having distinguished himself in that post in leading a squadron of the vanguard, he was raised by order of the minister of war of 24 November 1809 to chief of squadron with the rank of lieutenant-colonel and adjutant at the side of Prince Józef. On the occasion of this appointment, confirmed on 11 December of that year by the Duke of Warsaw, King Frederick Augustus of Saxony, and of the disbanding of the so-called "Guides" company, the Prince issued the following [order of the day], most flattering to Stanisław Miączyński:

The Allied Army.

*Order of the day.* 27 November 1809.

At Headquarters in Kraków.

When the Polish army, on entering Galicia, had as yet freed only a small part of it, the young men of Galicia, crowding beneath their country's standards, already showed sufficiently what spirit moves the inhabitants of this Polish land.

"The hope thus raised was disappointed in nothing; a common striving became the mark of true love of the Fatherland, and the company of Guides that was formed gave, by the praise of all, by its devotion to the good cause of our countrymen, many proofs under my eye that the young men who made it up lacked only the occasion to show themselves worthy of their brothers.

"Since the return of peace does not allow me to employ it longer in this form, it is a pleasure to me, by placing it in the army of the line, to increase there the number of good officers; and from the way of thinking of those who made up the company of Guides one may confidently promise oneself that by zeal in service and exemplary conduct they will always uphold the honour of the corps from which they come.

"To Captain Miączyński, who has commanded the company of Guides until now, *I can give no better proof of the satisfaction he has earned than by returning him to my side as Adjutant, with the rank of lieutenant-colonel.*

(signed) **Józef Prince Poniatowski.**

"A true copy of the Original.

Brigadier-General

Chief of the General Staff

*Fiszer.*"

Worn out [printed "Stérauy", for Stérany] by the hardships of the Moscow campaign, Miączyński asked to be discharged. In answer he received the following letter from Prince Józef:

"Warsaw, 13 January 1812.

"Having received the Note in which you, Lieutenant-Colonel, ask to be released from the duties of the service, I have not failed to lay this wish before His Royal Majesty.

"In informing you of this, *I hold it my duty to declare to you that, having had occasion during the time you were with me to be convinced of your zeal in fulfilling your duties, I am glad to take this opportunity of doing you the justice owed you in this respect, as I regret that in you I lose a subordinate whose qualities I know how to value.*

"With the assurance of true esteem.

"Minister of War. **Józef Prince Poniatowski** [printed "Poniatowki"]."

"To the Honourable Lieutenant-Colonel Miączyński, knight of the Military Cross, *here.*"

The discharge he asked for was issued, with the rank of colonel, on 25 January 1812; after which Count Stanisław went first to Dresden, and then settled on his wife's estates at Trąbczyn in the Kalisz country. — For already earlier,

[p. 221]

as a brilliant officer surrounded by the aureole of courage, he had met in the capital a rich and young widow of eminent virtues, Michalina née **Prusimska Ostroróg** [printed "Prasimskich"], Countess Dąmbska, wife of Józef, daughter of Antoni, starosta of Nieszczewice, and of Maryanna Dąmbska of the Godziemba arms,[^1] daughter of the castellan of Kowal (see vol. I p. 59; vol. VII p. 188), and having won her heart, he married her in August 1809. The marriage settlement — by which Michalina née Prusimska, Countess Dąmbska, wife of Józef, assigns to her future husband all the estates named above, while Count Stanisław secures to his future wife, out of the sum of 100,000 złoty of his share after his mother, already received, 90,000 złoty secured on the estates of Count Ignacy Miączyński, starosta of Kamieniopol, and the remaining 10,000 złoty placed on the property of Count Józef Ossoliński, his stepfather, and promises to settle on his wife the rest of his mother's share too after his mother's death, in all a sum of 280,000 złoty — was signed by: Stanisław Count Miączyński; Michalina Dąmbska née Prusimska; in the name of his wife, Teresa née Rafałowicz, by her first marriage Miączyńska, Count Józef Ossoliński; Józef Nowicki, colonel; Adam Broniec; Countess Agnieszka Skarbek née Dąmbska; Walenty Skorochód Majewski, notary; and so on.

The couple lived very happily, now on the Trąbczyn estates in the Kalisz country, now at Pawłowo near Kiszkowo in the Grand Duchy of Posen, where the trees planted by Michalina's hand still form a splendid park. Four sons brightened the evening of the brave Napoleonic veteran's life. When his health failed he went to Berlin for medical advice. There, feeling his strength ebbing, he disposed of his property as follows: to the eldest, Aleksander, he left the Pawłowo estates; to the second, Włodzimierz, then living in exile, capital; to the third, Mieczysław, already the heir of Wieniec in the Kingdom of Poland, he gave, in the form of a sale, the Trąbczyn estates and the village of Szetlewek in the Kalisz country; and lastly to the youngest, Witold, also in the form of a sale, the estate of Brzeź with its appurtenances in the district of Brześć Kujawski. — Count Stanisław's widow ended her life on 19 January 1851 on her estate of Trąbczyn, and was buried there beside her husband. The "Kuryer Warszawski" of the time (no. 65, of 9 March) gave her the following notice: "The late Countess Michalina Miączyńska, having begun the spring of her life in brilliant times in the great world, educated without conceit, beautiful without vanity, had the right to love the bustle of the world, which received her as its ornament; but a mother's feeling showed her another road. Choosing a quiet retreat on her native soil, she spent there a peaceful life, given to the duties of a model wife and model mother. After the loss of her husband the late Mrs Miączyńska redoubled her efforts, for her lofty heart told her to be a mother and to take the place of the dead father. Sparing in her own expenses, sparing in all the pleasures of life, she was generous in holding out a hand to suffering humanity, generous in helping her neighbour. A model citizen, a guardian of the peasants worthy to be followed, a kind neighbour and mistress of her house, she held to the last moments of her life to the principles and virtues that had adorned her from her youth."

2\. Count **Włodzimierz** [12th generation], *officer of 1831*, heir of the Trąbczyn estate, which he bought from his brother Mieczysław after his return from exile; born 29 July 1811, died 18 December 1879 at Trąbczyn and buried there. Unmarried. The Poznań papers gave him the following notice:

"The late Włodzimierz, son of a powerful family known throughout the country, did not, it is true, take a prominent part in public life; but as a citizen of rare uprightness of character, of steady and conscientious work, and of great love of his native soil and its tradition, he was everywhere held up as a model to the younger generations. Brought up with great care in his parents' house and in the then famous school of the Piarists at Żoliborz, at seventeen he went to Kraków to complete his studies at the Jagiellonian University. The events of 1830 broke off the course of his further studies and condemned Count Włodzimierz, with his elder brother Count Aleksander, to a stay of many years abroad. When at last he was allowed to return to his native home, Count Włodzimierz, having taken over the family estate of Trąbczyn in the Konin country, gave himself with extraordinary zeal to the care of the piece

[p. 223]

of native soil that had fallen to his share from his forebears, and became, although he had until then spent almost his whole life abroad in the great capitals, a model farmer and a true father to his peasants. More than once his family itself and his friends urged him, on account of his poor health, to give up the troubles of country husbandry; he always answered that the most sacred duty of a Polish citizen was to guard the piece of native soil entrusted to him, and to keep with the Polish peasant that relation of mutual trust and attachment which alone can save our younger brethren from the treacherous whisperings of hostile elements. The late Włodzimierz Miączyński held to this principle steadfastly until his death, and for that alone he deserves honour.

"No less warmly was he attached to his family. When the death of the late Count Aleksander Miączyński orphaned his two children, Count Włodzimierz loved the two orphans with his whole soul, and from then on the one aim of his life was to prepare the most brilliant future for them both. The untimely death, in the flower of youth and grace, of his niece, the late Wanda Potworowska née Miączyńska, wife of Gustaw, dealt a heavy wound to his heart. With all the greater care he surrounded his remaining nephew, whom he had early named his heir. As guardian he administered until the coming of age of Count Władysław Miączyński his property, Pawłowo, and handed over to him this estate in model order.

"Feeling death approach, he prepared for it with Christian calm, pouring out his heart into the bosom of his companion and friend of more than ten years, the worthy canon Father Karkowski, parish priest of Trąbczyn. Almost the whole family gathered at Trąbczyn and surrounded the failing old man with the most tender care; to the last moment he kept his full presence of mind and, like an old soldier, would not die in bed but as if at his post, busy all the while with the affairs of his estates and with the future of those nearest him in view. Death overtook him sitting on a sofa and giving orders. There was barely time to hand him the cross and the blessed candle. Pressing the image of the Saviour to his stiffening lips, he died as he had lived: "with God and with honour", as was rightly and beautifully written on the wreath that adorned the coffin.

"That the gentry of the neighbourhood and the clergy would gather in numbers to pay the last service to so worthy a man, an officer of 1831, could not be doubted [printed "watpić"]; but what warmed every heart was the sight, beside the dead body of their lord and benefactor, of several hundred peasants in sincere tears of grief.

"The body was carried out of the larch-wood manor house to the little wooden church [printed "drewniane", for drewnianego], which by a generous gift of the deceased will soon stand in brick, late in the evening, by the light of torches and of burning tar barrels set out along the gravel road. The next day, after a solemn service, the body was laid in the chapel on the cemetery a verst from the church, where the family vault of this branch of the Counts Miączyński stands. Both from the manor to the church and from the church to the cemetery the coffin was carried in turn by the family, the gentry, the officials of the Trąbczyn estates and the peasants. Three priests spoke during the funeral ceremonies, and the words of the preacher who took leave of the body at the cemetery brought tears to all present."

Count **Mieczysław** (12th generation), born 13 February 1818, heir of the estates of Wieniec and Izbica in the Kingdom of Poland, married Marya **Słubicka** of the Prus I arms, born 1828, died 18 October 1865, buried at Trąbczyn, daughter of August, general of the former Polish army [printed "wojsk wojsk", the word twice], and of Łucya née Countess Zboińska, daughter of a voivode. Of this marriage there was only one daughter, Countess Zdzisława, born 10 June 1851, who died in the flower of her age on 23 August 1876, wife of the late Zbigniew Dzierzykraj **Morawski** of the Nałęcz arms, son of Józef, member of the Prussian House of Lords, former director-general of the Old Landschaft of Posen, and of Eugenia née Dzierzykraj Morawska, daughter of Józef, referendary of state, and of Paula née Countess Łubieńska. Zbigniew Morawski, a man of great gifts, died in June 1889, leaving two little daughters, Joanna and Zdzisława Morawska,

[p. 224]

heiresses of Wieniec and Izbica. Count Mieczysław, the known composer of the "Kujawiaki", known throughout the country and founded on folk songs, ended his life suddenly on 16 March 1887 at Włocławek and was buried there. Having received the most careful upbringing in his parents' house and in schools of the first rank, and having then visited foreign capitals and countries, the late Mieczysław came back to his native soil as a young man richly endowed with every quality that opens the way into the first society and makes a man the favourite of the salons. A distinguished family, an ample fortune, a splendid and truly lordly figure, a face of regular and handsome features, rare courtesy and warmth of manner, set in the most refined forms, and with all this the charm of a soul breathing poetry, feeling everything that is lofty and delighting above all in everything that is of the homeland, and lastly an uncommon musical artistry, won the late Mieczysław a wide circle of friends and gained him the hand of a maiden as beautiful as she was radiant with womanly virtues. This unclouded domestic happiness was broken by the death of his beloved wife, and twelve years later by that of his only daughter, after barely two years of married life. Almost at the same time as this bright star went out in his lonely widower's life, Count Mieczysław's sight went out too. He went blind; but even this terrible blow could not break his life. On the contrary, from then on he lived a doubled inner life. The serenity of a clear conscience and an upright character spread over his face; his mind kept the freshness of youthful flight, which flowed from under his fingers in a broad stream of melody when in hours of longing and loneliness he sat down at the piano, or sparkled in quickness of view, deeper learning and knowledge of the world and of men, and in a wit that wounded no one, when he found himself among relations, friends and acquaintances. One often had to admire this aged but hale old man, who bore the most grievous infirmity on earth with such calm of spirit and such ease! Deeply attached, as we have said, to his native soil and its tradition, he knew Polish literature excellently and delighted in its treasures. He liked to listen to the songs of the country people, and from this folk yarn, from these simple flowers of feeling, he spun and then set his "Kujawiaki", now sad and tender like the quiet murmur of a spring rain, now full of that dashing verve by which the melodies of our people are marked. And so Miączyński's "Kujawiaki" will always remain a lasting monument to him in the field [printed "dzisdzinie", for dziedzinie] of Polish music, and will be a precious source of folk song for our composers to come. His was a noble and strong nature; he was an oak of past centuries amid the stunted generation of today, and he too fell, unshaken to the last moment by the storm of misfortunes, like a mighty oak that only a sudden thunderbolt from heaven or a cruel, treacherous axe can bring to the ground.
