## Chapter I

### THE SECOND PARTITION OF POLAND — GREATER POLAND UNDER PRUSSIAN RULE

[7] <!-- p.7 -->

In 1792, when the Targowica Confederation held sway in Poland, two German powers in the west of Europe, Austria and Prussia, were waging a bloody struggle against revolutionary France. The campaign, which had begun successfully in the spring, took an altogether unfortunate turn in the autumn. The stout resistance offered by General Dumouriez at Valmy on 20 September, and the diseases raging among the soldiers[^1], induced both allied monarchs to retreat, burying the hopes of a swift suppression of the revolution.

Frederick William II, in a letter of 17 October, informed Catherine of the retreat of his troops from the field of battle, and at the same time reminded her of the compensation due to him for the losses and war costs he had sustained[^2]. The question of compensation for taking part in the struggle against the revolution had, indeed, been the principal concern of the Prussian king throughout the whole campaign. As early as the second half of July the rulers of Prussia and Austria had met at Mainz to consider this important matter. There they deliberated on the exchange of Belgium for Bavaria to the benefit of Austria, and on the compensation of Prussia with Polish lands. The Prussian minister Schulenburg, who was present at the conference, named the Poznań [Posen], Gniezno [Gnesen] and Kuyavian voivodeships and part of the Sieradz voivodeship, all the way

[8] <!-- p.8 -->

to the Prosna, as future Prussian acquisitions[^3]. In a note drawn up three months later under the eye of the king himself in the village of Merle near Luxembourg, and soon communicated to the Viennese court, Prussia made its further participation in the war against France dependent on Austria's consent to its taking possession of Polish provinces. How large an area Prussia meant to take for itself the Austrian statesman Spielmann soon discovered, when he travelled with the Prussian minister Haugwitz from Merle to Luxembourg. Haugwitz spread before him a map of Poland on which the king had drawn with his own hand the boundary of the Polish lands that were to fall to him as his share in return for continuing to support his ally. The country that Prussia demanded was almost twice as large as that demanded at the Mainz meeting. "If," said Haugwitz, "obstacles were placed in Prussia's way in the seizure of this territory, the King of Prussia would limit his aid in the new campaign against France to only those 20,000 men to which he was bound by the February alliance, but in any case he would seize a smaller part of Poland for the losses suffered so far"[^4].

Emperor Francis II long delayed expressing his consent to such exorbitant pretensions on the part of his ally. The prospects of realising his own plans concerning Bavaria became very slim from the moment when General Dumouriez, after the victory won in November over the Austrians at Jemappes, began to overrun Belgium. Two possibilities now arose for Austria: to conclude peace with France, as England, which was opposed to the partitions of Poland, desired, or to take the field against the victorious French with redoubled energy; and in that case Prussian help was indispensable.

Meanwhile fruitless deliberations on Prussia's demands were being conducted in Vienna with the Prussian minister Haugwitz

[9] <!-- p.9 -->

, until finally, on 5 December, a motion of the Austrian vice-chancellor Cobenzl [Translator's note: the vice-chancellor was Philipp Cobenzl; the envoy at St Petersburg named later in this paragraph, whom the text prints as "Ludwik", is his relative Ludwig Cobenzl. The author distinguishes the two only by office.] moved the matter of compensation onto a new track. Cobenzl declared that the emperor consented to Prussia's pretensions, but that in this case the assent of Russia was necessary. Should the Prussian king, however, occupy Polish lands before Austria had effected the exchange of Belgium for Bavaria, the emperor would then be compelled to occupy a corresponding area in Poland himself until the Belgian-Bavarian exchange had come about. Cobenzl's words provoked violent opposition from Haugwitz. He knew that Catherine would never allow Austria to acquire new lands in Poland, and in that case all Austrian efforts in that direction would needlessly protract the whole affair. He therefore demanded that the Viennese court declare itself clearly regarding Prussia's intentions, without advancing pretensions of its own in respect of Poland. News arriving from Belgium of further successes of Dumouriez, and the fear that Prussia might refuse to support with its army the Austrians fighting the revolution, finally induced the emperor, on 19 December, to make concessions. Austria officially agreed to Prussia's demands, but its game was a double one, for, in secret from the Prussians, it intended to obstruct them at St Petersburg. The envoy Count Ludwik Cobenzl received instructions in accordance with which he was to demand from Catherine compensation for Prussia in Poland, in order to secure for Austria Prussia's help in the war against France, but on the other hand he was to oppose an excessive growth of Prussia. Cobenzl was also to announce that, in the event of a new partition of Poland taking place before the Bavarian exchange was accomplished, Austrian troops would then enter the southern voivodeships of Poland. This last instruction, however, the Austrian envoy did not venture to put forward[^5].

[10] <!-- p.10 -->

To set up a barrier against Prussia at St Petersburg, however, was by now too late. As early as 16 December the Prussian representative in the capital on the Neva, Count Goltz, had learned of the approval of his monarch's demands by the Russian court. Catherine, who had long resisted a new division of Poland, planning to seize it for herself by peaceful means, now, under the influence of the fear that the colluding German states might proceed to the division on their own without her cooperation, consented to the seizure by the Prussian king's troops of the booty he had marked out, while she herself intended to seize a corresponding tract of country in the east of the Commonwealth[^6].

From then on the work of the Second Partition proceeded at a rapid pace.

The territories that were to become part of the Russian empire were already occupied by the tsarina's troops[^7]. For in the spring of 1792, in order to support and shield the opponents of the Constitution of 3 May, two armies under Generals Kachowski and Kreczetnikow had entered Poland[^8]. Prussia announced the introduction of its troops within the borders of the Commonwealth in a declaration of 6 January 1793. Wishing to conceal his true intentions, Frederick William II, greedy for plunder, gave as the reason for this invasion the spread in Poland of "French democratism" and the support given there to Jacobin emissaries. Greater Poland in particular, the Prussian king asserted, "is infected with this dangerous poison" and has contact with the French clubs; therefore the entry of Prussian troops into its territory is dictated solely by Prussia's own danger[^9]. On 16 January[^10] the Prussian envoy in Warsaw, Buchholtz, handed this declaration to Chancellor Małachowski [Translator's note: the author gives no first name; probably Jacek Małachowski, Grand Chancellor of the Crown, not Stanisław Małachowski, Marshal of the Great Sejm.]. A few days later, on 23 January[^11], the formal Prusso-Russian partition agreement was concluded at St Petersburg, in secrecy[^12] from the world. Austria was to be informed of this fact

[11] <!-- p.11 -->

only after the exchange of the mutual treaties of ratification[^13].

By virtue of the convention concluded, Prussia received Gdańsk [Danzig] and Toruń [Thorn], the remainder of the Poznań, Gniezno and Inowrocław [Inowrazlaw] voivodeships, the Brześć Kujawski voivodeship with Dobrzyń [Dobrin] Land, the Łęczyca [Lentschütz], Kalisz [Kalisch] and Sieradz voivodeships with Wieluń [Welun] Land, and in addition the Płock voivodeship, part of the Rawa and Mazovia voivodeships on the right bank of the Vistula (Zakroczym Land), and a strip of the Kraków voivodeship (Częstochowa [Tschenstochau]). The boundary line ran from the north, from Działdowo [Soldau] on the Wkra, to the Vistula opposite Wyszogród, thence along the Pilica to Koniecpol, and finally below Częstochowa to the borders of Silesia[^14].

The parts of the Poznań, Gniezno and Inowrocław voivodeships lying on the Noteć had been obtained by Prussia already in the First Partition[^15]; as the so-called Netze District they formed, together with Royal Prussia, which likewise fell to Prussia at that time, the province of "West Prussia". Having now received the remainder of the Poznań, Gniezno and Inowrocław voivodeships, and the voivodeships of Brześć Kujawski with Dobrzyń Land, Łęczyca, Kalisz and Sieradz with Wieluń Land, Prussia possessed the whole of Greater Poland[^16].

Russia seized part of the Vilnius voivodeship, further the Minsk voivodeship, the eastern part of the Nowogródek, Brest-Litovsk and Volhynia voivodeships, and the Kyiv, Bratslav and Podolia voivodeships — an area up to the line running from Dünaburg through Pinsk to the Zbruch.

Prussia was enlarged by 1,060 square miles, Russia by over 4,500; there remained to Poland about 3,830 square miles[^17].

Meanwhile the Targowica Confederation, taken aback by the announcement of the Prussian invasion, resolved to defend itself. In a letter to the Berlin court it denied the existence of clubs, and appealed

[12] <!-- p.12 -->

to the measures undertaken to suppress the revolution and to the protection of the "great sovereign" as sufficient to maintain legal order in the country, and finally demanded that the King of Prussia "be pleased to revoke" the orders issued to his army. At the same time it sent letters to the Empress and to King Stanisław August, and ordered both armies, the Crown and the Lithuanian, to stand in readiness[^18].

These measures, however, were futile.

On 24 January the Prussian troops began to enter Greater Poland[^19]. General Möllendorff, commanding a corps of more than 15,000 men[^20], marched in six[^21] columns from the direction of Silesia, the Neumark and East Prussia. His first column went towards Wschowa [Fraustadt] and Leszno [Lissa], the second and third towards Poznań, the fourth set out towards Gniezno, the fifth occupied Toruń, and the last advanced in the direction of Dobrzyń[^22].

Seeing in this fact a new partition, the Targowica Confederation protested solemnly before the world on 3 February against the entry of Prussian troops into Greater Poland, as well as against any seizure of lands of the Commonwealth; it declared that it "is not party to any agreements on the severance of any part of them" and appealed to the imperial courts not, in their own interest, to "look with indifferent eye upon the violation of the nation's rights[^23]".

While the confederation was thus lodging its desperate and ineffectual protests, the Prussians advanced into the interior of the country, meeting with only slight resistance from the Polish troops stationed in Greater Poland.

These troops formed part of the so-called Greater Poland Division. It was then commanded by the seventy-year-old and infirm General Arnold Byszewski, who had his quarters at Pyzdry [Peisern]. The individual units of the division were disposed as follows: the first brigade

[13] <!-- p.13 -->

of National Cavalry under Madaliński stood in the Gniezno and Kalisz regions along the banks of the Warta; the second brigade of National Cavalry, under Biernacki, in the Kalisz and Sieradz regions, likewise partly on the banks of the Warta; the sixth infantry regiment under Szyrer at Wschowa; the first under Kurcjusz at Kalisz; the ninth under Gordon at Poznań; the seventh under Zawisza at Łowicz [Lowitsch]. In all, the number of soldiers in the Greater Poland Division amounted to about 6,200 men[^24].

With such a force, moreover without guns or ammunition, the considerably more powerful Prussians would have had to be faced, had Byszewski not received from Grodno an order to withdraw before them behind the Pilica and the Bzura[^25]. Even before that, however, there had been some lesser clashes.

On 26 January Major von Platen of Trenck's hussar regiment clashed in the small town of Sieraków [Zirke] with a detachment of Polish cavalry numbering 150 horse. After brief resistance the Poles surrendered. Two days later there was a clash at Kargowa [Unruhstadt], where Major Więckowski was stationed as garrison with 40 men of Brodowski's regiment. Major Milkau, entering the town with Frankenberg's infantry battalion, called upon the Polish soldiers to surrender. Więckowski, however, did not heed the summons but, shutting himself up in the town hall, defended himself with his detachment for some time until he fell, struck by an enemy bullet. Among his men several were wounded or killed; the rest laid down their arms[^26].

General Möllendorff reported these events to the King of Prussia on 1 February, the day after his own occupation of Poznań. He wrote that Toruń too had offered slight resistance, since Lieutenant-General Schwerin, on entering there, had found the city gates closed and had been obliged to break them down; that everywhere the wealthy nobility, the clergy and the burghers of the large towns showed the Prussians

[14] <!-- p.14 -->

discontent and even defiance; and that among the latter a true Jacobin spirit prevailed[^27].

Among the towns which did not, after all, go under the Prussian yoke without protest was also Gniezno. There Vice-Brigadier Jan Henryk Dąbrowski, with two squadrons, bravely resisted the detachments of General Amandrüz for two days and did not allow himself to be driven from the town until he received the order to retreat[^28]. This movement, Dąbrowski writes in his Pamiętnik wojskowy legionów polskich we Włoszech[^29], was carried out on 8 February. The Greater Poland Division took up positions from Łęczyca and Łowicz along the Bzura as far as Sochaczew and Kampinos; the main headquarters was moved from Pyzdry to Łowicz.

Various rumours reached the new outposts. It was said that the Prussians intended to surround the entire division and force it into Prussian service, and that the Russian court "looks with disfavour upon this entry of the Prussians"; and King Stanisław August himself wrote in warning to General Byszewski "that he should be on his guard against any unexpected approach on the part of the Prussian troops".

In view of such reports a bold plan of offensive action against the Prussians arose in Dąbrowski's mind. He advised moving the entire division towards Warsaw, seizing the arsenal, which was occupied by the Russians, and then striking at the Prussian forces. He supposed that if action were taken against the Prussians alone, "the Muscovites will look on with folded arms". Should the Prussians press too strongly, Dąbrowski proposed, the division should shift to Gdańsk and there await help from France, of which the patriots told so many "extraordinary things". But it was necessary to act at once. Meanwhile Byszewski, who owed the whole of his

[15] <!-- p.15 -->

career to the king, did not dare to undertake anything without his consent, and "at least without the assent of his brother-in-law Gorzeński, the king's adjutant general". In the end the plan fell through. The Russian commandant in Warsaw, General Igelström, learned of it and reinforced the Russian garrison in the capital, which brought Dąbrowski's designs to nothing. Byszewski, whom Dąbrowski had finally managed to persuade to move a day's march closer to Warsaw, received an order to proceed with his division into the Sandomierz region[^30].

Meanwhile the Prussians were completing the garrisoning of their new acquisitions. They reached Rawa and Łęczyca, then occupied Łowicz, where Möllendorff himself arrived on 22 February, and finally on 5 March, with the capitulation of Częstochowa, which had long resisted them, they completed the military occupation of the Polish lands[^31]. They could now proceed to the official taking of possession of the new lands and to receiving the homage of the inhabitants. For under the St Petersburg partition convention the military occupation was to come first, and only then the acts of official taking of possession and of homage.

The prelude to the first of these acts was the notification patent of Frederick William II of 25 March, proclaiming the passage of territories hitherto belonging to the Commonwealth under the Prussian sceptre. In it the King of Prussia listed all the voivodeships and lands which he was incorporating into his state, and stressed his complete solidarity in this matter with the Russian Empress and the consent of the Holy Roman Emperor; he declared that the Polish Commonwealth, in view of the anarchy spreading within it and dangerous to its neighbours, was to exist within boundaries which, in the opinion of both partitioning powers, corresponded better to its internal strength and situation; and finally he expressed the hope that the nation, shortly to assemble in the Sejm, would ratify the annexation, in return for which it would gain an undisturbed peace[^32].

[16] <!-- p.16 -->

On 9 April the Prusso-Russian declaration announcing the Second Partition was handed to the Targowica Confederation by the new Russian ambassador in Warsaw, Sievers, and the Prussian envoy Buchholtz[^33]. Many days still passed, however, before the last Sejm of the Commonwealth was compelled to recognise the partition treaty.

The Prussians carried out the official taking of possession of the new province between 7 and 24 April. This operation was directed by two commissioners appointed by the king, General Möllendorff and the Silesian minister of justice Danckelmann. A whole staff of officials came to their assistance. Some surveyed the boundaries exactly and set up posts upon them bearing the inscription "Royal Prussian Territory"; others announced to the inhabitants the notification patent of 25 March and summoned them to swear homage to the king on 7 May. On official buildings and town gates the Prussian emblem[^34] now appeared.

After this official taking of possession of Greater Poland and the rest of the seized lands, the newcomers from Brandenburg proceeded to organise them. The country was named South Prussia, since this name pleased the King of Prussia most[^35]. Von Voss became chief minister of South Prussia, and von Buggenhagen Oberpräsident (chief president). Voss set about organising the administration at once, with the help of the Silesian minister Hoym[^36]. The whole territory was divided into two departments. The first, called the Poznań department after the seat of the authorities in Poznań, comprised the former Poznań, Gniezno and Kalisz voivodeships and was divided into 17 districts (powiaty; German Kreise). The second, the Łęczyca department, named the Piotrków department soon after the seat of the authorities was transferred from Łęczyca to Piotrków [Petrikau], comprised the former Sieradz voivodeship with Wieluń Land, part of the Kraków (Częstochowa)

[17] <!-- p.17 -->

and Rawa voivodeships, the Łęczyca voivodeship, both Kuyavian voivodeships together with Dobrzyń Land, the Płock voivodeship, and part of the Mazovia voivodeship on the right bank of the Vistula, forming altogether 27 districts[^37].

In the department the supreme magistracy for general administrative, police, fiscal and confessional affairs was the Chamber (Kriegs- und Domänenkammer, "War and Domains Chamber"). Jurisdiction in the aforementioned branches of administration also belonged to it[^38]. The post of President of the Poznań Chamber went to v. Grape, and that of the Piotrków Chamber to Oppeln[^39] Bronikowski. In general and fiscal administration the organs of first instance were, in the districts, the Landräte (district commissioners) and, in the towns[^40] grouped into inspection districts, the tax inspectors, the so-called Steuerräte[^41].

The organisation of the judiciary on the Prussian model was undertaken by Minister Danckelmann. In Poznań and Piotrków there arose, as the highest judicial authorities, the so-called Regierungen (provincial high courts of justice), to which belonged criminal and civil cases together with mortgage and guardianship matters. Steudener took charge of the Poznań Regierung, and Count Reuss of the Piotrków one. For matters of lesser weight and for the receiving of acts of voluntary jurisdiction, district justice commissions (Kreis-Justiz-Kommissionen) were installed. For a few years Polish law, especially property, family and inheritance law, remained in force. To the great dissatisfaction of the population, however, Prussian procedure and the German language were introduced into the judiciary. Poles had no access whatsoever to judicial posts[^42].

The school authority in the territory of South Prussia was not uniform. Catholic schools were subject to the Chambers, Protestant ones to the consistory, which depended on the Regierung. Supreme supervision over the whole of the school system belonged to the Higher School Board (Oberschulkollegium) in Berlin[^43].

[18] <!-- p.18 -->

In the work of introducing the new institutions in Greater Poland there were occasional interruptions on account of exceptional celebrations.

First of all, to seal the new state of affairs, in accordance with the provisions of the partition convention of 23 January 1793, the inhabitants had to pay homage to the King of Prussia. It was rendered in all the larger centres on 7 May 1793. On that occasion 1,500 representatives of the noble estate and 700 of the urban estate came to the capital of Greater Poland and, into the hands of the commissioners Möllendorff and Danckelmann, swore the oath of subjection to their new ruler. To lend splendour to the moment, a solemn service was held in the former Jesuit church, and in the evening a magnificent ball was given for the deputies in the Gurowski Palace, later called the Działyński Palace. On the occasion of so great a celebration, festivities were held throughout Greater Poland. Frederick William was pleased. Möllendorff —[^44] a man "shrewd and cunning, a master of political intrigue, able to cover everything with a semblance of good nature and to charm with his grey hair, sweet glance and courteous manner"[^45], soon popular in Poznań and among the nobility of Greater Poland — received, in recognition of his services, his nomination as field marshal[^46].

More than three months later, on 18 August, it was ordered that the surrender of Mainz by the French be celebrated, and on 25 September — at the very time when the Sejm assembled at Grodno, terrorised by Sievers and Buchholtz, was signing the partition treaty with Prussia — the king's birthday[^47].

Not long afterwards the king himself visited Greater Poland and the rest of the seized lands. He set out from Frankfurt an der Oder at the beginning of October and on the 10th of that month, in the company of Lucchesini, a man well remembered from the time of the

[19] <!-- p.19 -->

Polish-Prussian alliance of 1790, and of several officers, arrived in Poznań. From there he went to Kościan [Kosten], then to Wschowa, Rawicz [Rawitsch], Kalisz, Płock, Łowicz and Piotrków. Everywhere he was warmly welcomed by Jews and some Poles, although few in number. The Primate, Michał Poniatowski, came from Warsaw to Łowicz to greet the king, and the Radziwiłł princely couple gave a magnificent ball in honour of the Prussian monarch at Nieborów. Having stayed on Polish lands until the end of October, Frederick William returned to Berlin on 10 November[^48]. The fruit of this journey was the conviction that the Greater Poles were not glad to have passed under Prussian rule, whatever Lucchesini vainly tried to make the king believe. For the generality of the inhabitants did not even come out to meet their new master[^49].

Nor is it surprising that this was so. In those few months of rule the Prussians had already made themselves felt by the Greater Poles. Slowly but systematically they uprooted everything that breathed of the Polish past and stamped the Prussian imprint on every sphere of life. Efforts were made to give the offices a Prussian character. Poles were removed from them. Only out of necessity, in order to communicate with a population that did not understand German, was the office of Landrat[^50] entrusted to Poles, together with the dignity of one of the chamber councillors. These officials, however, were forbidden to wear Polish dress, lest they recall the bygone order of things. The Prussian government extended its supervision over the Catholic clergy, the confraternities and the guilds, it checked the activities of the town councils, it sent in colonists, and it took the youth of Greater Poland as recruits. Deepening the distinctions of estate among the inhabitants, it permitted by the edict of 24 July 1793 only the nobility to own estates. It threatened the clergy with the loss of their landed property, and the holders of starostwa (crown estates held by a starosta) with the loss of these, intending to turn these estates into

[20] <!-- p.20 -->

state domains; in the end, however, so as not to alienate its new subjects, it left them until the year 1796 in the hands of their existing holders, against a levy of 50 per cent on income[^51].

Embitterment among the local population was caused by certain Prussian ordinances touching everyday life, such as, for example, the raising of the price of salt by nearly 50 per cent, and the order to pay a tax on the so-called "Musikzettli" (music permits), which authorised the holding of entertainments accompanied by music, and so on[^52].

Although the Greater Poles received the Prussian innovations with reluctance, they nevertheless did not rise to any more serious resistance against the invader. At the beginning of the Prussian invasion, when Prussian troops were entering Poznań and a Polish infantry regiment was withdrawing to Kórnik [Kurnik][^53], the Poznań burghers were not moved by this to any great extent. This passivity must puzzle us all the more since it was precisely they who had played a great role in the movement that led to the Constitution of 3 May[^54], and at one time maintained lively relations with the plenipotentiary of the towns of the Poznań district at the Four-Year Sejm, Józef[^55] Wybicki. A few months later, in the year 1792, when danger began to threaten the new constitution, the Poznań burghers were ready to stand in defence of the endangered order. This is stated plainly in the letter of the then syndic and town councillor Sobolewski of 25 May, in which he wrote to Wybicki[^56] that among the burghers "zeal is growing for putting up lanterns for the opponents of the constitution"; Sobolewski himself gave his assurance "that if he heard anyone speaking against the constitution", he would be "the first to put the noose" on him. And so the calm behaviour of the inhabitants of Poznań at the moment when Prussian troops entered the city exceeded even the expectation of Möllendorff himself. Möllendorff

[21] <!-- p.21 -->

had expected disturbances in the city instead, and had assigned a strong garrison to it. For he knew that the nobility of the surrounding area was gathering in Poznań in great numbers and that the wealthy burghers favoured the slogans of the French Revolution[^57]. His fears nevertheless proved groundless. The capital of Greater Poland then managed nothing more than the tearing down of Prussian proclamations[^58]. Nor did the population of other localities react any more keenly to the Prussian incursion.

It was only the insurrection that was to rouse the Greater Poles from their slumber.

## Chapter II

### THE OUTBREAK OF THE KOŚCIUSZKO INSURRECTION — THE REASONS WHY IT DID NOT SPREAD TO GREATER POLAND FOR A LONG TIME

[22] <!-- p.22 -->

The intention of saving the fatherland, threatened in its very existence, by means of armed struggle against the partitioning powers began to mature among the Poles in the spring of 1793. Numerous secret societies arose with the aim of preparing an insurrection. There were three principal centres of conspiracy: one in the army, which felt the humiliation of the country with particular keenness and which, moreover, as a result of the curtailment of the state's frontiers, was threatened in its own existence; a second among the "Jacobins" in Warsaw; a third among the émigrés in Saxony, extending in part to Paris. The moderates, such as Ignacy Działyński, colonel-proprietor (szef) of the 10th infantry regiment and the "soul of the conspiracy"[^59], such as Kapostas, a Warsaw banker, such as Jelski, starosta of Starodub and a deputy from the time of the Great Sejm, together with the emigration headed by Ignacy Potocki and Kołłątaj, intended to take up arms after suitable preparation and mutual understanding between the emigration and the country, and with support from abroad. The radicals, who were young and inexperienced men such as Józef Pawlikowski, a burgher's son and a writer of the era of the Four-Year Sejm, Eliasz Aloe, of a family of French descent

[23] <!-- p.23 -->

, Jan Czyż, a cavalry major, and others, wished to proceed to action at once[^60].

How the conspiratorial activity developed we do not know precisely. In any case, the conspirators in the country were not idle. They intended to extend the plot to other provinces, as is proved by the sending out of emissaries, who soon reported the readiness of Greater Poland, Lesser Poland and Lithuania. They then dispatched a delegation to Leipzig, made up of Aloe and Walichnowski, to come to an understanding with Kołłątaj and Potocki, and with Kościuszko, generally marked out as the future Commander (*Naczelnik*) of the insurrection. According to a plan adopted, it seems, under the influence of the radical faction, it was intended to begin the uprising in the autumn, when the enemy troops would go into winter quarters. The outbreak was to take place simultaneously in many places, and Warsaw was to give the signal for battle. It was supposed that the five-thousand-strong garrison of Polish soldiery in the capital, aided by almost as many burghers, would easily overcome the eight-thousand-strong Russian garrison. Beyond this it was intended to rely on the regular soldiery stationed between the Pilica and the Vistula; of its total strength of thirteen thousand, six thousand would strike at the Prussian troops, and the remainder, together with part of the Warsaw garrison, at the Russians in Volhynia and in the Lublin region[^61].

Kościuszko, like Kołłątaj and Potocki, took a sceptical view of the conspirators' plan. He judged that an outbreak of the uprising in the autumn would be premature, but, in keeping with the promise given to the delegation, he appeared on the Galician border in order to become better acquainted with the preparations. Meanwhile Zajączek, who accompanied him, went to Warsaw and from there reported to Kościuszko that the uprising could count for the moment only on the army, and in particular on the readiness of only two generals,

[24] <!-- p.24 -->

Madaliński and Działyński, and besides these on the common people of Warsaw and the petty nobility in some voivodeships, but nowhere on the help of the peasant masses. Under the influence of this news the future Commander was confirmed in his opinion that the time for action had not yet come, and in order to await a more suitable moment, and at the same time to lull the vigilance of the partitioning powers, he went to Italy. He left with the Warsaw conspirators instructions ordering them to form patriotic clubs in all the voivodeships and to awaken the people's awareness. He himself intended to seek a loan from the Committee of Public Safety, and to persuade the Porte and the Swedes to render assistance to Poland[^62].

In the first days of November a delegation set out again, in the persons of Jelski and Gorzkowski, to Kościuszko, who was staying in Italy. It did not, however, receive a positive answer. Kościuszko ordered them to wait. The reason for the delay was, besides the failure of the conspirators to carry out his instructions, his conviction that foreign political relations were developing unfavourably for the insurrectionary cause[^63].

Empty-handed, too, more than three months later, the last emissaries from the country returned from Kościuszko at Dresden: Prozor, Dmochowski, and Gliszczyński, who had arrived with a report from Greater Poland. This time, too, the same considerations as before, and chiefly the wish to await help from France, led Kościuszko to hold back the outbreak of the uprising. Unfortunately Kościuszko did not know that the revolutionary government had no thought of giving the Poles any real help. Its empty promises had as their sole aim the exploitation of the situation in Poland for its own plans in the struggle against the coalition[^64].

The constant postponement of the outbreak of the uprising did the national cause no good. In the spring of 1794 the prospects of

[25] <!-- p.25 -->

success of the armed movement were no longer as favourable as in the autumn. The political situation had by no means improved. The conspiracy had not embraced the peasant masses, who, like the greater part of the landed gentry, were to remain indifferent in the year 1794. To make matters worse, the country's strategic conditions had deteriorated in the course of the winter. The Russians had regrouped the Polish corps in such a way that none of them could resist the Russian troops surrounding them; Madaliński's brigade[^65] was moved as far as Ostrołęka [Ostrolenka], and the greater part of the Polish forces was pushed into Volhynia, between two cordons[^66].

The conspirators, however, could no longer delay. Ferment reigned in the army when the reduction of the Polish army from 30,000 to 12,000 soldiers was ordered. It was intended to begin military action even without Kościuszko. On 7 March the conspirators held their final deliberations in Warsaw, fixing the outbreak of the uprising for 9 March. General Igelström, however, learned of the meeting and ordered the arrest of several Poles, among others Kapostas and Działyński. The former happily hid himself in Kraków, but Działyński was seized on the Galician border[^67].

The capture of the chief leader of the conspirators no longer halted the course of events, which tended towards armed action. On 12 March the bold deed of Antoni Madaliński put an end to all uncertainty. This general, initiated into the conspiratorial plans, did not obey the order to disband his soldiers. On that day he set out from Ostrołęka with 700 horse of his brigade, composed chiefly of Greater Poles, first north to Mława, and then southwards, moving along the new Prussian border[^68].

The impression made by this step throughout the country was extraordinary. The carrying out of the reduction suddenly stalled. General

[26] <!-- p.26 -->

Igelström lost his head and for a long time could not reach a decision on action. At last he sent two small columns in pursuit of Madaliński, but they did not manage to overtake him. Madaliński soon crossed the border of South Prussia, and in the night of 14 to 15 March attacked at Szreńsk [Schrensk] a post of Prussian hussars, scattering it and seizing the cash box. He then turned towards the Vistula, and having attacked the Prussians again at Wyszogród on the night of 16 to 17 March, crossed the river the following night below that town, went on along the Bzura, and then towards the Pilica[^69].

Command over the Prussian army in South Prussia was then held by the governor of Toruń up to that time, Lieutenant-General Count Schwerin. His headquarters was at Piotrków. At first, in consequence of information received from Buchholtz, he thought that Madaliński intended to enter Prussian service; only the entry of the Greater Poland brigade onto Prussian territory and the events at Szreńsk changed the whole aspect of matters. Schwerin therefore sent several detachments to stop Madaliński; they fought feeble skirmishes with him near Skierniewice [Skiernewitz] and on the Pilica, but did not manage to stop him. When, after crossing the Pilica, Madaliński halted at Opoczno, Schwerin supposed that he intended to reinforce his detachment there in order afterwards to break into South Prussia once more. For rumours had appeared that on 25 March an uprising, allegedly long prepared, would break out in South Prussia, and Schwerin regarded Madaliński as its commander. Madaliński, however, after a rest at Opoczno, went on further south, into the Kraków region[^70].

The news of Madaliński's entry onto Prussian territory caused lively agitation in the Berlin Cabinet. Fear arose for the security of South Prussia, in which until then there had been stationed in the more important towns

[27] <!-- p.27 -->

and at certain militarily important points, apart from a few infantry detachments drawn from West Prussia, only 8 battalions and 30 squadrons, as well as 1 horse battery. To reinforce this inadequate garrisoning, Lieutenant-General Schwerin was to draw in, according to the instruction of 20 March, as many troops from East Prussia, West Prussia and Silesia as he should consider necessary to secure that province, and moreover, in order to achieve this security the better, he was to move troops into the Commonwealth and occupy its territory from the Vistula as far as the Narew. In this last case the Prussian king would be committing a new act of lawlessness towards the Poles, though this lawlessness had long been foreseen. Already at the time of the Second Partition, Russia, Austria and Prussia had jointly resolved to occupy the country in the event of disturbances in Poland; to Prussia fell the observation of the entire course of the Vistula and the greater part of Samogitia. At this opportune moment, the violation of the territory of the Commonwealth by Prussian troops was now announced by Buchholtz's diplomatic note of 24 March, sent to the Permanent Council. In it Buchholtz complained of the violation of Prussian territory by Madaliński and reported that the Prussian king, to secure his borders, would draw a cordon from Zakroczym to Ostrołęka. In reply the Permanent Council expressed the hope that the entry of Prussian troops into Poland did not signify a lasting occupation, and that the Prussians would make good the damage caused in connection with this entry[^71].

As a result of the instruction received, Lieutenant-General Schwerin carried out the following changes in the disposition of the Prussian troops along the eastern Prussian border. From the area south of the Vistula, from Silesia, he sent Eisenhart's Jäger battalion from Namysłów [Namslau] to Mysłowice [Myslowitz], and Hanenfeldt's grenadier battalion from Nysa [Neisse] to

Tarnowice [Tarnowitz] and the vicinity of Bytom [Beuthen]; from Brzeg [Brieg] to Woischnik (?) There were likewise regroupings of troops north of the Vistula. Lieutenant-General Wolki concentrated on 27 March at Dobrzyń Thiele's fusilier battalion, 5 squadrons of Brückner's dragoon regiment and 3 squadrons of the regiment bearing his own name, and having crossed the South Prussian border on 1 April, reached Pułtusk and its environs 5 days later and remained there. Lieutenant-General Wildau united at Nidbork [Neidenburg] 4 squadrons of dragoons from Werther's regiment, Frankenberg's dragoon regiment, and the infantry regiment bearing his own name, and occupied the border at Mława. Major-General Günther, with his Bosnian regiment, which stood as garrison on the East Prussian border, and Rembow's Jäger battalion, set out from Königsberg towards the Narew to secure the sector between the mouths of the Omulew and the Bóbr. Favrat's infantry regiment, stationed at Brunsberg [Braunsberg], together with 1 regiment of grenadiers and 1 battalion, occupied the Vistula at Płock, Wyszogród and Zakroczym, and also took possession of the small town of Szreńsk. Bonin's infantry regiment, quartered at Grudziądz [Graudenz], was moved together with half a regiment of grenadiers and 1 battalion to Toruń, while with half a battalion it occupied the Drwęca [Drewenz] between Lubicz [Leibitsch] and Brodnica [Strasburg]. The rest of Wolki's hussar regiment took up position at Dobrzyń. General Brüneck, who held the supreme command in East Prussia, moved, to cover the treasuries

at Gąbin [Gumbinnen] (East Prussia), Bardeleben's dragoon regiment from Wystruć [Insterburg] to Stołupiany [Stallupönen] and Piłkały[^72] (Pillkallen).

Meanwhile, in Kraków, under the influence of Madaliński's movement, decisive events took place. To the city, which had been freed of its Russian garrison as a result of the concentration of all Russian forces ordered by Igelström, Kościuszko, who had been hiding in the vicinity, came on 23 March, and on the following day proclaimed the official Insurrection Act[^73]. The aim of the armed struggle became "the liberation of Poland from the foreign soldier, the restoration and securing of the integrity of her borders, the extirpation of all violence and usurpation, foreign as well as domestic, the consolidation of national liberty and of the independence of the Commonwealth"[^74]. The Act was directed against Russia and Prussia alike. In its second, dispositive part it contained the organisation of the insurgent government. This government was represented by Tadeusz Kościuszko as "Supreme and sole Commander" and "administrator" of the entire armed movement, and by the Supreme National Council, appointed and organised by him. In the voivodeships the citizens were to form local Commissions of Order. A criminal court, composed of members appointed by the Supreme Council, was to punish with death crimes against the nation, and acts contrary to the aims of the insurrection[^75].

For some days after the Act of Kraków Kościuszko did not leave Kraków; he was occupied with military matters. He set out only on 1 April and near the village of Luborzyca joined some of the Polish detachments, among others the brigade of Madaliński. The armed force numbered at first some 4,000 men, but it soon grew, owing to the peasants from the surrounding villages brought in by a member of the Kraków Commission of Order. At Racławice on 4 April a clash with the Russian army took place; Kościuszko, with the help of the peasant

[30] <!-- p.30 -->

masses, scattered the corps of General Tormassow. For the national cause this victory was of great significance, for it brought enthusiasm into the insurgent ranks, deepened confidence in the commander-in-chief, and above all cleared the way for a swifter spread of the insurrection[^76].

For at the beginning of the armed movement insurrectionary enthusiasm had not shown itself decisively. Let the conduct of the inhabitants of Mazovia in the areas along the Narew serve as an example.

At the news of Madaliński's march, his ranks were reinforced only by a few representatives of the petty nobility, while the great landowners, in contrast to their wives, who sincerely favoured the uprising, preferred to sit quietly on their lands. Among the common people national awareness still lay deeply dormant beneath the cover of their own misery. When Prussian troops occupied this territory, the hitherto downtrodden peasant rather expected relief in his lot from the invader. Among the burghers there, the only opponents of the Prussians were those whose commercial interests had suffered as a result of the war. The Jews, having their own advantage in view, took the side of the occupiers, whom they considered the stronger. Only the clergy proved a determined enemy of Prussia[^77].

Things began to go differently after the victory at Racławice.

The organisation of the uprising in Chełm Land and in the Lublin voivodeship followed. Most momentous, however, was the outbreak of the insurrection in the capital. The Warsaw conspirators, having learned of Igelström's planned stroke against the arsenal, hastened the outbreak of the uprising to the night of 17 to 18 April. In the struggle with the Russian troops and the Prussians reinforcing them

[31] <!-- p.31 -->

from the corps of General Wolki, the enemy was driven out of Warsaw. The new authority became the Provisional Deputy Council, headed by the newly elected president of the capital, Ignacy Zakrzewski. General Mokronowski became commandant of the city. On 19 April the inhabitants of the Duchy of Mazovia, together with the king, declared their accession to the uprising. The example of Warsaw was soon followed by Vilnius, which, on the night of 22 to 23 April, under the leadership of Jasiński, removed the Russian garrison from the city and created its own insurgent authorities[^78].

Only Greater Poland did not stir. In 1793, when the secret societies arose, this region too was embraced by the conspiracy. Among the agitators active there were Gliszczyński, and also Wybicki, who found fertile ground for his work among the citizenry of Poznań, Gniezno and Kalisz. After all, both the chief leader of the conspirators, Ignacy Działyński, and President Zakrzewski were natives of Greater Poland. Nevertheless the insurrection was to break out there later than elsewhere, although the inhabitants of that land declared their solidarity with the Act of Kraków and felt themselves bound to fight in defence of the fatherland. What held them back from active action was the scattering of Prussian troops about the country, and above all Kościuszko's policy towards his western neighbour[^79].

At first Kościuszko, as the future Commander, was undoubtedly resolved to act against the Prussians, when in mid-September 1793 he is said to have spoken these words to the conspirators: "I myself shall strike at Piotrków, at Möllendorff, with the troops that are between Warsaw and Kraków, and having cleared Greater Poland I shall turn towards Warsaw[^80]...". Fear, however, of inadequate preparation of the uprising did not then permit Kościuszko to carry out this intention, although the chances of success were considerable in view of

[32] <!-- p.32 -->

the small number of Prussian troops in the newly annexed territory. The Act of Kraków of 24 March, as we know, proclaimed a settling of accounts by force of arms with Frederick William II, when it expressed indignation at the falsehood, hypocrisy and deceit of Prussia and Russia and announced the restoration to the fatherland of its integrity and independence. To carry out that announcement, however, a struggle on two fronts was necessary. Yet Kościuszko, although he was devoted with his whole heart to the cause of liberty, would gladly have avoided that struggle. For that reason, no doubt, he passed over the best opportunity of striking at the Prussians right at the beginning of the uprising, when their mobilisation was only in its initial stage and when Lieutenant-General Schwerin felt himself so weak that he did not dare to heed the repeated summonses of Igelström and of the War College in Berlin to attack Kościuszko, in conjunction with the Russian forces stationed west of the Vistula, and crush the uprising in the bud. Soon the situation became so unfavourable for the Commander (his being pushed into a corner by the Vistula) that he could act in no other way than by proclamations. He then abandoned the idea of fighting for the western borders of the Commonwealth, and the work of the emissaries, supposedly already dispatched to the Sieradz and Łęczyca, Kalisz and Poznań regions in order to extend the movement in those parts and to carry it over into Silesia, could for the time being produce no result[^81].

The policy of the Supreme Commander towards Prussia is characterised most eloquently by the instructions he gave to the president of Warsaw, Zakrzewski. He ordered him, namely, to come to an understanding with Buchholtz in secret and to declare that it was "only a war declared on him for policy's sake", and that therefore "let his Court give peace to our borders, and we shall not touch him[^82]". And indeed negotiations on this matter with the Prussian envoy went on throughout May. Hence, out of regard for Kościuszko's intentions,

[33] <!-- p.33 -->

efforts were made in Warsaw to prevent Buchholtz from leaving, when he received from Berlin an order to quit the borders of the Commonwealth. Unfortunately it was not suspected that detaining him suited the Prussian king down to the ground. For the order was only a formality, and Frederick William secretly instructed Buchholtz to remain in Poland for some time yet and to conduct negotiations, so that in the meantime Prussia might arm itself properly. For this reason, therefore, Buchholtz remained in Warsaw until 23 June, although he did not cease to complain, falsely, of being detained. Throughout this time he beguiled the Polish diplomats, with Dzieduszycki at their head, with the favourable attitude of his court[^83].

President Zakrzewski himself took a different view of the relation of the insurrection to Prussia. A thoroughgoing patriot, he wished to extend the uprising to that region as well. For that reason he advised Kościuszko to send Colonel Sokolnicki there, who was ready to undertake the task, and as proof of the favourable prospects of the cause he cited the small number of Prussian troops in Greater Poland. In a letter of 12 May he wrote thus to Kościuszko: "There have come to me from the Greater Poland voivodeships delegates with an assured and settled plan of our uprising; these I shall send to You in person, Commander of the nation's rising, for they are worthy both of Your trust and of Your orders, and I assure You that they will not fail"[^84]. Kościuszko, guided, as is known, by a different policy towards Prussia, did not avail himself of the services of his brethren from Greater Poland. On 22 May the delegation, with the chamberlain Moskorzewski at its head, returned to their family seats, bringing with them no signal for the immediate beginning of the uprising[^85].

Meanwhile the Prussians' preparations for war pointed unmistakably to their hostile intentions towards the insurrection. Lieutenant-General Favrat, deputising for Schwerin, who had been on leave since the middle of

[34] <!-- p.34 -->

April, was accelerating the mobilisation, which until then, because of difficulties with equipment, had proceeded at a very slow pace. He contributed to the establishment in Poznań of a field war commissariat, and, anticipating a possible offensive against Kościuszko, he ordered the magazines to be filled and pushed forward. For the same reasons he drew from Nysa into the vicinity of Bytom the remainder of Hanenfeldt's infantry regiment, and from Reichenbach Schulz's Jäger battalion. He then concentrated near Łowicz and Częstochowa the corps that until then had been scattered along the left bank of the Vistula over a distance of 40 miles, so as, in the event of an attack by the insurgents on South Prussia, to hold them back until the mobilisation was completed and reinforcements had arrived[^86].

Soon the Prussians' preparations for war were nearing completion. During May and at the beginning of June reinforcements arrived. From Silesia came Pellet's Jäger battalion and Dolfs's cuirassier regiment; from the Mark the infantry regiments of Lichnowski, Jung-Schwerin and Frankenberg; from Pomerania the infantry regiment of Klinkowström and the dragoons of Bieberstein; from West Prussia the infantry regiments of Amandrüz and Bonin; Anhalt's infantry regiment left Poznań and Gniezno and joined Favrat's field army, and to garrison the two Greater Poland cities Kunheim's regiment came up from Frankfurt an der Oder[^87].

At the beginning of June the disposition of the Prussian army assigned to cover the borders and to take the offensive against the Poles was as follows: South of the Vistula, on the Pilica, stood Lieutenant-General Favrat with 17 battalions, 27 squadrons and 2½ batteries. Major-General Rüts, with 7 battalions and a cavalry detachment totalling 1,300 horse, guarded the Upper Silesian border. To cover the magazines there stood at Piotrków 3 battalions, and at Częstochowa 1 battalion and 2 squadrons

[35] <!-- p.35 -->

. With the exception of 4 battalions from Rüts's corps, this entire force was already mobilised. In addition, the following still unmobilised units stood at the main centres: Lichnowski's regiment with a grenadier battalion at Poznań, with its 1st battalion at Wieluń, with its second at Częstochowa; Jung-Schwerin's regiment with a grenadier battalion at Piotrków, with its 1st battalion at Łowicz, its 2nd at Sędzin [Translator's note: probably the village in Kuyavia; author to confirm.] and Koło; Frankenberg's depot battalion (Depot-Bataillon) at Kalisz and Krotoszyn [Krotoschin]. General Bonin, with 8 battalions, watched the Prussian border against the Poles. He also exercised command over the Russian troops who had fled from Warsaw.

The Prussian forces north of the Vistula, on the Narew, were at first commanded by Generals Wildau, Günther and Wolki, who skirmished from time to time with the insurgents. In the second half of May the place of all these commanders was taken by Lieutenant-General v. Schönfeldt. His corps numbered 12 battalions, 32 squadrons and 1½ batteries.

In East Prussia, near Stołupiany, stood General Brüneck at the head of 7 battalions and 20 squadrons.

In the theatre of operations there were magazines: principal ones at Częstochowa, Piotrków and Płock, and staging magazines, for a period of 4 weeks, at Sieradz and Łęczyca. At the end of May the magazines and field bakeries intended to supply Rüts's corps were moved forward to the vicinity of Katowice [Kattowitz] and to Bytom; those intended for Favrat's corps, to Siewierz, Żarki and Koniecpol. Bonin's corps and the Russian troops subordinate to him had a magazine at Łowicz at their disposal. For Schönfeldt's corps a staging magazine was established at Zakroczym[^88].

Soon the movements of the Prussian army were to be directed more closely by King Frederick William II himself.

On 12 May he decided to set out for Poland. Hitherto an ardent champion of the struggle against revolutionary France, he yielded to the

[36] <!-- p.36 -->

persuasions of Lucchesini and of his adjutant, Colonel Manstein, to reach for the easier booty in Poland. Having left Berlin on 14 May, the Prussian king arrived two days later in Poznań and took up residence in the suburb of Kundorf, in the small house of a citizen of Poznań, Klug. On the same day there appeared, issued in Poznań, a proclamation summoning Prussian subjects in Polish service to enter the Prussian ranks. After a stay of nearly two weeks the king left Poznań and, accompanied by the crown prince and Prince Ludwik Wirtemberski, proceeded via Wrocław [Breslau] and Lubliniec [Lublinitz] to Favrat's army[^89].

That same Favrat, in May, when Kościuszko was occupied at the camp near Połaniec with organising an armed force, was planning an offensive on Kraków. He had been entertaining the intention for a long time, as the measures he carried out immediately after taking over the command would suggest. He even made the first moves, but owing to the slow movements of his troops and the impossibility of coming to an understanding with the Russian general Denisov, who was to support him in the whole undertaking, he soon abandoned the march on Kraków. He fought only a skirmish with the insurgent forces, on 18 May at Skała, the farthest point to which he advanced on this march, but two days later he began a retreat towards his former positions on the Pilica. From the strategic point of view the undertaking of this action must meet with criticism. For although the possession of Kraków was important to Kościuszko, since he drew his manpower from there, yet the loss of that city could not have been decisive for his further conduct of the struggle[^90].

Although in May no cooperation between the Prussians and the Russians came about, it was now only a matter of a few days. Denisov, after unsuccessful attacks on

[37] <!-- p.37 -->

Kościuszko's camp at Połaniec, was marching towards the Prussian border in order, on Igelström's orders, to join the Prussians. Having taken up a position at Szczekociny, he called on Favrat to strike jointly at Kościuszko, who was following in the Russians' footsteps. He met, however, with a refusal. Favrat, although at bottom he acknowledged the justice of Denisov's intentions, remained passive, contenting himself with moving from the Pilica to Żarnowiec. For he knew that the king was coming to his camp, and wished to leave to him the laurels of victory.

Yet even the king, after arriving on 3 June at Favrat's camp at Wola near Żarnowiec, did not at once resolve to attack Kościuszko. He wished first to await the arrival of the horse battery and at the same time to induce his opponent to send part of his troops back towards Warsaw, so as to defeat him the more easily once weakened.

To this end, General Frankenberg with the crown prince's corps was to march towards Warsaw on 8 June, while General Schönfeldt was to begin preparations for crossing the Vistula, in order to create in Kościuszko the impression that the enemy intended to strike at the capital. The manoeuvre failed, since Kościuszko had already decided earlier on the movements of his army. On 5 June news suddenly reached the royal quarters at Wola that the insurgent troops had begun a march on Denisov's position and had forced his advance guard to retreat. The Prussian king could therefore no longer delay. Fearing that the Poles might defeat Denisov, he immediately issued orders for a march towards Szczekociny. The battle was fought the next day. Kościuszko took the field with his 14,000-strong corps, of which roughly ⅓ were peasants, facing an opponent superior to him by 12½ thousand[^91]. In such conditions the encounter ended in a disastrous defeat. The insurgents' loss amounted, by Kościuszko's own estimate

[38] <!-- p.38 -->

, to 1,000 men killed and wounded, and 8 guns[^92]. To this must be added the prisoners, mostly wounded, whom the Prussians counted at 500 and of whom Kościuszko makes no mention[^93].

The defeat at Szczekociny finally revealed the true intentions of the Prussian king. The mere fact of a hostile move by the Prussians could not have surprised Kościuszko. He had, after all, received from time to time diplomatic and military warnings to this effect, from within the country and from abroad; he had not supposed, however, that the two enemy armies would join so early. The Commander rather believed, taking into account the skirmishes with the Prussians, weak thus far and not sufficiently determined, that Frederick William would continue to hang back, and that he himself would meanwhile defeat the nearest Russian corps, that of General Denisov. Having accomplished that, he hoped to come to terms with the Prussians alone, and as a last resort to beat them. These intentions of Kościuszko's, justified though they were, were nevertheless obstructed by the slowness of the insurrectionary troops' movements. It was necessary to act quickly, which was possible only with the use of efficient regular forces. Meanwhile Kościuszko, leading with him a mass of peasantry, was forced to move so slowly that Denisov, withdrawing from Połaniec, drew near to the Prussians[^94].

After the defeat Kościuszko fell back in the direction of Warsaw, in this case the best base of operations[^95].

On 10 June he issued from the camp at Kielce an order by virtue of which he directed the commanders of the corps of line troops to advance into Prussian and Muscovite territory and to call the oppressed people to rise. At the same time, by the same order, he commanded the leaders of the levy en masse "to lead at once either volunteers or the border country people... into the lands torn from

[39] <!-- p.39 -->

the Polish Commonwealth, and afterwards also into the lands of the former Prussian and Muscovite possession, and to extend a hand everywhere to the inhabitants who wish to return to the sweet liberties of their own fatherland or who wish to win a free fatherland"[^96].

Two days after Kościuszko's by now official move against the Prussians, on 12 June, there appeared an appeal of the Supreme National Council "to the citizens of the Greater Poland province" calling for a rising. Depicting emphatically the falsehood, the hypocrisy and the unparalleled greed of the Prussian king, it declared the Prussian annexations "unlawful" and the voivodeships and lands of the Greater Poland province "indivisible parts of the Commonwealth"; it called traitors to the fatherland, and resolved to punish, those "who would insinuate any subjection to the unlawful power of appropriators and invaders"[^97].

Yet it was not then that the "revolt" flared up in Greater Poland; garrisons of Prussian troops that were still fairly strong stood in its way. Only later, after this region had been stripped of Prussian troops, which were used partly for the siege of Warsaw and partly on the Rhine, did the appeals of the Supreme National Council turn into deed[^98].

## Chapter III

### THE SPREAD OF THE INSURRECTION INTO GREATER POLAND — THE INSURGENTS' ARMED MOVEMENTS AND DĄBROWSKI'S EXPEDITION TO GREATER POLAND

The events after the defeat at Szczekociny were not favourable to the insurgents. General Zajączek suffered defeat on 8 June at Chełm [Cholm], inflicted on him by the Russian general Derfelden. A week later came the capitulation of Kraków [Krakau]. The commandant of the city, Colonel Wieniawski, without having exhausted the means of defence, surrendered the city to the Prussians, and thereby Kościuszko's design collapsed, which was to hand Kraków over in deposit to the Austrians, whom he regarded as the best of the three partitioning powers[^99]. The unfortunate capitulation soon had its epilogue in the events in Warsaw. The people, stirred up on this account, carried out a lynching of the Targowica confederates. A further series of reverses was the unsuccessful expedition of Major Liberadzki to Volhynia[^100].

At this time only the rising in Lithuania, Courland[^102] and Samogitia[^103] was achieving successes. Soon the most important event became the siege of the capital.

The victorious Prusso-Russian troops from Szczekociny were making their way there. Their march, however, by the road through Kielce, Końskie and Opoczno, with numerous rests, proceeded with such slowness that the Prussian king's original intentions, to forestall Kościuszko in the march toward Warsaw, came to nothing. For when Kościuszko had already on 28 June taken up positions at that city, the allies were to cross the Pilica only between 30 June and 2 July. Earlier still the Prussians had occupied the Bzura, so that armed insurrectionary detachments should not break through it into Greater Poland. The Prussian crown prince took up guard over this river until such time as the main army should advance closer to Warsaw, and on 27 June he established an entrenched camp at Potoki near Łowicz[^104].

In the first half of July the Prussians and Russians drew up before the earthworks of Warsaw. The siege began, lasting over two months, from 13 July to 6 September.

After the Prussians' capture of Wola on 27 July, a period of little activity followed among the besiegers, while the opposing side for its part tried to act offensively[^105]. The Poles undertook a number of sorties against the enemy positions, and even tried to break through the cordon of troops surrounding the capital. In this last case, however, the attempts missed their mark. On the one hand there was a lack of capable leaders, and on the other the undertaking itself proved extraordinarily difficult. The attempts to cross the Narew and to spread the rising in those parts were renewed

[42] <!-- p.42 -->

as well, but only at Rzewnie and Słońsk were any gains achieved. Less often now was it intended to get through the division of Gen. Wolka, which guarded the ground between Zakroczym and Zegrze and formed part of the right wing of Schönfeldt's corps[^106]. One of these plans was Madaliński's expedition. On 24 August Madaliński wanted to cross the Narew between Dęby and Zegrze, in order afterwards to cross the Vistula and cut his way through to Greater Poland. The Prussians, however, put up such strong resistance to him that he had to fall back[^107].

Completely successful, however, was Bielamowski's reconnaissance, which this lieutenant, from Rymkiewicz's detachment, undertook around the middle of August. He set out from Warsaw with 40 horse from Dąbrowski's Mazur regiment by way of Buraków and the Kampinos Forest, and, keeping constantly westward by byways, reached as far as the Bzura and beyond, to Gąbin [Gombin] and Włocławek [Leslau]. This bold deed alarmed the Prussian officials, since Bielamowski in several instances took the cash boxes of post offices, excise offices and salt depots, and even intercepted a transport of money intended for the pay of the Prussian troops before Warsaw. To render Bielamowski harmless, a Prussian detachment was sent out from before Warsaw. Bielamowski, aware of the impossibility of holding out with his weak little detachment against the Prussians pursuing him, and seeing no possibility either of effectively supporting the armed force that was to be formed in Greater Poland, returned safely to the capital by the same road. To Kościuszko he brought the news of the insufficient number of Prussian troops in those parts. In the entourage of the Prussian king Bielamowski's expedition aroused

[43] <!-- p.43 -->

apprehension for the rear of the besieging army. It turned out, namely, that the weak Prussian garrisons in Greater Poland might not be able to cope in the event of possible insurgent movements there[^108].

These apprehensions were not groundless. In Greater Poland a rising soon broke out, which was later to cause the Prussian king to withdraw from before Warsaw, and to inspire the besieged with new zeal.

Already in July the starosta of Śrem [Schrimm], Niemojewski, and the chamberlain Moskorzewski, sent as a delegation to Warsaw, began after their return, in accordance with the instructions, letters and proclamations of the Commander which they had brought with them, lively agitation, calling everywhere for taking up arms. They were assisted in this action by the women citizens of that region. Some of them carried about patents for voivodeship generals, colonels and majors, others, like Droszewska from the vicinity of Leszno, niece of General Madaliński, went from house to house and informed people in secret of the outbreak of the rising that was about to take place[^109].

The moment was opportune. In South Prussia the Prussians had only 8,000 soldiers, distributed in the more important centres, whose purpose was to maintain communication between the army operating in Poland and the rest of the Prussian lands; the main forces had already been directed either to the Rhine or to Warsaw. This slender quantity of Prussian troops in the second annexation was strengthened by reinforcements that arrived in the middle of August. These were the infantry regiment Götze, sent from Silesia under Gen. Manstein, and further detachments of the cuirassier regiments Dolfs and Manstein, and another cuirassier regiment, that of Prince Ludwik Wirtemberski, dispatched from Pomerania. This entire force amounted to 1,500 men[^110].

In the second half of August the torch of "rebellion" was raised. The armed movement was admittedly very weak

[44] <!-- p.44 -->

and often flagged, yet it must be remembered that the Greater Poles were for the time being dependent only on their own forces. For the help of regular troops promised by Kościuszko they were to wait for quite a long time yet, since as long as the enemy besieged Warsaw it was impossible to send it.

Nevertheless, small detachments of partisans were more than once able to harass the Prussians[^111].

The first in Greater Poland to raise the standard of the rising was the castellan of Kuyavia, Dionizy Mniewski. On 20 August, with a detachment of Kuyavian partisans, he attacked the Prussian posts at Brześć Kujawski [Brest-Kujawski], destroyed them and took possession of the town treasuries. There fell then "the valiant citizen Skarbek", and "Trzebuchowski, deputy for Kuyavia, made a colonel of the rising, and Wichrowski and Kosiński, citizens, contributed much to this rising"[^112]. From Brześć Mniewski hastened to Włocławek and halted there on 22 August. In driving out the town's garrison, Lieutenant Marwitz of Alt-Pfuhl's regiment and several soldiers fell. An hour later 11 boats with ammunition, intended for the Prussian besieging army, came up the Vistula from Grudziądz. Taking advantage of the opportunity, Mniewski with his detachment struck at the Prussians escorting the boats. The attack succeeded. Thirteen Prussians fell, and the whole transport fell into the insurgents' hands. Being unable to use all the ammunition, the Poles sank the greater part of it. For themselves they kept 100 barrels of powder, of which, however, 3 blew up. After accomplishing so bold a deed, Mniewski's insurgents[^113] made camp near Włocławek on the road leading to Brześć. According to further reports carried by the newspaper then appearing in Poznań, the "Südpreussische Zeitung", an entrenched camp of the Kuyavian insurgents was also supposed to be at Radziejów, from where through the surrounding country as far as Toruń there spread

circulars signed by Mniewski and dated at Nieszawa. In the same newspaper there appeared at the same time a report of the appearance in the forest near Rypin in Dobrzyń Land of 100 armed peasants under the leadership of Zieliński, a native of the village of Skąpe. Lieutenant Branky, who had set out against them, was captured, whereupon the treasuries in Rypin fell into the hands of the insurgents[^114].

While these things were happening in Kuyavia, the inhabitants of the other voivodeships likewise set about action.

In the Poznań voivodeship, citizens such as Hipolit Rogaliński, Józef Niemojewski, Antoni Gliszczyński, Józef Miaskowski, Antoni Wyganowski, Hieronim Jaraczewski, Wojciech Sadowski, Sebastian Bieńkowski, Ireneusz and Bonawentura Koczorowski, Piotr Zaremba and Jan Zembrzuski gathered on 21 August at a general assembly in Kościan, resolving to act at once. First they seized the Prussian magazine in Śrem and set up an insurgent camp near that town. From there, 600 horse strong, they moved towards Rąbiń[^115], where they drew up and swore the insurrection act. At the same time they issued a proclamation calling on the peasants to join their ranks, promising each, as a reward, a reduction by half of the dues to the lord. At Rąbiń, too, members were appointed to the insurgent authorities for the Poznań voivodeship, that is, to the Commission of Order and the criminal court. The first of these consisted of: Celestyn Sokolnicki, Józef Jaraczewski, Hipolit Rogaliński, Antoni Wyganowski, Antoni Gliszczyński, Jan Zembrzuski, Józef Sczaniecki, Piotr Zaremba, Rev. Śmigielski, Ignacy Kurowski, Hieronim Jaraczewski and Stanisław Mycielski. The criminal court was made up of: Wojciech Węgorzewski, Józef Miaskowski, Ireneusz Koczorowski, Faustyn Zakrzewski, Eustachy Kowalski, Ignacy

Wilczyński, Wit Pomorski, Antoni Żółtowski. From Rąbiń the insurgents moved under Niemojewski, chosen general for the Poznań voivodeship, to Śmigiel [Schmiegel], where the inhabitants declared for the uprising and handed over whatever arms each had. From there they proceeded in turn to Racot, whence their detachment[^116] then set out for Kościan and there, on 31 August[^117], fell by surprise upon the already mentioned General Manstein, who had arrived with 150 horse[^118] and, having garrisoned Leszno and Wschowa, was marching on Poznań. During the attack the insurgents captured the general's adjutant and took 6 horses. But Manstein soon recovered the adjutant, and the insurgents had to fall back before him with a loss of 16 men and made for the village of Mchy[^119]. Manstein, who had lost 5 men in the skirmish and had 20 wounded, did not continue his march but set out on the return road to Leszno and Wschowa. In the palace of the Sułkowski princes at Rydzyna [Reisen], near Leszno, all the arms were confiscated on his orders on 1 September, namely 6 large cannon, 1 small cannon and 31 old rifles, 9 sabres, 4 rapiers, pistols, etc.[^120].

Independently of the insurgents who had gathered under Niemojewski's leadership, other armed detachments were also active in the Poznań voivodeship. Thus, according to the memoir of Adam Turno, there assembled on 22 August[^121] in the village of Morków in the Leszno district "some 2,000 of the people, infantry for the most part armed with scythes, and cavalry of nearly 200 horse". Unfortunately, however, the commander of the insurgents, Bolesz, proved a most imprudent man. "Having taken up quarters in the manor house with all the citizens, and having set no picket or guard even on the chimney, he ate and drank in peace. Following the commandant's example the peasantry too "drank on the highway, having stood their scythes in the stable in the yard". Such carelessness soon brought disastrous

[47] <!-- p.47 -->

consequences in its train. Unexpectedly there arrived at Morków the detachment of Czettritz's hussars, subordinate to Manstein, who took away the scythes, wounded 8 insurgents and took 30 prisoner. "The commandant with the other gentlemen" saved himself by flight through the windows. Only the village head from Koronowo, who stood with his men on a hill beyond the village, suffered no loss and withdrew safely; the rest of the insurgents were dispersed[^122].

Another insurgent episode took place in the district of Rawicz, at Jutrosin. The armed movement in that small town began through the doing of the mayor Walenty Zatorski, the parish priest Gogolewski, Skoroszewski, the leaseholder of the parish estate at Dubin, and a Mr Budziszewski. On 21 August, for 1 o'clock in the afternoon, Mayor Zatorski summoned the burghers to the town hall, whereupon he ordered them to assemble in the market square and there await the arrival of Skoroszewski's detachment of insurgents, with which they were to unite and march on without delay. Whoever had no rifle was to stand at the place of assembly armed with a scythe, a pike or a pitchfork, and each was obliged to provide himself with food for three days. On 24 August a certain Umiński arrived in the town at the head of a detachment of 24 men and 4 wagons, took down the Prussian eagles and took, against receipts, a small amount of money left in the town treasury. He forbade the Prussian officials, on pain of death, to communicate with the authorities. The insurgents, however, were not masters of the little town for long. From Rawicz there came up Colonel Dossow, who, having just come from Silesia at the head of the 2nd battalion of the Götze regiment and 2½ squadrons of Dolfs cuirassiers, was marching by way of Rawicz and Zduny to Kalisz to cover the magazines there[^123], and he began a search for the accused. The mayor was betrayed by his own farmhand, who, when seized, confessed that on his orders he had carried scythes

[48] <!-- p.48 -->

to the blacksmith. Nor did Father Gogolewski, hidden from them in a cupboard, escape the Prussians[^124].

In the Gniezno voivodeship, in the part that came under Prussian rule in 1793[^125], the most zealous propagators of the idea of insurrection were Józef Lipski, lord of Czerniejewo[^126], and the Rogoziński brothers. The nobility from the neighbourhood of Gniezno declared for the fight against the Prussians, and succeeded in winning over even the peasants of that area and part of the burghers of Gniezno. On 22 August the insurgents arrived at the assembly point at Gniezno and the next day attacked the Prussian garrison, numbering about 210 men, and disarmed it. At the same time the Prussian emblems were removed from the official buildings, after which the citizens assembled in the town hall swore the insurrection act. Seated on the commission chosen for civil and criminal matters were: the castellan Walenty Godzimirski, elected chairman against his will and in the end removed as suspect; Korytowski, who succeeded him in that office; Józef Jaraczewski; the chamberlain Piotr Kaliszowski; the canons Loga, Skiwski and Murzynowski (the last soon expelled); Colonel Twardowski; the Protestant clergymen Bothe, Schocken, Koch and Hanke; the burghers Gostomski and Kłosowski; and several peasants[^127]. The town hall became the seat of the commission, from which appeals went out over the whole district calling the population to arms. At this call peasants armed with scythes and pikes began to stream in from all sides. The monastery of Wągrowiec [Wongrowitz] alone supplied 100 peasants. Near the village of Winiary[^128] they set up a camp[^129], where the sense of discipline was instilled in the masses with great diligence. Unfortunately, however, right at the very start, the camp did not escape quarrels. Lipski, the commander of the armed force, having fallen out with the Rogozińskis, all but cut down one of the brothers. The peasantry, again, rose against the nobility, regarding some of them, such as, for example,

[49] <!-- p.49 -->

Count Wołłowicz of Witkowo, as traitors. The dissensions were all the more deserving of condemnation because the arrival of Prussian troops from Poznań was expected at any moment. To prevent the German officials from reporting everything that had happened to their superiors in Poznań, all the Prussian officials were imprisoned on 24 August in the Franciscan monastery[^130]. On 3 September the commandant of Poznań, Colonel Diether, came up with one battalion, three squadrons and as many guns. The insurgents[^131], with whom the Kalisz and Poznań voivodeships under Niemojewski had by that time already joined, withdrew before the Prussians in the direction of Słupca. For there the union of all the voivodeship risings was to take place. In the haste with which the departure was made, 2 cannon and part of the magazine were left behind in Gniezno, but, most important of all, the order to withdraw the post at the Franciscan monastery was forgotten. Made up of 30 peasants, it at first would not give way, but in the end fled. Only two peasants from Żerniki[^132] held out to the last with scythes in their hands, until they fell to enemy bullets. In recognition of their sacrifice the Poles later laid their bodies in the cemetery of the Franciscan monastery near the tomb of the Blessed Yolanda. Col. Diether, having carried out a search for arms in the monastery and set the Prussian emblems up again in the town, marched back to Poznań. The released officials went with him[^133].

We must now sketch in turn the early history of the insurrection in the south-eastern part of Greater Poland, in the neighbourhood of the upper Warta and Prosna.

In the Sieradz voivodeship, after consultations among the more eminent citizens at the house of the starosta of Brodnica, Ignacy Błeszyński, in Złoczów, they assembled in arms on 23 August in the forest near Sieradz. Those who

[50] <!-- p.50 -->

had no firearms came supplied with iron rails, beaten flat in the shape of broadswords. On the following day, at one o'clock at night, after prior agreement with the inhabitants of Sieradz, they fell unexpectedly upon the Prussian garrison in the town, which consisted of 46 soldiers.

The surprised Prussians shut themselves up in the town hall, but after an hour's resistance they surrendered, losing to the victors a rich magazine and a cash box containing 700 thalers. The prisoners taken, after swearing that they would never take up arms against the Poles, were sent by the insurgents, together with the Prussian officials, to the Silesian border[^134]. On the same day Lipski and Zbijewski were elected land generals, the Rev. Prelate Ostrowski, who had arrived at the insurgent camp with a detachment of 20 men, was made a rotmistrz (cavalry captain), and a civil-military commission and a criminal court were called into being. An expedition to Łęczyca undertaken on 6 September to attack the garrison there did not succeed, and the reserve of 50 men left behind in Sieradz for that time had to retreat before the troops of Major Bonin, who was leading from Silesia a grenadier battalion from Pfuhl's regiment to cover the magazines at Sieradz and Kalisz [^135]. Bonin took cruel revenge on the people of Sieradz for their joining the rising.

For he plundered the town and the surrounding country, then set out toward Piotrków, and, having received a relief force of 150 infantry and 800 cavalry, turned through Sieradz toward Kalisz. On the way he seized the castellan of Wieluń, Karśnicki, the master of the horse Bartochowski, and the citizen Bąkowski, whom he put in chains and sent off to the Silesian fortress at Nysa. At that time even several women fell at the hands of the Prussians. Thus there were murdered the rotmistrz's wife Kożmińska, Miss Gątkiewiczówna, and the citizen Krąkowska, who was cut down with broadswords[^136].

Kościuszko's proclamations were brought to the neighbourhood of Wieluń by the citizen Konopnicki. Psarski, podkomorzy of Wieluń and owner of numerous estates, contributed greatly to the outbreak of the rising in those parts. He arrived in Wieluń on 26 August with his sons, his son-in-law Ciemniewski and his servants, took up quarters at an inn, and ordered the court official he had summoned to enter an insurrection act in the castle-court registers. Despite his threats he was unable to induce the authority to carry out this legal formality, but in return the town council and the officials swore to him an oath of fidelity to the national cause. On that same day Psarski set out for the village of Naramice to persuade Józef Stokowski to take command of the rising in the district. His son Mikołaj he sent to Opajowice, the estate of the citizen Topolski, where on that day the local nobility gathered in large numbers, so that he might call them to arms. On the next day many of the nobility, armed and mounted, arrived in Wieluń. Among those assembled confidence in success grew. Still on that same day, 27 August, the rising was proclaimed in the Wieluń and Ostrzeszów [Schildberg] districts, Stokowski was entrusted with the dignity of general, and a commission for civil and criminal matters was appointed. To the membership of this authority were called Psarski, Ciemniewski and Wierzylecki, who summoned the inhabitants of the surrounding towns and villages to declare themselves for the rising. Soon further ordinances of the commission appeared, directing the population to arm, to prepare pikes and scythes, to hand over to the commissariat, against receipts, the taxes and goods that the commander of the armed detachments, Stokowski, might demand, to detain all Prussian transports and mail, and finally to send spies and suspect persons before the criminal court. For the success of the insurrection services were held in the churches, and the priests read proclamations from the pulpits, endeavouring to convince the people of the impor-

[52] <!-- p.52 -->

tance of the moment. Since the commission's chief concern was to have a suitable armed force, at the end of August Michał Jerlicz, formerly a colonel in the Polish army, was ordered, under threat of punishment, to see to its formation from various fugitives, riflemen and cantonists. The craftsmen were ordered to work on the making of the things needed for the army, and the Jews of Działoszyn were to supply arms and ammunition. In the summonses that were sent out in the neighbourhood of Wieluń and Ostrzeszów, dated 15 August and signed by Stokowski, the nobility were called upon to present themselves under arms. Whoever could not come in person was obliged to send substitutes. From the noble estates one recruit was to be supplied for every 5 houses, and from the royal and ecclesiastical estates two in the same proportion. In the small town of Grabów in the Ostrzeszów district the armed movement had begun earlier, on 23 or 24 August. At that time a detachment of insurgents under the command of Kazimierz Madaliński and Jabłkowski came to the town; they received from the town council and the burghers an oath of fidelity to the fatherland and took the cash box. To complete the account of the events that took place in these parts, it must be added that Psarski summoned 12 citizens from Kępno [Kempen] to Ostrzeszów, swore them in, and chose 4 of them for the Commission of Order and as many for the criminal court. At Bobrów he urged the burghers to rise against the Prussians, set up insurgent authorities, and ordered the collection of a double hearth tax and of the "*ofiary*"[^137].

In the Kalisz voivodeship the insurrection was proclaimed on 22 August; Paweł Skórzewski became commander of the armed force[^138]. Jan Sieroszewski, a former colonel of the Bar Confederation, took a lively part in the rising in this land. In the middle of August Sieroszewski, having received

[53] <!-- p.53 -->

the summons and patent from Kościuszko, "in consequence of the proclamation sent to him was the very first to mount his horse and ordered the proclamation to be published at once from the pulpits." Later he gathered 25 horsemen and, on news that the town of Kalisz was fighting the Prussians, hastened there with them. The alarm proved false, but it aroused the vigilance of the Prussians, who placed two cannon on every road and reinforced the guards. The insurgents therefore withdrew, attacking on the way a detachment of Prussian hussars convoying wagons loaded with grain; 13 hussars and 700 korcy of oats fell into their hands. Sieroszewski then went with his detachment to Stawiszyn, and from there to the village of Kosmów, to rescue the citizen Kawiecki, whom the Prussians had seized for supplying arms to the insurgents. The detachment of Prussian soldiers conveying the prisoner, at the sight of the insurgents, retreated into the fields, abandoning Kawiecki. Sieroszewski dashed after the retreating men and took their whole detachment, consisting of 30-odd soldiers, prisoner. In this action the deserving citizens Maciej Konopnicki and Józef Cybulski fell. Sieroszewski then set out back to Stawiszyn, where General Skórzewski marched in with scythemen, whereby the insurgent detachment grew to 300 men. From there they pressed on to Koło, near which town a junction was made with the detachment of Andrzej Jachowski. The Prussian hussars were driven out of Koło[^139] and pursued as far as Kłodawa[^140]. 19 Prussians were killed on that occasion and 40 were taken prisoner. The Poles had 4 killed and 6 wounded. After this event there was again a return to Stawiszyn, whence, after a few days' rest, the insurgents went to Słupca, the assembly point of all the voivodeship forces[^141].

Of the events that took place in the course of August in the Kalisz region we also have an account from the opposite side, the Prussians,

Milkau set out, by order of the commandant of Poznań, Diether, on 10 August into the Kalisz region with half a depot battalion from Frankenberg's regiment and 1 cannon, to cover the magazines at Koło and Konin. On 23 August it was reported to him at Koło that the insurgents, whose gathering had long been rumoured, were planning a simultaneous attack on Koło and Konin with a force of 800 men. From Konin, on the following day, Lt. v. Prittwitz, who was stationed there as garrison, informed him that the insurgents had likewise gathered in large numbers in the neighbourhood of Miłosław and intended on the next day to go to Słupca with a detachment of 400 armed men, as a result of which communication with Poznań would be cut. Shortly before that he had also received news from Kalisz of the appearance of insurgents at the gates of that town and of their firing on the guard. On 25 August Lt. Milkau arrived at Konin to cover the considerable magazine in that town. He learned then that a detachment of 300-400 insurgents had entered Stawiszyn[^143]. He remained at readiness through the nights, and his pickets were constantly alarmed. On 27 August the outposts of the insurgents appeared before Konin at a distance of half a mile. Milkau sent a patrol against them, but the insurgents attacked it. They killed 1 Prussian and took two prisoner. On the same day a Prussian detachment of 23 men escorting 25 prisoners arrived at Konin from Łęczyca. Insurgents attacked it on the way, between Koło and Konin, but achieved no success. On 29 August 200 armed men burst into the suburb of Konin and took the cash box from the salt depot there. Communication was cut in all directions and no one was willing to offer the Prussians his services as a messenger[^144]. On the following day, 30 August

[55] <!-- p.55 -->

news reached Milkau that the insurgents were uniting in order to strike at Kalisz or Konin. Having received an order to maintain communication with Kalisz and to join the garrison there, he set out on the road the next morning, although he was surrounded on all sides by insurgents. He nevertheless reached Stawiszyn without hindrance. There he learned that some 1,500 insurgents, foot and mounted, were stationed in the town. He at once formed his detachment into a square and moved on, and in passing Stawiszyn he fired the cannon several times, as a result of which the enemy withdrew somewhat deeper into the town. Soon, however, 300 peasants, armed with scythes and pikes, sallied out of the town to block the Prussians' passage. Under a hail of bullets they nevertheless fell back, and Milkau passed the town. During the further march he still had for some time to skirmish continually with the insurgents, who had set out after him from Stawiszyn but afterwards turned back. At night he finally reached Kalisz; he had 1 soldier killed and 1 wounded. The insurgents' loss was said to have amounted to 54 killed and many wounded. Two hours after the events that had taken place at Stawiszyn, another 500 insurgents arrived there, whereupon the whole host set out for Pyzdry with the intention of entrenching themselves near that town.

Having thus become acquainted with the beginning of the insurrection throughout the entire second Prussian annexation, let us move farther north, to that part of Greater Poland which the Prussians had already seized in 1772. This is the so-called Netze District [Netzedistrikt], forming part of West Prussia.

The commander of the armed force in those parts was Grudziński. His troop never numbered more than 1,300 men, yet it proved sufficient to alarm the local Germans and Jews. Hence requests for help were sent from Bydgoszcz and Inowrocław to the King

[56] <!-- p.56 -->

of Prussia, but in vain. The Prussian headquarters reported that it could detach no troops, although in the whole Netze District there was only one company of invalids, at Bydgoszcz. On 26 August a detachment of insurgents entered Żnin [Znin], removed the Prussian eagles, seized the treasury, induced the citizens to swear an oath of loyalty to the Commonwealth, and issued a proclamation summoning the owners of estates to appear at Gniezno together with their subjects[^145], bringing arms and provisions. These proclamations, as elsewhere, were announced from the pulpits by the parish priests. From Żnin the flame of revolt began to spread farther and embraced Szubin [Schubin], Barcin, Gąsawa, and even Sępolno [Zempelburg] in Pomerania[^146]. The Prussian authorities, seeing no other way of checking the spreading insurrection because of the small number of troops, issued proclamations calling on the population to remain faithful in their subjection and to seize the insurgents, and also recalling the benefits that flowed to it from Prussian rule. A circular was also issued at Kwidzyn [Marienwerder], warning the clergy against supporting the rising and forbidding clerics from South Prussia to enter West Prussia. For the Prussians considered priests very dangerous. They were, after all, above all the ardent propagators of the insurrection, and through their frequent journeys they could easily spread the watchwords of revolt. These measures were, however, ineffective. The rising spread, and even in Piła [Schneidemühl], so thoroughly Germanised later, it had its supporters. Everywhere the clergy and the poor nobility sincerely favoured it. Thus in the small town of Kcynia [Exin] Krupecki was a zealous champion of the insurrectionary idea. Seeing the sluggishness of the magnates toward the national cause, he compelled several of them to take part in the insurgent movement. There was, however, little benefit from the Poles won over in this way. Two

[57] <!-- p.57 -->

of them, Fryderyk Skórzewski and Baranowski, fled at the first opportunity to Piła, under the protection of the Prussian government. Nor did the starostas Dąmbski of[^147] Kaczków and Mieczkowski of Żelechlin[^148] prove any better; they lodged a complaint against the insurgents with the King of Prussia.

Meanwhile the Germans in the towns, unable to wait for the Prussian army for which they had petitioned, began to arm themselves. At Barcin and Łabiszyn [Labischin] they tried to repulse the insurgents, and in the latter place they captured 15 and sent them to Bydgoszcz. The insurgents, however, quickly dealt with such slight resistance, and still at the end of August the whole southern part of the Netze District was in their hands. Krupecki, having obtained from the Gniezno confederation the dignity of commandant, continued to bustle about the Kcynia area. The receipts he issued in return for the requisitions made in the name of the insurrection of the Gniezno voivodeship bore the inscription: integrity, liberty, independence[^149].

The spread of the insurrection throughout Greater Poland was particularly favoured by local conditions there. For the whole country was covered with forests, the largest belts of which stretched along the Noteć [Netze] and south of the sources of that river, and farther along the Warta [Warthe] and the Prosna. So great a wealth of forests was of first-rate importance for guerrilla warfare. The individual detachments of Greater Poland partisans, who in the open field would not have been able to match the Prussian army, nevertheless found a safe hiding place for themselves once they had taken refuge in the forests. There the Prussian soldier, not yet familiar with the terrain, could not track them down, whereas they could undertake unexpected forays into the countryside, cut communications, paralyse the Prussian authorities, take money from the Prussian treasuries, and escape pursuit safely.

[58] <!-- p.58 -->

To crush the rising was therefore not easy. To secure the royal magazines at Sieradz and Kalisz, troop detachments arrived at the end of August under Colonel Dossow and Major Bonin, of whom the first dispersed the insurgents at Jutrosin, and the second did the same in the Sieradz region. At about the same time reinforcements were even detached from before Warsaw under Colonel Székely and Major-General Schwerin. The first went in the direction of Gniezno, the second to Kalisz[^150]. The rising in Greater Poland thus absorbed Prussian forces and constituted a successful diversion on behalf of the besieged capital, and in the end was chiefly to induce the Prussian king to withdraw from the entrenchments before Warsaw.

How, then, did the situation in Warsaw develop at this time?

At the end of August the enemy began to attack the city with increased strength. On 26 August the Prussians launched a violent assault and took Wawrzyszew and the Swedish Hills, but two days later, when they renewed the attack, they were completely repulsed. Rymkiewicz distinguished himself on that occasion by his defence of Bielany, being at once promoted from lieutenant colonel to general, and so did General Dąbrowski by his stubborn defence of Powązki. Facing the opponent on 28 August inspired the Poles with new zeal. Attacks were undertaken on the Prussian positions: on 29 August on Wola, though without success, and on 31 August on Wawrzyszew, and part of this outpost, lost five days earlier, was taken back from the Prussians[^151].

The hearts of the besieged were also fortified by news of the favourable development of the rising in Greater Poland. On 3 September envoys from that land, the citizens Hoffman and Buchowski, appeared before Kościuszko; they gave an account of the armed movement up to that time in their

[59] <!-- p.59 -->

region and asked for help to be hastened[^152]. On the following day Kościuszko shared the joyful news "with the citizens, inhabitants of Warsaw", and, pointing at the same time to the effects that the Greater Poland rising might have on the further course of the siege of the capital, included in the proclamation the following words: "This happy event will upset certain designs of our enemies, but may also drive them to desperate steps and to hastening against Warsaw all the exertions of their fierce and vindictive pride[^153]". Kościuszko also held up the Greater Poles as a model to the citizens of the Volhynia voivodeship and others "torn from the body of the Commonwealth by the last violent Muscovite seizure", reproaching them in a proclamation sent out on 5 September that, while everything that bears the Polish name seizes upon every means of throwing off the "insulting yoke", they alone "remain in sluggish tranquillity", and with these words he encouraged them to rise: "You have no army in your country, but you have people, and these people will soon become an army. The Greater Poles had no army, yet having made a holy pact in one day in seven voivodeships, they crushed the invaders and raised an armed force of more than ten thousand. They gave all their brethren an example, one very necessary for them, that a rising can be carried out without the help of an army, provided only that the interest of the Fatherland unites the minds of all, and the love of liberty kindles them with a holy fire. The difference of faith and creed did not hold them back from this work. Both of these are no obstacle to loving the Fatherland and liberty. Let everyone honour God according to his own faith, and there is no faith that forbids a man to be free. This truth united the Greater Poles; despite the multitude of people of various creeds in those parts they felt that they cannot be

[60] <!-- p.60 -->

happy without freedom and dared to be free. Do likewise yourselves"[^154].

The news of the Greater Poland uprising also found a lively echo in the chambers of the last of the Polish kings. On 4 September Stanisław August wrote to Naruszewicz: "Today God has given an event that revives our hopes: from the Wieluń Land as far as Kuyavia all the Greater Poland voivodeships have risen, joining themselves to the rising of the Commander," and at the end, as if by way of warning, he added: "whether we shall know how to make good use of it"[^155]?

Meanwhile the reverses of the last few days had shaken the Prussian army's faith in certain victory, and in the staff quarters they made a depressing impression. For 2 September the Prussian king was preparing a new assault, but on the evening of 1 September he withdrew the orders already issued and resolved to break off the siege[^156].

The reasons for this decision were also weighty.

Lieutenant-General Schwerin, ever cautious, who had returned to the army in mid-July[^157], drew the king's attention to the shortage of provisions and ammunition, to the great number of sick soldiers, and to the possibility that even after the capture of Warsaw the weakened Prussian army would meet in the Polish capital the same fate that had once befallen Igelström's garrison; finally he put forward the strongest argument, namely he pointed to the ever more violent spread of the insurrection in Greater Poland and advised that peace be restored there first. In addition, relations with the commander of the Russian forces, General Fersen[^158], which had long been strained, had worsened, and from Vienna came word that Austria, which at the beginning of July had itself brought its troops into the territory of the Commonwealth[^159], would not support the besiegers with troops. This sufficed to convince the king of the pointlessness of continuing the siege. Not wishing to expose his army to greater danger, and above all not wishing to

[61] <!-- p.61 -->

have behind his back a country engulfed in the fire of revolt, Frederick William began the retreat from the entrenchments of the capital[^160].

In the night of 5 to 6 September the Prussians began to draw in their troops. During this operation, so as not to draw the attention of the besieged, guns thundered incessantly and watch fires burned. After the guns withdrawn from the trenches and the baggage had been transported away, the individual corps marched off. On 5 September General Amandrüz set out for Zakroczym in order to join the corps on the Narew. On the same day General Frankenberg began his march toward the Bzura by the road through Sochaczew, and General Elsner turned southwest by way of Mszczonów to suppress the insurrection between the Vistula and the Silesian border. At the same time General Pollitz was detached to the Pilica to take over the command of the troops there. On the morning of 6 September the main army under General Bonin and the heir to the throne set out toward Raszyn. Following the Prussians' example, General Fersen also left his position before Warsaw and made for Piaseczno, in order afterwards to cross the Vistula and cooperate with the Russian corps operating in Lithuania[^161].

The unexpected withdrawal of the enemy troops from the entrenchments of Warsaw filled the hearts of its inhabitants with joy. The capital breathed again, but it soon became apparent that the situation was by no means easy. It was found that the Prussian troops on the left bank of the Vistula, though they had withdrawn a few miles, nevertheless continued to blockade the city. Their posts stretched without a break along the left bank of the Bzura and the right bank of the Pilica, and between these rivers, on the stretch from Łowicz to Rawa, the main army was quartered with a fortified camp at Chrzonowice. North of the Vistula the column on the Narew stood opposed to the corps of General Favrat[^162],

[62] <!-- p.62 -->

but in Lithuania the insurgents were faring ever worse. After the capitulation of Vilnius [Wilna] on 11 August, the Lithuanian army resisted the Russian troops with difficulty and began to fall back toward Grodno and Warsaw[^163]. Only the events in Greater Poland were a brighter page of the uprising, although even there the situation was now becoming less easy.

The Prussian detachments under Col. Székely and Major-General Schwerin, which had been withdrawn from before Warsaw to Greater Poland while the siege was still in progress, were stamping out the insurgents there; and as soon as the siege was raised General Elsner was dispatched against the rebellious provinces and went to the left bank of the Warta, General Frankenberg toward the Bzura. The greater part of the Prussian forces, which until 9 September remained at Raszyn, then began a further retreat, drawing close to the borders of Greater Poland[^164].

There, of the Prussian commanders, the most dangerous was Székely.

He set out from the camp before Warsaw on 29 August at the head of Hinrichs's rifle battalion and the squadrons of Trenck, Czettritz and Wirtemberg, by the road through Sochaczew, in order to rout the insurgents gathering near Gniezno. He did not, however, go in the intended direction, but on hearing of the notorious events in Kuyavia changed his route and made for Włocławek. Having reached it, he met with considerable resistance from the insurgents, from whom he captured 4 guns, but he could accomplish no more. For 600 armed peasants and 200 horsemen were moving west to join the other Greater Poland risings. On 8 September Székely sent detachments of cavalry after them, while he himself remained with the rest of the corps in Włocławek. Only when he was informed of an alleged assembly of all the voivodeship forces near Inowrocław did he leave a company of infantry in

[63] <!-- p.63 -->

Włocławek and set out in the direction of Toruń and Bydgoszcz to cover the rich magazines in those towns; he reached Bydgoszcz on 12 September. On the same day the Kuyavian and Sieradz insurgents took Inowrocław, seized the treasuries and the Bydgoszcz post, but on the very next day, on the march against Bydgoszcz, they suffered defeat at Rynarzewo at Székely's hands[^165] and fell back to the neighbourhood of Szubin and Kcynia. As a result of the victory Bydgoszcz was saved from capture by the insurgents[^166].

In this short time Székely became a veritable terror to the insurgents and to the districts through which he passed. According to Rymkiewicz's words[^167], his entry into Greater Poland was "like a violent storm that overturns and lays waste everything it finds on its way". Already on the day after his departure from Warsaw, on 30 August, he issued at Sochaczew a proclamation[^168], which he ordered published as quickly as possible by all ecclesiastical and secular authorities. The four articles of this proclamation contained the broad plenipotentiary powers he had received from the king. Thus he threatened all those captured with arms in their hands with hanging. The same fate, or the penalty of life imprisonment in fortresses and in addition confiscation of property, was to befall those of the more prominent nobility who should be leaders or participants in the rising, and even their wives if they proved to be accomplices. Corporal punishment or death threatened also all those who hid insurgents on their estates and failed to report them to the nearest authority. Székely was likewise to seize suspected persons and send them to fortresses. All sentences were to be passed without prior investigation and without any legal formalities. Although in the proclamation Székely, on the express order of the king, threatened the penalty of death in many cases, in practice he did not dare to carry out this ruthless

[64] <!-- p.64 -->

threat. Fear for the fate of the Prussians in Polish hands, as we shall see later, obliged him to proceed more mildly. In return he strictly enforced the other penalties mentioned in the proclamation. In the neighbourhood of Gąbin, Gostynin and Włocławek he seized the leaders of the rising and priests and sent them to the fortress of Toruń. The property confiscated from the insurgents he used for his own enrichment. He simply stole whatever could be taken from manors, churches and monasteries, for which he earned severe condemnation even from the Germans of the day[^169].

Yet Székely was not to rage with impunity much longer on the lands of Greater Poland. From Warsaw General Jan Henryk Dąbrowski was marching there with regular troops, a capable and valiant leader who was to win a complete triumph over the brigand commander. This was the help promised to the Greater Poles by Kościuszko.

Its dispatch was preceded by writs of the Supreme National Council of 6 September appointing several Greater Poland citizens as plenipotentiaries for the voivodeship there. According to extensive instructions the plenipotentiaries were to publish the declaration of the Supreme National Council of 12 June, which annulled the duty of obedience to the Prussian king; to swear the citizens to fidelity, obedience and defence of the fatherland; to call upon them to take up arms and join the army of the Commonwealth; to order that a foot recruit from every fifth hearth, and a mounted one from every fiftieth, "armed and clothed in the shortest possible time", be delivered "where the commanding general shall make disposition"; to collect taxes; to requisition against receipts the things needed by the soldier; to establish, together with citizens of unblemished character, a Commission of Order, which would see to forage and produce for the army and the whole armed force

[65] <!-- p.65 -->

composed of citizens, and to take into administration the national estates, namely those of the primacy and of the archbishoprics of Gniezno and Poznań; to seek out a citizen fit to be a major-general, ordering him to "gather military strength in his voivodeship and unite with the army"; and finally to inflame the civic spirit and to punish severely those "who should show ill will toward the fatherland". The dignity of plenipotentiary was conferred on: for the Poznań voivodeship, Hipolit Rogaliński; for the Kalisz voivodeship, Hiacynt Zakrzewski; for the Gniezno voivodeship, Nicefor Gorzeński; they were to send the Commander a report of their activities every week[^170]. On that same day, 6 September, there also appeared an appeal of the Supreme National Council to the Greater Poles, whose purpose was to strengthen the civic spirit among the citizens. It emphasised the service the Greater Poles had rendered to the national cause by their action in these words: "You have risen, fellow citizens, and God has not failed to bless your rising. Numerous hosts of hired champions trembled at the first sound of your endeavour, and those who had come with insolence to the capital of the country to threaten a free people and finally to put it in the chains prepared for it, fled shamefully by night, giving by their example a loud lesson to such invaders that the assailing of a free land cannot go unpunished[^171]".

Three days after the issuing of the appeal, on 9 September, a corps stood ready to march to Greater Poland[^172] at Młociny. In view of the anticipated clashes with Russia, which was drawing troops from Livonia, White Ruthenia and Ukraine, Kościuszko did not send many soldiers, wishing as he did "to make up for the deficiency in the person of the commanding general"[^173]. He therefore entrusted the command of the corps to Dąbrowski, the more so as the Greater Poles had earnestly asked for it[^174]. On that same day, 9 September, Dąbrowski also received

[66] <!-- p.66 -->

an order from the Commander "to proceed with the army entrusted to him to Greater Poland by the road and by the means that his prudence and talents shall show him to be best and most convenient". The further words of this order ran: "He shall endeavour to unite as speedily as possible with the corps assembled in that province, shall assume the supreme command over them, and shall have the power to command and to issue his orders to all voivodeship generals and to all officers of the Greater Poland armed force, of whatever rank, to turn their detachments wherever he shall judge necessary; he shall confer with the civil government concerning the provisioning and supplying of the national army with all necessities. He shall use all his strength to harass the enemy and drive him from the invaded lands, to seize treasuries and magazines, in a word, to do the enemy the greatest possible harm and destruction; he shall endeavour to maintain the greatest possible communication with the main corps and its divisions, and shall give reports of his movements as often and as cautiously as possible[^175]".

Aware of the importance of the mission entrusted to him, Dąbrowski set out at once. He took with him the brigades bearing his own name and Rzewuski's, amounting to 900 cavalry and 1,000 infantry from the 4th and 13th regiments, together with 100 riflemen of Sokolnicki, recruited from Warsaw youth alone, and in addition 12 three- and six-pounder guns. The strength of the corps was soon increased by the joining of Madaliński's detachment, consisting of 600 horse and 400 recruits from the first regiment, together with 4 cannon. In quality the soldiery was mixed. The cavalry was the best, and among it Madaliński's brigade had already more than once given proof of its mettle; Dąbrowski's brigade, formed only during

[67] <!-- p.67 -->

the siege of Warsaw, was less practised. It was different with the infantry. It consisted for the most part of scythemen, and only the 13th regiment was wholly skilled in the handling of firearms[^176].

Dąbrowski was accompanied on the march by Józef Wybicki, appointed on 9 September plenipotentiary for the Łęczyca, Kuyavian and Sieradz voivodeships. He was to remain constantly with the division of "citizen General Dąbrowski", and received from Kościuszko instructions identical with those that Rogaliński, Zakrzewski and Gorzeński had received three days before; with them he was also obliged to confer and cooperate[^177].

Wybicki writes of himself in connection with this appointment in his memoir[^178]: "Whether from his own conviction, or from the inspiration of President Zakrzewski, or finally at the request of General Dąbrowski himself, I do not enter into it, the Commander named me plenipotentiary with the corps destined for Greater Poland. For some days I resisted my nomination, for I confess frankly that it was easy to reckon that, despite the Spartan zeal of my Greater Poles, that small handful of regular troops which was coming to their aid could not in the end overcome the enormous Prussian force... Yet I yielded at last, as befitted a good Pole, to the Commander, and accepted with ample powers the instrument of plenipotentiary, which was very flattering to me".

Meanwhile, in order to cover Dąbrowski's march toward the Bzura, Kościuszko dispatched several detachments. There set out from Warsaw: Madaliński, with a force of 1,000 men, toward Witkowice; Kołysko, with a detachment half as large again, by way of Nadarzyn toward Bolimów; Prince Józef Poniatowski, at the head of 2,000 soldiers, to Mszczonów.

Dąbrowski decided to cross the Bzura at Kamionna, on the left wing of Frankenberg's position, and made the crossing on 13 September, attacking the weak Prussian posts

[68] <!-- p.68 -->

at Kamionna and Witkowice. Driven out of both places, the Prussians lost 100 men; several officers and 80 soldiers were taken prisoner, and in addition rich provision magazines fell into Polish hands. Kościuszko, informed of this event in a report, congratulated Dąbrowski on his success, promising to send "tokens of the nation's gratitude" for the officers who "distinguished themselves"[^180]. A few hours later a detachment sent out by Dąbrowski attacked the Prussian detachment at Pieczyska, where the magazines "were taken despite the enemy's strong defence, and burned through carelessness"[^181]. On the following day, 14 September, Madaliński joined Dąbrowski at Witkowice. Wybicki was present at the meeting of the two generals, and it is also his merit that Madaliński, although "the general senior in rank"[^182], submitted to Dąbrowski's authority. He was, after all, "only a soldier of fortune and did not have those talents which Dąbrowski could have acquired in the Saxon army"[^183], yet he continued to lead his own brigade[^184].

To continue the further march in safety, Dąbrowski asked Kościuszko to order Gen. Bronikowski, who was standing idle with a considerable detachment, to join him. Bronikowski did indeed receive such an order, but sent out his infantry too late. For this reason Dąbrowski was unable, as he himself recalls, to strike at the rear of the enemy army marching on Koło and Radziejów, nor to arrive in time at Gąbin and halt that army's pursuit of the Kuyavian insurgents[^185].

The enemy army of which Dąbrowski speaks was commanded by Major-General Schwerin, formerly an adjutant and favourite of Prince Henry of Prussia. He set out from Łowicz at the head of two battalions of the Kunheim regiment and 2 squadrons[^186] for the Warta region by the road through

[69] <!-- p.69 -->

Gąbin, Gostynin, Izbica and Sompolno. On 14 September he halted at Konin, sending from there 2 squadrons against the insurgents toward Koło. This small detachment forced the insurgents to retreat and inflicted on them a loss of 80 men and 2 small cannon. Two days later Schwerin attacked, near Konin, the Kuyavian rising under Mniewski, who had hastened there after the defeat at Rynarzewo. Having occupied Konin, he wished to move after the retreating insurgents to Koło, but learned that Dąbrowski had sent there a detachment of regular troops, 300 men strong, under the command of Gen. Rymkiewicz. He therefore did not go in the intended direction, but by way of Tuliszków and Stawiszyn to Kalisz, where a considerable magazine was guarded by only a garrison of 1½ battalions[^187].

At that time Dąbrowski with the main forces, having before him the corps of Major-General Schwerin, on his left flank the greater part of the Prussian army withdrawn from before Warsaw, and on his right Colonel Székely, was heading into the heart of Greater Poland. He himself marched on Gąbin and Kłodawa; his right wing under Bielamowski went along the Vistula through Tokary toward Włocławek, his left under Madaliński toward Sochaczew, Łowicz and Łęczyca[^188]. On the way Wybicki issued a proclamation[^189] "to the citizens and inhabitants of the Evangelical confession, as well as to the so-called Hollanders in Polish towns", calling them to arms, and Madaliński intercepted the Prussian post coming from Łowicz. It contained very valuable correspondence. Thus there was found a letter of Frederick William II to Col. Székely with an order that he should enter into as few negotiations and promises with the insurgents as possible, and, when nothing was gained in that way, should at once carry out the instructions concerning the suppression of the insurrection; there was also a letter from the commandant of Gdańsk informing him that "because of the spirit of revolution making itself manifest" he had had to take arms from the citizens[^190]

[70] <!-- p.70 -->

finally a letter from Székely to the king, in which Székely reported from near Inowrocław that he had seized insurgents. They were: Stanisław Dąmbski, the cavalry captain (rotmistrz) Kownacki, Lt. Potocki, the castellan Bogatko with his wife, then the castellan's wives Lasocka and Mikorska with her sister, together with the citizens Sopliński and Korczelski[^191], and the canons Osiński, Wochelski, Ostrowski, Złotnicki and Żynda[^192], and the parish priest Piotr Osiński[^193]. Székely would gladly have hanged "some half a dozen" of those seized, had he not feared for the fate of the Prussian officials held in Polish hands. He therefore confined himself to setting the captives beneath the gallows in order to frighten them, after which he sent them all off to the fortress at Toruń [^194].

In a report of 18 September Dąbrowski informed Kościuszko of the interception of the mail, but, being unable to entrust the "letters of much interest" which he would have liked to see printed, he confined himself to giving the most important news contained in them. He wrote at the same time that the detachment advancing along the Vistula had taken possession of the Prussian salt magazines at Tokary[^195]. The next day he reported that Madaliński had beaten the Prussians at Kutno, where there had been eleven of them, six had been taken prisoner, and the rest had saved themselves by flight[^196]. Although he had earlier received a warning from the Commander that the Prussians had moved from the Narew toward Wyszogród[^197], he did not abandon the plan once conceived, but hastened with the corps to Koło "to save the union of Greater Poland[^198]." He merely asked Kościuszko that, in support of his expedition and to maintain communications, he extend one observation corps along the Bzura and another from Błonie to Łowicz[^199].

At the same time serious changes took place in the Prussian camp. On 18 September Frederick William II left the army and, ill

[71] <!-- p.71 -->

and in very bad humour, made his way to Berlin by the road through Wrocław [^200]. Supreme command on the left bank of the Vistula was given to Lieutenant-General Schwerin. Before his departure the king gave him the following instructions: 1) to deploy the army along the Bzura, the Rawka and the Pilica, so as to keep Warsaw shut off at a distance and to prevent new corps from breaking through out of Warsaw; 2) not to strip the garrisons from the Kraków and Sandomierz voivodeships; 3) to suppress the rising in Greater Poland[^201]. It was a difficult task, and the means of achieving it were meagre, but the Prussian king counted in this case on the help of Fersen's Russian troops on the Pilica. He also hoped that Schwerin, better than other commanders, would manage to maintain good relations with the Russian headquarters and, by joint forces, to starve out Warsaw, hold the line of the Bzura and the Pilica, and above all defend the territories newly acquired by Prussia against the insurgents and against Austria, which was likewise striving for new acquisitions[^202].

At the moment when Lieutenant-General Schwerin assumed supreme command, of the army that had taken part in the siege of Warsaw there remained in the fortified camp at Chrzonowice[^203] only 12 musketeer battalions, 10 squadrons of dragoons and the same number of hussars, one six-pounder battery and one field battery, and in addition two 12-pounders. The remainder of the corps entrusted to him was scattered over a wide area. Thus the corps of Major-General Frankenberg, with 6 musketeer battalions, 5 squadrons of dragoons, a detachment of 120 horsemen picked from among hussars and dragoons, and 1 six-pounder battery, stood guard over the Bzura from Łowicz to Kamionna. Major-General Pollitz, stationed in the Sandomierz voivodeship, guarded with 6 musketeer battalions, 1 rifle battalion, 4 squadrons of hussars and 1 heavy six-pounder battery the line of the Pilica from Nowe Miasto all the way to its confluence

[72] <!-- p.72 -->

with the Vistula. Further, in the southern part of that voivodeship, in the vicinity of Opatów, stood Major-General Lattorff with 1 rifle battalion and two musketeer battalions (all three unmobilised), and in addition a strong cavalry detachment, composed of 1,891 horse. The corps which Major-General Schwerin commanded, operating near Kalisz, consisted of 2 musketeer battalions (on a peacetime footing), 4 squadrons of hussars and 2 field guns. The detachment of Major-General Elsner, no longer so strong, composed of only 1 rifle battalion and 3 squadrons of hussars, operated in the vicinity of Leszno. Finally Colonel de Székely, with the same strength and in addition 2 field guns, manoeuvred near Inowrocław[^204].

On news of Dąbrowski's march, Schwerin, on the king's advice, reinforced the Prussian posts on the Bzura, sending there the infantry regiment of Jung-Schwerin, 3 squadrons of Wirtemberg's hussars and 2 of Prittwitz's dragoons together with two field guns. With the help of these forces the Prussian commander on the Bzura, General Frankenberg, with a corps consisting of 9 battalions, 12 squadrons and 2 cannon, was to recover Kamionna or at least to prevent the Polish army from fresh attempts to break through toward Greater Poland, to garrison Łowicz and Sochaczew with strong forces, and at the same time, jointly with Major-General Schwerin and Colonel de Székely, to destroy Dąbrowski. Soon, however, General Frankenberg was relieved of his command, and his place was taken by Major-General Klinkowström, who was now left to carry out the orders given to his predecessor, and above all to restore Kamionna to the Prussians at all costs. According to a further royal order, Lieutenant-General Schwerin was to storm Kamionna with the help of 3 battalions of Rüts's from Pollitz's corps and a detachment of soldiers taken from the Chrzonowice camp, and afterwards to strike

[73] <!-- p.73 -->

from the rear at the column of Polish troops supporting the Greater Poles[^205].

In carrying out the commissions of his monarch, the old Schwerin proved extraordinarily cautious and timorous. He was kept in this state by rumours reaching his headquarters which bore only too eloquent witness to the grave situation of the Prussians. It was said that the 4,000-strong corps of Prince Józef Poniatowski and Zajączek had halted near Kamionna, that an army of 5,000 had encamped at a distance of 2 miles from Błonie which might threaten Łowicz or Sochaczew, and that another detachment of Polish troops, numbering 3,000-4,000 soldiers, had pitched camp, as Jews reported, near Nadarzyn. All these rumours so affected Schwerin that he still held Frankenberg back from attacking Kamionna, ordering him to await further events, while he himself, in order to preserve for the king the whole of his acquisitions, saw no other way out than to withdraw his troops from the Sandomierz and Kraków lands or to obtain reinforcement of the army by fresh relief forces. On the strength of new royal instructions he tried to operate as circumstances required, but this too was not easy. Communications, increasingly obstructed, made proper liaison with the separate corps impossible. With Berlin there was a connection only by the road into Silesia through Częstochowa, and with the centre of Greater Poland it was at times completely broken. The commanders of the individual corps in this sector, receiving orders late because of the interruptions in communications, and unable themselves to send reports in good time, acted on their own and therefore rather chaotically[^206].

This circumstance, as we shall see, was to have a very favourable effect on the course of the expedition to Greater Poland. Its commander meanwhile, having rested a day

[74] <!-- p.74 -->

at Koło, then crossed the Warta at Konin on 20 September and the next day encamped at Słupca. Here, on 24 September, took place the planned union with the risings of the Kalisz, Łęczyca and Kuyavian voivodeships and of the Poznań and Gniezno voivodeships. The latter two, two days earlier, in conjunction with the insurgent detachment of Krupecki, had clashed with the detachment of Székely's corps at Kcynia [^207]. The armed strength of the Greater Poland insurgents who had arrived at the assembly point did not exceed 4,000 and had first to be "regulated"[^208]. It turned out that the cavalry, though on good horses, was badly armed and for the most part ignorant of the simplest rules of military service. Yet young men and decrepit old men alike had presented themselves, and all were seized by the ardour of love of country. The main voivodeship forces retained a separate organisation and entered Dąbrowski's corps as regiments of light cavalry. Employed together with the regular army on various patrols and pickets, they gradually, under the skilled hand of their leader, acquired a sense of discipline. The infantry, which in Dąbrowski's division was in general inferior to the cavalry, swelled the ranks of the regular soldiers. The whole strength of the corps now amounted to over 6,000 men, besides the 600 horse of Madaliński's brigade[^209].

With such an army Dąbrowski set out in 3 columns for Gniezno and arrived there on 25 September[^210]. On the following day he sent to Kościuszko the citizen Miaskowski, "known to the public," with a request for the dispatch of a few 12-pounder guns and howitzers, paper money and more infantry. Miaskowski was also to renew the request that the corps stand at Gąbin, "not allowing the enemy to extend himself over the Warta and the Bzura"[^211].

[75] <!-- p.75 -->

In this case, however, a bitter disappointment awaited Dąbrowski.

The position on whose retention the fate of his entire corps depended had already been lost. Prince Józef Poniatowski, who had entered Kamionna after Dąbrowski left the line of the Bzura on 17 September, withdrew from there after a few days. In the night of 23 to 24 September word of this reached the Prussian camp at Potoki, but at first they were unwilling to believe that the Poles had vacated so important a position. When, however, a cavalry detachment that had been sent out confirmed the news, Colonel Keppern hastened with one battalion of the Frankenberg regiment, one squadron and four guns and occupied Kamionna. On the initiative of Captain v. Brodowski two redoubts were raised there, and the garrison was increased by yet another battalion of the Frankenberg regiment. In this way the division that had been dispatched to Greater Poland, surrounded on all sides by enemy forces, suffered a grievous blow, losing the possibility of joining up with Warsaw[^212].

Not yet aware of his grave situation, Dąbrowski spent three days of rest at Gniezno with his whole corps, full of the best hopes for the future. "The 27th was devoted to thanking God for supporting, with His almighty hand, the prosperity and glory of Polish arms in this province"[^213]. The suffragan bishop Szajowski celebrated a solemn Mass in the cathedral, concluded with the hymn "Te Deum", at which General Dąbrowski was present "with his staff and officers of the first ranks, with the heads of the risings and their foremost citizens[^214]". After the Mass "an excellent address, suited to the occasion, was delivered by citizen... Lipski, as head of the rising of this voivodeship. Once again they swore to live free or to die. This oath, repeated with enthusiasm even by feeble youth,

[76] <!-- p.76 -->

decrepit old men and the delicate sex, could not but be moving and draw tears... General Dąbrowski then invited everyone to a soldiers' dinner, where, with a modesty becoming to republicans, they made merry until evening[^215]". On the same day Dąbrowski and Madaliński issued a proclamation in which they acquainted the population at large with the plunder and cruelties committed by Col. Székely. They declared that all this had happened on the express order of Frederick William II, as was irrefutably proved by a royal letter, intercepted between Łowicz and Gąbin and now in their possession. They announced that in this letter the king expressly recommended to Székely that he set up gallows for the nobility and imprison them, and burn the letter "C" into the hands of the peasants, and that only the fear that a similar fate might befall the Prussian officials taken prisoner had kept Székely from carrying out the ruthless order. Yet this fear, as they demonstrated in the proclamation, was in vain. For Székely, as a "barbarian", did not know the magnanimity of the Poles, who abhor such deeds. Finally, the proclamation appealed to the Poles of the First Partition to return to the bosom of the fatherland, joining those who had come to rescue them, and to the inhabitants of both banks of the Oder, the former Slavs, the Brandenburgers and the Prussians, "subjugated for centuries by the house of Brandenburg[^216]".

On the following day, early in the morning, the Polish army left Gniezno[^217]. Dąbrowski sent a detachment of 200 horse under the command of the intrepid partisan Bielamowski toward Poznań, "to put it into the enemy's mind"[^218] that the Poles intended to attack that city. He himself, as he writes in his Memoir[^219], could not march on Poznań, since he had "no more than 2,400 regular men, among them only 1,400 infantry and 16 pieces of

[77] <!-- p.77 -->

small-calibre guns", and with such a force alone to take Poznań "with fitting success" was an impossibility. Bielamowski's expedition achieved its intended aim. For three days the Prussians reckoned with the possibility of an assault on the city by Polish troops[^220]. They took Bielamowski, who had reached the very gates of Poznań, for the advance guard of Dąbrowski's corps and hid themselves in the city and in the entrenchments[^221]. The alarm of the commandant Diether was all the greater at that moment because he did not know how large the enemy was, while the newspapers reported that Dąbrowski was leading a corps of 18,000[^222]. Diether himself, it is true, had done what was in his power to secure Poznań and had already thought of strengthening the city's fortifications almost from the outbreak of the Greater Poland insurrection, that is, from 24 August. He therefore reinforced, and raised new, entrenchments before the Wrocław Gate and on the hill of St Martin, which was designated as the main point of defence; he then threw up a redoubt before the "Brumska" gate, at the spot where the Muscovites had entrenched themselves 23 years earlier[^223]. He regarded these measures as insufficient, however, and so, in response to his alarm about the danger threatening the capital of Greater Poland, the generals Elsner with a thousand men from Leszno and Manstein with a detachment half again as large from Kościan, by way of Mosina, were hastening there at a rapid march to help[^224].

While Bielamowski was marching toward Poznań, General Madaliński at the head of 100 riflemen with two guns marched on Trzemeszno [Tremessen], into the region of Inowrocław, in order to draw the enemy's whole attention to himself, so that the enemy could not guess Dąbrowski's real intentions and hinder the corps itself in its crossing of the Noteć. The third column, dispatched from Gniezno and made up of Greater Poles, went under its general Lipski to Barcin and destroyed the Prussian post there. Dąbrowski

[78] <!-- p.78 -->

advanced with the corps in the direction of Łabiszyn "in order to meet Colonel de Székely at Bydgoszcz or Inowrocław[^225]. On the way the armed force grew, and "through voluntary ofiara the soldier abounded in plenty and almost in luxuries[^226]".

General Madaliński was the first to reach Łabiszyn, on 29 September, and he destroyed the Prussian post there, which defended itself fiercely in the monastery under the command of Lieutenant Beyer. In the course of the fighting 3 riflemen were wounded, and in addition the towarzysz (cavalry trooper of noble rank) Stan. Grabowski and the citizen Leszczyński from the Kalisz Land, who succumbed to his sufferings after 3 days. Dąbrowski estimates the Prussians taken prisoner at Barcin and Łabiszyn at 6 officers, 63 soldiers and 10 officials. Having spent the night at Łabiszyn, Dąbrowski intended to "fall upon" Bydgoszcz, while Madaliński was to keep watch on Székely. Events, however, took a wholly unexpected turn. Székely, having learned that Dąbrowski was standing at Łabiszyn, wished to forestall him in the march on Bydgoszcz. He therefore set out from Inowrocław at one o'clock in the afternoon and at twelve o'clock at night on 29 to 30 September attacked Dąbrowski's camp outside the town. He thought that in this way he would take by surprise the Poles quartered in the town and already felt himself certain of victory, since he estimated Dąbrowski's armed force at only a few hundred regular riflemen, regarding the remainder as a "rowdy rabble of rebel Greater Poles". He began the attack with guns, "so that the balls reached into the town", while he himself with his troops turned to the hill beyond the town and struck at Dąbrowski's left wing. He "advanced" with such insistence that it came to small arms, and "some wounded one another with bayonets". The fighting grew fiercer when Dąbrowski's brigade fell upon the enemy infantry with such courage "that it left its pennants among the corpses and only then to retreat

[79] <!-- p.79 -->

began." In the skirmish the infantry of Bielamowski's regiment held "most valiantly," sustaining, under the command of Lieutenant Colonel Mchowski, who "together with Captain Grzegorzewski distinguished himself," platoon fire that lasted nearly an hour. There was a moment when Dąbrowski very nearly fell into captivity, and only thanks to the help of the citizen Dziewanowski, from the Kuyavian uprising, and of Lieutenant Dąbrowski did he escape the hands of the enemy cavalry. The battle ended in defeat for the Prussians. Székely, leaving on the field 1 officer and 23 privates, together with many severely wounded soldiers and dead horses, withdrew under cover of night in the direction of Bydgoszcz, dragging behind him 12 wagons of the gravely wounded. The Poles had 7 killed and 9 wounded, and in addition 30 horses run through with bayonets. The most grievous loss was the death of Major Jarząbkowski of the 13th regiment, who "performing his duty with distinction," died the next day of the wounds he had received[^227].

In pursuit of the retreating Székely General Rymkiewicz dashed off, on Dąbrowski's orders, with 800 horse toward Bydgoszcz and reached it without hindrance. Half a mile from the town he scattered the hussars who raised the alarm against him, then took the heights above the town, posting vedettes on them, while he himself halted with the cavalry by the forest in such a way "that everyone had to suppose that infantry was with him." The town itself, surrounded by an old and not very high wall, lay before him as on the palm of a hand. Székely stood beyond the town in the forest, on the other side of the water[^228]. From Germans who had run out of the town Rymkiewicz learned that there were no more troops in the town than three battalions of garrison infantry. He therefore sent a report to General Dąbrowski on the night of 1 October with the assurance that, having placed guns on the heights, he "held the town in his hand." He wrote that half of the corps would have sufficed to take the town, for he had found it "unprepared."

[80] <!-- p.80 -->

At the same time as the report Rymkiewicz dispatched a letter to Wybicki, entreating him, for the love of the fatherland, that in case Dąbrowski should delay in striking at Bydgoszcz, he should forget on this occasion that he was his friend, and make use of the power of the law entrusted to him by the nation as its plenipotentiary, and prevail upon him to take this much needed step[^229]."

Dąbrowski was at that time still at Łabiszyn, from where he had sent out numerous detachments to patrol as far as Walkowice [Wałkowice], Żnin, Rynarzewo, Strzelno [Strelno], Barcin, Pakość [Pakosch], Gniewkowo [Argenau], Nakło [Nakel], Podgórz near Toruń, and Inowrocław. Major Bielamowski he ordered to set out from the vicinity of Poznań toward Pyzdry, in order to observe Major-General Schwerin, who was supposed to be in the neighbourhood of Kalisz and Śrem. He did not move to Bydgoszcz with his whole corps, as he himself says, because he did not wish "to expose the soldiers and ammunition at such a distance, in driving the enemy from his entrenchments and storming the town[^230]."

Meanwhile Rymkiewicz's report settled the whole matter. When Wybicki, "full of satisfaction that Bydgoszcz would be ours," went to Dąbrowski "with the determination not to leave him until the order to march was given to the corps," he found him already "disposed" to this step. Dąbrowski only enjoined Rymkiewicz, before setting out, that, "since the whole fate of this expedition depended on the seizing of the heights," he should seek "by various means to hold on to them, and, having reconnoitred all the positions as exactly as possible," should man them with artillery, which would hasten the surrender of the town[^231].

On the evening of 1 October, at 9 o'clock, the corps set off, and despite the wearisome night march it stood the next morning before Bydgoszcz. At once General Niemojewski was sent

[81] <!-- p.81 -->

out with the advance guard, in order that he should "move secretly along the forest to the right and occupy Bartodzieje [Bartelsee]." The rest of Dąbrowski's brigade, with 2 three-pounder guns, turned toward Wielki and Mały Wilczak, to observe the enemy and prevent him from crossing the Brda and the Bydgoszcz Canal. Dąbrowski himself, at the head of the Greater Poland insurgents and 4 battalions, moved out in 2 lines; on both wings of the infantry he placed the remainder of the cavalry; in reserve he left Colonel Sokolnicki with 1 battalion and several detachments of riflemen, while the guns he set up in front of the first line so that they could fire on the town and the Prussian artillery[^232].

In this battle order the Polish army advanced right up to Bydgoszcz and took up positions near the Jewish cemetery. Székely, with three companies of Hinrichs's rifle battalion, with artillery and cavalry, stood on the left bank of the Brda; in Bydgoszcz there remained only 4 garrison companies under the command of Colonel de Witten. Before long firing began on both sides, but it had little effect. Dąbrowski, not wishing to waste ammunition, therefore sent his adjutant, Major Zabłocki, to Colonel Székely with a proposal to surrender the town. Székely, acting "against all the customs of war," replied that "either Dąbrowski had himself lost his reason, or he supposes that he has lost his," in demanding such a thing of him; that Dąbrowski "ought to know that he is dealing with the famous de Székely, who has beaten the French everywhere." Finally he threatened Major Zabłocki that, if he did not leave the town at once, he would "have him shot in the head"[^233].

Having received such an answer, Dąbrowski gave the order to storm Bydgoszcz. Four battalions

[82] <!-- p.82 -->

of regular infantry began to attack the town from four sides. The first battalion of the 13th regiment, with 1 gun, struck under Colonel Burzyński at the gate on the Łabiszyn side; the second battalion of the same regiment, commanded by Major Szwykowski, advanced straight toward the sugar refinery; the first battalion of the 4th regiment, with 1 gun, attacked under General Rymkiewicz's command the Nakło Gate; finally the first battalion of the 1st regiment "marched forward, as if intending to enter the town between Łabiszyn and the Nakło Gate." Székely, wishing to induce Dąbrowski to send away a part of his forces and thereby to weaken the strength of the Polish attack on Bydgoszcz, began a manoeuvre in the direction of Czersk. There, however, the reserve under Sokolnicki was at once dispatched by the shortest road, and "with this relief force the detachment of Niemojewski, to whom the guarding of the Brda had been entrusted, was reinforced." The attack continued. When Székely learned that those 4 garrison companies would not be able to defend the town, he hastened in person with his corps to their aid, but arrived too late. Major Szwykowski at the head of his battalion had already burst inside the walls from the side of the refinery and, in street fighting, won the victory over the Prussian garrison, which defended itself for 2 hours with great stubbornness. At the moment when Székely came running up with his troops and was crossing the bridge over the Brda, a cannon ball shattered his left leg. Lying "on the paving of a street near the bridge," he called on the Polish soldiers for help, and then one of the scythemen recognised him and cried: "This is Székely, who burned and plundered us." At these words the soldiers threw themselves upon him, but he was rescued from the perilous situation by that same Major Zabłocki whom Székely had treated so badly an hour and a half before. The unfortunate enemy begged for forgiveness and for his life. "You would have received both without asking for them,"

[83] <!-- p.83 -->

Zabłocki answered, whereupon he ordered the wounded man to be carried to a nearby house and "decently provided for"[^234].

Meanwhile Colonel Hinrichs, who had taken over the command in Székely's place, seeing the Prussian defeat, withdrew with the remainder of the corps and with two guns in the direction of Świecie [Schwetz], losing "many captives" on the way. Madaliński dashed after the retreating force at the head of 2 brigades of cavalry and pursued it as far as Fordon. The Polish riflemen also went there, leaving their former position between Bartodzieje and Czersk. On that same day Dąbrowski extended his outposts toward Wtelno as far as Osielsko[^235] and Pałcz[^236] on the Vistula. In the battle, which was the bloodiest that fell to be fought in the course of the whole expedition, 100 Prussians fell, 50 were wounded, and 313 were taken prisoner[^237]. The Poles had 25 killed and 30 wounded[^238]. During the fighting, as Dąbrowski states in his reports, the Greater Poland scythemen distinguished themselves most particularly. Madaliński also stood bravely, setting a good example to his soldiers[^239].

When the victorious Polish troops entered the town, they found the houses shut up, and the inhabitants of both sexes "made an immense outcry and lamentation, begging for life and mercy." From some of the houses shots were even fired; Dąbrowski, however, ordered these to be opened at once, warning that "if any house is found closed, it will be subject to plunder." Assured of their safety, the people of Bydgoszcz came out of their houses and treated the soldiers, but lest "on account of the treating any offences should occur," the latter, at the first signal for departure given on the commander's order, "fell in in their usual order." Dąbrowski pitched his camp on the right bank of the Brda. 300 soldiers from the reserve formed the garrison of Bydgoszcz[^240].

[84] <!-- p.84 -->

In his report to Kościuszko[^241] Dąbrowski stressed with full satisfaction the orderly conduct of his own soldiers, who in the first moment after the victory, being masters of the city, "did little to violate this natural power". They neither plundered nor wronged the inhabitants, and there was no need to restrain them from excesses. There were even cases in which the old soldiers forbade the scythemen entry into houses, lest it be thought that they wished to rob[^242].

This great discipline was Dąbrowski's merit. For it was he who, brought up on the military principles of Frederick the Great[^243], sought to instil in the soldiers a sense of discipline, punishing even the slightest offence against the service[^244]. The second man who saw to it that the army did not commit abuses in captured Bydgoszcz was Wybicki[^245]. He did not allow the private property of the inhabitants to be touched, and of what was found in the public stores he ordered, as he himself writes in his memoir[^246], an inventory to be drawn up and the goods to be turned to the needs of the soldiers[^247]. In a proclamation to the people that he issued[^248] he declared that the Poles had come "to free their brothers from foreign rule and to return them to the bosom of the common mother". Using the authority entrusted to him by the Commander, he abolished the Prussian court of justice and the Prussian Administrative Chamber, and in their place he restored "to the city, which had been Polish from time immemorial, the national offices[^249]". As the highest administrative authority he set up a Commission of Order under his own direction, which occupied itself chiefly with intelligence work and with the use of the richly stocked Prussian magazines[^250]. Its members were: Zakrzewski as president, and as members Twardowski, Nadratowski, Karski, Bulewski, Rudowski, Modliński, the Protestant preacher Stellmann, and the merchants Oppermann and Ziegler. At the same time the newly appointed town council, headed by Mayor Puhan, also took up its

[85] <!-- p.85 -->

office[^251].

On the day after the capture of the city, 3 October, according to the account of a German eyewitness[^252], all the officials and some of the citizens were summoned to the building of the Chamber. Dąbrowski, Wybicki and a number of Polish generals and officers also arrived there. In the presence of all, the cash boxes were counted and carried off, and the portraits of the Prussian kings Frederick II and Frederick William II were removed from the conference hall. After this had been done, General Dąbrowski, who spoke correct German and was "a very modest man, wise and of great polish", stepped forward and ordered the local merchants to pay a contribution of 300,000 thalers, which in the end, owing to the intervention of 2 citizens, was reduced to 10,000 thalers. Next the officials were required to swear that they would hand over all the money and valuables in their keeping that Colonel de Székely had plundered, to which the Germans questioned, however, answered unanimously that they knew nothing. After a while General Dąbrowski spoke again, declaring that the Poles had come not as enemies but as friends. He referred to the cruelties perpetrated by Col. Székely, described his inhuman treatment of the clergy at Włocławek and of persons of both sexes whom he had robbed and wished to hang, and finally had brought beneath the gallows at Inowrocław. As proof that the Prussian king approved of Székely's "tyrannical" conduct and had recommended that he be even more savage, Dąbrowski showed those present a royal letter intercepted near Gąbin. Finally he announced that he was arresting all the Prussian officials, so as to hold hostages in exchange for the imprisoned Poles[^253]. Wishing, however, to show that the Poles are magnanimous and do not take revenge even on

[86] <!-- p.86 -->

an enemy, he ordered the arrested men to send a delegation to their king with a request for the release of the Poles, whereupon they too would regain their freedom[^254]. After this speech all proceeded to the parish church, and only in one of the rooms did the arrested men remain, under the guard of 8 soldiers. In the church the citizens swore loyalty to the Commonwealth, after which Wybicki went once more to the board of the Chamber, where in the presence of the officials he called the Prussian king a "tyrant". At that moment the arrested councillor Broskovius approached him, asking him to release his countrymen. Wybicki, in his excitement, interrupted him and called him an "unreasonable man, a slave who does not perceive the yoke of his bondage". Broskovius, however, did not give up and presented his request for the release of the arrested men a second time. This time Wybicki heard him out, but soon flared up again and called Székely a brigand, threatening that, should the Prussian king wish to hang any of the imprisoned Poles, ten Prussian officials would immediately meet the same fate[^255].

When everything had calmed down, Dąbrowski visited the wounded Colonel Székely. Székely feared that the Poles would take revenge on him, but Dąbrowski assured him that he could be at ease and that he would endeavour "to sweeten his lot as much as possible". At the wounded man's request doctors and a priest were sent to him, and Dąbrowski gave him money. Other Poles came to visit him as well[^256], among them Mniewski, "a general worthy in every respect of admiration", in whose house Székely had formerly been a guest, and whom during the insurrection he had robbed "first of all". On seeing Mniewski, Székely fainted, and when he came to, the latter addressed him in these words: "as far as my own person is concerned, I forgive you everything, and if you wish to be at peace, return the plundered property to those from whom

[87] <!-- p.87 -->

you took every means of living". Thereupon Székely declared that he wished to speak with Dąbrowski and Mniewski himself, and when all had left he told them that his baggage was at Grudziądz and advised that the Poles should not advance further, because a corps was marching from Pomerania and Brandenburg that was to have joined him. As a token of friendship he gave Dąbrowski 6 riding horses, which the latter never claimed. The doctor who was called did not predict a long life for the wounded man, and indeed four days after the assault Székely died and was buried with military honours[^257].

The capture of Bydgoszcz made a great impression throughout Prussia. Berlin was ashamed of the defeat and at the same time anxious for the fate of the seized provinces[^258]. In Poznań people simply would not believe Schwarz, the eyewitness of the Bydgoszcz events[^259]. He himself relates that when, on 6 October at noon, the day after his arrival in Poznań, he stood before General Elsner, who was then in command in the city, the latter at first took the news of Székely's disaster and the capture of Bydgoszcz by the Poles for a fable, until towards evening a rider who had come from Dąbrowski's corps confirmed it. At the court of the Prussian monarch there now arose a fear for the security of West Prussia. It was thought, namely, that in that whole region there were too few troops to be able to oppose the Poles effectively. The garrison of Toruń under Colonel Hundt could be reinforced by only seven more companies, and the state of the city's entrenchments left much to be desired. Frederick William was therefore ready, in the last resort, to heed the advice of General Favrat and, to save Toruń, to fold up the front on the Narew and thus reinforce the threatened fortress with troops. Grudziądz too, where Colonel Pirch was commandant[^260],

[88] <!-- p.88 -->

had a weak garrison, and besides, in the whole of West Prussia, apart from the garrison of Gdańsk (6 battalions of the Raumer and Manstein regiments, 2 depot battalions, an artillery battalion and a squadron of dragoons[^261], whose composition, moreover, was made up of soldiers recruited in the former Polish provinces[^262]), there were no more Prussian troops. The remnants of Székely's corps, which Col. Hinrichs had gathered at Świecie, were too weak to undertake any action. In the Prussian camp, therefore, there was a lurking fear that Dąbrowski, whose forces were greatly overestimated, would strike at Gdańsk[^263].

The commandant of Gdańsk, General Raumer, also reckoned with this possibility, especially when he learned that Polish patrols were already reaching Chojnice [Konitz], and he applied appropriate measures. He placed guns on the ramparts, strengthened the guards, erected palisades, sent out patrols, and finally set up a camp on Bishop's Hill (Bischoffsberg)[^264]. The matter could not be taken lightly all the more because the people of Gdańsk favoured the Poles, and even the inhabitants of Starogard [Preußisch Stargard], Kościerzyna [Berent], Skarszewy [Schöneck] and Nowe Miasto were ready to supply them with help and with men for the ranks[^265]. In order, therefore, to halt Dąbrowski's possible march on Gdańsk, General Raumer used a ruse[^266]. He sent small detachments of troops into the vicinity of Kościerzyna and Starogard and ordered them to collect provisions and forage, to create the illusion that a larger corps was approaching in those parts. General Dąbrowski, who had previously learned of the concentration of General Beyern's corps[^267], and, not being informed of it in any detail, formed the supposition that it was this corps that was marching to West Prussia, abandoned the march on Gdańsk[^268].

The success that had attended the column of Polish troops sent to Greater Poland must have terrified

[89] <!-- p.89 -->

also Lieutenant-General Schwerin. He saw no other way out of the difficult situation than to draw General Pollitz's corps away from the Pilica and turn it against the South Prussian insurgents. He therefore divided the corps in question into two detachments. The first, made up of 4 battalions, 3 squadrons, two field guns and one howitzer, set out on 2 October under Colonel Ledivary for Toruń to reinforce the garrison there; the second, under General Pollitz himself, consisting of 3 battalions, 3 squadrons of hussars and 2 howitzers, marched out on 8 October from Wolbórz by the road through Lutomierz and Szadek, and then along the Warta through Koło and Konin to Gniezno.

General Pollitz was to destroy any enemy he encountered, in accordance with the plan of cooperation previously drawn up with Major-General Schwerin, Elsner and Székely. Lieutenant-General Schwerin also made other arrangements. On 4 October he ordered General Lattorff to withdraw behind the Nida, and four days later to quit the Sandomierz region altogether. He took this step chiefly on account of reports received from Lieutenant-General Lichnowski at Radomsko and from the Prussian treasury office at Piotrków, reporting unrest among the nobility there. He also intended to send some troops into the neighbourhood of Sieradz and Wieluń, and for that purpose dispatched General Lichnowski there with 1 battalion and a regiment of cuirassiers, whose mobilisation he had carried out. 3 battalions of infantry and 3 regiments of cuirassiers, whose mobilisation was nearly complete, he stationed from Zitena(?), Koniecpol, a place on the Pilica near the source of that river, and from Skała, as far as Kraków. To cover Gdańsk, he asked the king to send new corps to West Prussia from Berlin or Szczecin [Stettin]. He himself remained in the camp at Chrzonowice[^269].

Dąbrowski meanwhile was thinking of spreading the insurrection in the Netze District and in West Prussia. To that end, and in order to obtain fresh reinforcements as well as provisions and forage, he sent numerous cavalry detachments out from Bydgoszcz [^270].

Colonel Sieroszewski set out for Nakło and on the Noteć seized 13 vessels coming from Berlin with salt, iron, linen, cloth, etc.[^271]. The detachment of Lipski, major-general of the Gniezno voivodeship, sent in the direction of Chełmno [Kulm] and Grudziądz, dispersed an enemy patrol at Chełmża [Culmsee], taking 18 prisoners. Colonel Wyganowski, with 300 horse from the Poznań rising, went to the Wałcz district in order to raise an uprising there and choose recruits. Colonel Sokolnicki hastened to Fordon, to occupy that town and gain control of the banks of the Vistula, and moreover to round up vessels, wagons and men fit for transport, and to procure provisions of every kind. General Mniewski, with the whole detachment of his cavalry, and Colonel Mchowski, with 50 horse, together with infantry and artillery, were also directed to the Vistula, toward Solec Kujawski [Schulitz]. The former was to keep in touch with General Lipski, patrol toward Toruń, leave one detachment at Panadzewa and another at Podgórz to observe the Toruń bridge, which he was to burn in case of need; the latter was to station himself at Solec, send patrols toward Toruń and Gniewkowo and spies into the neighbourhood of Toruń, round up as many vessels and men useful for that purpose as possible, and maintain communication with General Mniewski. Dąbrowski ordered Lieutenant Moszczeński to remain with the cavalry at Łabiszyn, to send patrols from there toward Żnin, Wałkowice and Inowrocław, and to report as often as possible on the enemy's movements. Finally, Vice-Brigadier Łaszczyński, "a weak officer"[^272], was dispatched

[91] <!-- p.91 -->

with the remainder of Dąbrowski's brigade to Świecie, to halt vessels and carts, round up provisions, and always keep in touch with Wybicki, the plenipotentiary attached to that expedition.

According to a contemporary account[^273] by Lainé, the town secretary of Świecie, the Polish troops arrived there at noon on 6 October[^274]. Wybicki took possession of the treasuries, abolished the Prussian offices, and established a commission for civil matters, composed of 5 representatives each from the nobility, the burghers and the clergy. The town secretary Synakiewicz was elected president of the town, but as he refused to take up the new office, the mayor in office hitherto, Gannowski, was left in that post. Citizen Kruszyński became general of the rising, and Moszczeński, the owner of the village of Niewieszczyn, colonel. On the following day a fair was held at Świecie, and taking advantage of this the insurgents seized cloth and leather goods against receipts. The inhabitants were disarmed and ordered to furnish a recruit. 60 young men from the town itself and 200 from the commune were incorporated into the insurgent ranks. Craftsmen had to work day and night preparing the necessary equipment. On the night of 11 to 12 October the Poles marched out of the town, but returned the next day, 13 October, and left for good on the same day, taking with them, besides horses, all the local tailors and musicians. To supplement this account it should be added that when the Yellow Hussar regiment, stationed on the other bank of the Vistula, saw the Polish troops entering Świecie, it did not dare to attack them. Wybicki, who writes of this in his memoir[^275], then experienced "what power belongs to opinion spreading alarm in a revolution". For the enemy took all "objects, trees, herds" for "an enormous force", with which he supposed that

[92] <!-- p.92 -->

detachments of insurgents and regular troops were "marching into Prussia"[^276].

Eight days after the capture of Bydgoszcz, on 10 October, Dąbrowski set out in 2 columns for Toruń. The first, which he led himself, crossed the Vistula at Solec; the second, under Rymkiewicz, crossed at Fordon. At Zła Wieś (Bösendorf), on the Fordon-Toruń route, the two columns joined. The strength of this force was 1,000 infantry, 1,500 cavalry and 10 guns. Only 1 battalion of the Greater Poland rising remained in Bydgoszcz. On the following day, 11 October, Dąbrowski halted in the vicinity of Przysieka, where preparations were being made for the assault planned for the next day. According to the plan drawn up, the cavalry were to blockade the town on all sides and set it on fire in several places, in order in this way to create confusion in the town and enable the infantry to attack from a single point[^277]. Nor was Hundt, the commandant of the fortress of Toruń, idle. Fearing that the strength of the garrison might prove insufficient, and knowing how important Toruń was to the Prussians on account of its richly stocked magazines of munitions and provisions, he applied, immediately after the capture of Bydgoszcz, to General Favrat, who commanded the corps on the Narew, with a request for reinforcements. Soon 2 companies of Pfuhl's regiment did arrive in Toruń; a further 5 companies of the same regiment took up position at Płock and Bobrowniki, in order to come to the aid of the threatened post if need arose. On 7 October detachments of Polish cavalry appeared on both sides of the Vistula near Toruń, intending to quarter at Podgórz. Hundt's adjutant, Second Lieutenant Voss, then crossed the Vistula with 30 hussars and dispersed the cavalrymen already in Podgórz. Those dispersed

[93] <!-- p.93 -->

retreated into the forests south of Podgórz and joined a detachment of infantry there. To gain his bearings, Hundt sent Lieutenant Voss toward Fordon, still on 7 October, with 40 foot soldiers, as many hussars and 1 three-pounder gun. Marching all night, Voss reached the vicinity of Czarnowo[^278] early on 8 October, and having learned that 100 insurgents were encamped in that village, resolved to attack them. The attack succeeded for the Prussians. 2 officers, 15 soldiers and 50 wagons of forage fell into Prussian hands. On news of stronger Polish patrols approaching in the direction of Chełmno and Chełmża, and of an intended crossing at Solec by 2 companies from Dąbrowski's corps with two guns, Voss set out on the return journey[^279].

Meanwhile Dąbrowski did not storm Toruń. When he already had everything in readiness, he received certain news "that relief had reached Toruń". At first he supposed that "a corps had arrived at Toruń from Płock", but soon he was informed by reports that this relief was the corps of Colonel Ledivary[^280]. On hearing of the danger threatening Toruń, Ledivary had set out at once from Brześć Kujawski. On 11 October he stood already on the left bank of the Vistula, at Podgórz, and after leaving one battalion and cavalry outposts there, he crossed the Vistula in the night of 11 to 12 October, sent detachments out along that river, and marched to the village of Mokre[^281] in order to strike the enemy's left wing with his main forces[^282]. In such conditions Dąbrowski, who "at almost the same moment received a report from Włocławek that a corps was marching from Poznań and that the posts around Łabiszyn were retreating", was forced to abandon the attack on Toruń and "return again across the Vistula to Bydgoszcz"[^283]. On 14 October he crossed at Fordon[^284]. The detachment

[94] <!-- p.94 -->

consisting of 200 infantry, 400 cavalry and 4 guns, was meanwhile stationed at Solec Kujawski, to prevent the Prussians from attacking Dąbrowski on the flank. During the crossing Madaliński's brigade and 200 riflemen raised an alarm at Toruń, and as a result of this manoeuvre "the enemy corps stationed at Podgórz [Podgorz] went in large part across the bridge into Toruń[^285]". Colonel Ledivary, whose soldiers were exhausted by the ceaseless march, confined himself to a trifling harassment of the rear of the retreating Polish troops[^286]. Dąbrowski himself admits that he did not lose a single man, because the enemy "followed him sluggishly[^287].

The unsuccessful march on Toruń was not what Dąbrowski had intended. His aim was "to remain with the combined forces at Bydgoszcz and hold out through the winter between the Vistula, the Brda and the Noteć, to detach his numerous cavalry in all directions, as far as possible, to watch every step of the enemy; to this end to send from each rising, as far as circumstances allowed, considerable detachments to their own voivodeships, lands and districts to increase their strength, collect taxes and alarm the enemy everywhere; to help the rising in those places where it could not yet be carried out; to set up magazines at Bydgoszcz and Inowrocław, to bring the old regiments and brigades up to strength and form new ones, all of which could be done without oppressing the citizens, simply from the domains (Ämter) of the Prussian king, which are very numerous in these parts; to uniform and drill the army, so that in the coming campaign it might act with greater energy and benefit to the country[^288]". To storm a fortress with a fairly strong garrison, with the help of only a small number of regular troops, who would first have to be ferried over to the other bank of the Vistula, was in his view a bad move. The situation was made worse by the circumstance

[95] <!-- p.95 -->

that Prussian troops stood behind him, and he was not certain whether communication with Warsaw had not been cut, leaving the corps entrusted to him at the mercy of fate[^289]. The march on Toruń, undertaken in spite of everything, might be explained by a wish to calm the agitated minds of Dąbrowski's subordinates, who were dissatisfied with his tactics[^290].

For after the capture of Bydgoszcz, even before the departure for Toruń, reproaches had arisen against Dąbrowski "that he is not active enough and has no determination at all, with the result that not only do all the operations of the war proceed at a tortoise pace, but many are even omitted..., that from day to day the march on Toruń is put off, giving the garrison time to strengthen itself"[^291] Rymkiewicz reproached him for not having stood before Toruń on the day after the capture of Bydgoszcz, for then "this town would have surrendered or been taken easily by force of arms[^292]. Even Madaliński, "always honest, forgot in this moment what he owes to the Fatherland". Envy arose in him "that the name of Dąbrowski is famous, while his own is barely mentioned, and he already imagined for himself a right to the supreme command, which was due to him as a general of higher rank, as the first to the national rising". Realising, however, that he could not himself command so large a corps "in the sight of a more numerous enemy", he proposed to Rymkiewicz that the latter should "command under his name". He met with a refusal, however. Rymkiewicz, when he "could no longer bear the spirit of faction, armed with pure patriotism, rose up with republican energy, reminding Gen. Madaliński of his praiseworthy deeds;...he opened his eyes to the consequences that must inevitably be expected from division; he recalled to him the confidence which the Most Serene Commander and the whole nation placed

[96] <!-- p.96 -->

in the virtue and talents of Gen. Dąbrowski, which he knew how to earn and still earns". But the "factionists" did not give way. "They advised forming a military commission, composed of brigadier generals, which would henceforth govern". Dąbrowski, aggrieved that they wished in this way to deprive him of the command, appealed to Kościuszko's order, by virtue of which he had received command of the army. He declared "that he had so far done nothing without common deliberation", but "to a formal commission he cannot consent from his post", and in that case he resigned the command. "The obstinacy had already reached such a degree that Madaliński with his brigade and some of the risings wanted to separate from Gen. Dąbrowski", and only Generals Rymkiewicz, Mniewski and Skórzewski and the plenipotentiary Wybicki "used all their eloquence and reason to soothe minds so bitterly at war with one another". In time "though only on the surface, harmony, friendship, unity and love returned again"[^293].

Bielamowski learned of Ledivary's march to the relief of Toruń during his stay at Strzelno, from a letter of Col. Ledivary of 7 October to Major-General Schwerin, intercepted by an insurgent from Mniewski's detachment. Ledivary wrote that he had been ordered by the chief commander to scatter and defeat Dąbrowski's corps together with Major-General Schwerin, and that he was now marching to Toruń, to save that town, which Dąbrowski was threatening. Bielamowski sent the intercepted letter on to Dąbrowski, but the latter received it too late to avoid the pointless march on Toruń. For the letter reached Dąbrowski only in the night of 11 to 12 October, whereas on the 10th Dąbrowski had already crossed the Vistula on the march to Toruń[^294].

[97] <!-- p.97 -->

After returning to Bydgoszcz on 14 October, the commander of the expedition to Greater Poland soon received exceedingly sad news. He learned that the Commander had been beaten and taken prisoner, that communication with Warsaw had been broken because Prince Józef Poniatowski had abandoned Kamionna, that Generals Schwerin and Elsner were to march out of Poznań to destroy him, that a corps was approaching from Pomerania and Brandenburg, and that Lieutenant-General Schwerin had marched from the neighbourhood of Rawa to cut him off from the Bzura[^295]. Nothing was left to him, therefore, but to fall back toward Warsaw.

In Greater Poland, from the first days of September and simultaneously with Dąbrowski's operations, independent guerrilla warfare continued to be active. We must return here to its description, although an exact picture of this movement, that is, all its manifestations, is difficult to reconstruct. For the sources dealing with the matter were exceedingly meagre and confined for the most part to a contemporary newspaper published in Poznań under the title "Südpreussische Zeitung". Given the state of the means of communication at the time, this periodical could not record all the events connected with the guerrilla movement of the Greater Poles, and moreover the insurgents, by their frequent attacks on the posts, prevented news from getting through to Poznań. We know, however, that the partisans, moving from one manor to another in more or less numerous bands, induced the owners of estates, partly by persuasion and partly by force, to join their ranks and supply recruits; they appropriated the chests of the Prussian king, took salt from the Prussian magazines and then sold it more cheaply to the population, imprisoned officials, and above all greatly obstructed communication[^296].

[98] <!-- p.98 -->

Events such as the above, although typical of guerrilla warfare in Greater Poland and frequent, nevertheless cannot be generalised to the whole area of this region. For the patriotic ardour that had shown itself there at the beginning of the insurrection was now no longer so great, and in places it died away altogether. This can be explained both by the increased counteraction of the Prussian troops, which followed once several corps had been detached to Greater Poland during the last days of the siege of Warsaw and after its lifting, and by the proverbial straw fire of the Poles.

A few fragments given below, which the sources have preserved, may serve to illustrate the development of the independent insurgent movement.

Thus on 4 September at daybreak the leaseholder of Porażyn[^297], Borzęcki, accompanied by 6 armed peasants and 2 representatives of the nobility, burst into the post office at Pniewy [Pinne]; he took 9 horses and a small mail cart, issuing the official in exchange a suitable certificate. The incident had the consequence that the Berlin mail reached Poznań with a delay of two days. The same had happened earlier at Słupca, Września [Wreschen], Kostrzyn [Kostschin], Czempiń, Kościan and Śmigiel. In these places the insurgents took horses and destroyed the postal emblems. At Słupca they deprived the postmaster Sadowski of his office, whose place was taken by the former retired postmaster Michalicki. In the Śmigiel district they again levied a recruit, but had to use force, because the peasants did not join them voluntarily[^298].

In the Netze District, in the neighbourhood of Kcynia and Gołańcz [Gollantsch], the insurgents under Krupecki's leadership continued to be active. In both places they seized the salt depots, confiscated the money collected from the hearth tax, removed the Prussian eagles, and tried to induce the mayors to swear the oath

[99] <!-- p.99 -->

to loyalty to the fatherland. Their whole band then set off in the direction of Wągrowiec, from where on 20 September an emissary of Krupecki appeared before the mayor in Rogoźno [Rogasen] with a demand that men be supplied to bear arms. Two days later this same detachment, together with the Poznań insurgents under Niemojewski and the Gniezno insurgents under Lipski, clashed, as we know, with a detachment of Székely's corps at Kcynia [^a99]. Colonel Hinrichs, who had come to the aid of the fighting Prussians, had to fall back to Szubin in the face of the steadily growing number of insurgents. The Poles had 22 wounded in this skirmish and as many taken prisoner; the Prussians only 5 wounded and 2 horses killed[^299].

According to the "Südpreussische Zeitung" of 24 September[^300], insurgents were also gathering in the vicinity of Zduny and near Śrem, from where a considerable detachment of theirs, though estimated on too high a scale, being reckoned at 2,000 peasants, was said to have set out in the night of 19 to 20 September in the direction of Kłecko. Only in Leszno and its surroundings, ever since General Manstein had been stationed there as garrison, did quiet prevail[^301].

From the time of the capture of Bydgoszcz, before Dąbrowski sent detachments out into the Netze District, dates an attack by insurgents on Chodzież [Kolmar]. On 4 October 40 armed men halted outside the town, whereupon 24 of them rode into it, took cloth from the local Jews against receipts and seized the whole stock of salt, at once selling it off at a low price to the citizens. With the 400 thalers received from the sale the insurgents set out on their way back. Even before they had left the town a small episode took place. A patrol of Trenck's hussars arrived from Piła, but, informed in good time of the presence of the insurgents, halted outside the town. One of the hussars, a bolder man, nevertheless dashed into the town and ran straight into 2 soldiers and 2 armed peasants standing in front of the mayor's resi

[100] <!-- p.100 -->

dence. Having wrested a lance with a pennon from one of the peasants, the Prussian fired his pistol and began to flee. The insurgents, alarmed by the shot, at once rushed in pursuit of the fugitive, but at the sight of the remaining hussars turned back. Three days later this same band of insurgents seized the Prussian mail sent out from Piła[^302].

In the districts of Wieluń and Ostrzeszów the armed movement, begun successfully in the third decade of August, weakened considerably in the course of September, although there were no Prussian troops nearby. Michał Jerlicz, who was to form an armed force, returned home to the region of Działoszyn. Nor did the head of the rising, Colonel Stokowski, hold out, and he soon disbanded his detachment for want of any means of feeding it. It was he, writes Kierzkowski in his memoir[^303], who, "standing with 500 insurgent horse", at the moment when the Prussian king was travelling by post from Warsaw through Wieluń towards Silesia, "played at cards, and the insurgents slept well". When he later learned of the passage of so important a person, "it grieved him greatly that, when he could have seized the king, he had neglected the opportunity. He threw his cap on the ground, but that was of no further use". Of the other organisers of the rising in this district, Psarski, so zealous at the outset, returned to his estate of Bobrownik[^304]. After some time the rising gathered strength again. At Bobrownik deliberations were held on how to restore discipline in the loosening insurgent ranks, and the dignity of deputy commander was entrusted to an old officer of the Polish army, Korycki. Psarski went to Ostrzeszów and there kindled anew among the citizens zeal for the national cause, and ordered Stokowski to justify himself for his conduct. On 9 October, with an armed force by now organised, they set out for Kępno and took away

[101] <!-- p.101 -->

a stock of salt there, in order to sell it off afterwards among the inhabitants of the neighbourhood. Lenartowicz, head of the rising in the Sieradz region, soon joined Korycki, and under Stokowski's supreme command expeditions were mounted to the neighbouring small towns of Wieruszów and Bolesławice[^305] and to the Opatów estates. Psarski, marching with the troops, set up commissions for civil matters in the several localities, took the oath of fidelity, and on the estates of Count Maltzahn ordered the labour dues to be reduced by half; he ordered the supply of recruits and provisions. It was also intended to extend the rising to the Silesian border towns, such as Byczyna [Pitschen], Rychtal [Reichthal], Międzybórz [Neumittelwalde], Wołczyn [Konstadt], Oleśnica, Bralin, Namysłów and Syców [Groß Wartenberg], and to send appropriate summonses there from the camp at Węglowice. The owner of Ligota, a village lying on the border, Wiewiórski, received these appeals, but forwarded them, still unopened, to the Landrat (district commissioner) Teichmann in Wartenberg. The armed movement in the neighbourhood of Wieluń and Ostrzeszów could not, however, assume any greater proportions, since many inhabitants of that land did not believe from the outset in its success, and the Jews were slow in supplying arms and ammunition. It even happened that the mayor of Wieluń, Olszanowski, taking advantage of the insurgents' having left the town, ordered the scythemen keeping guard in their absence to go home, and instructed the town servants to destroy their weapons[^306].

In another part of old Greater Poland, in Dobrzyń Land, the fortunes of the rising likewise took an unfavourable turn, because the citizens there lacked perseverance in the undertaking once begun. Around 10 September the rifles and uniforms once taken were returned to the Prussian post at Rypin and the captured hussars were released, together with Lieutenant Branky. Many insurgents even presented themselves

[102] <!-- p.102 -->

voluntarily to the Prussian detachment at Sierpc with the notification that they wished to "return to their duties"; the rest, who had not yet fallen so low, hid in the surrounding forests. These events, favourable to the Prussians, were promptly noted by the newspaper "Südpreussische Zeitung"[^307], with the remark that the insurgents seemed already to regret their deed; let us remember, however, that at just that time the formidable Colonel de Székely was raging in Kuyavia, spreading terror among the population by his inhuman conduct.

To subdue the Greater Poles there were, besides Colonel Székely, also Generals Schwerin and Elsner, of whom the first, as we know, had moved against the rebellious provinces a few days before the suspension of the siege of Warsaw, and the second at the moment of the Prussians' withdrawal from the entrenchments before the capital. General Manstein, who had arrived in Greater Poland shortly before the outbreak of the insurrection, and Colonel Dossow, sent there at the end of August, also went on clashing with the insurgents.

General Elsner with his corps arrived on 15 September at Rogoźno near Widawa in the Sieradz region and from there, for the purpose of cooperation, established contact with Colonel Dossow, stationed at Widawa; on 17 September he moved to Sieradz, cleared the forests there of insurgents, and then, having crossed the Warta, proceeded by the road through Kalisz to Leszno. His companion Dossow remained for the time being in the Sieradz region, in order, with the help of the Łęczyca garrison, to destroy a detachment composed of several hundred insurgents who were crossing the Warta at Uniejów in the direction of Łęczyca. General Manstein, until the middle of September, chased fruitlessly after the insurgents in the forests between Dolsk and Śrem, after which on the 21st of that month he set out for Mosina, wishing to manoeuvre in the neighbourhood of the Warta together with the commandant of Poznań, Colonel Diether. The plan of joint action was not

[103] <!-- p.103 -->

carried out, however, since Diether was afraid of weakening the garrison of Poznań in view of the favourable manifestations towards the insurrection in that city, and preferred first to await from Międzyrzecz [Meseritz] the reinforcing battalion of Cronsatz together with detachments of riflemen and cavalry. Manstein now moved towards Gostyń [Gostyn] with the intention of cooperating with General Elsner. On the road to Leszno, at Poniec, Elsner received on 27 September word from Manstein of his march on Gostyń, together with a summons to go with him towards Jarocin [Jarotschin], in order to support Major-General Schwerin in a planned attack on the insurgents at Konin and to prevent the Polish troops from withdrawing towards Silesia. But this plan too came to nothing. For on that very same day, 27 September, alarming news reached Elsner and Manstein that Dąbrowski was marching on Poznań; they therefore turned back from their road and by forced march went to the aid of the threatened city. Elsner arrived on the spot on 1 October, Manstein the next day[^308]. This was during Bielamowski's approach to Poznań, already described by us, which gave rise among the Prussians to the belief that it was Dąbrowski's advance guard, he intending to take the city. All suppositions of this kind, moreover, had their source in the lack of precise information. The capital of Greater Poland, as a result of the loss of communications, as the "Südpreussische Zeitung" of the day writes[^309], had become like an "island in the sea" cut off from the rest of the world.

The arrival of new troops in Poznań by no means put an end to further fears for the safety of the city. How very great these fears were is shown by a grotesque description, preserved for us by the Prussian official Schwarz, already mentioned, in his recollections, which, in view of the closeness of the dates concerned, can be connected with that consternation which

[104] <!-- p.104 -->

was caused by the appearance of Bielamowski near Poznań. Schwarz recalls that when he arrived in Poznań on 5 October he found the Prussian officials in the church of St Martin, where they had hidden in order to resist, at least for a time, the "superiority" of the insurgents from this highest point of the city and under cover of the Prussian troops. The sight that met Schwarz's eyes was as follows: the floor of the church was covered with straw used for sleeping, and those gathered there ate and drank, laughed and wept by turns, believing that the coming day would be the last of their lives. As time passed in this way and the insurgents somehow did not come, the Germans gradually recovered from their fright and on the following day left their hiding place[^310].

At that time the third of the Prussian generals operating in the territory of Greater Poland, Major-General Schwerin, was approaching Poznań. Until then he had been stationed at Kalisz, to which he had withdrawn before the advancing corps of Dąbrowski. On 23 September he set out by the road to Raszków against a band of insurgents near Dobrzyca, but after 2 days he returned to Kalisz, fearing for the magazine there. On 27 September he moved to Konin, where together with Generals Manstein and Elsner he planned an attack on the insurgents there; but on news that Dąbrowski was said to be at Słupca and a detachment of scythemen at Pyzdry, he went back again to Kalisz and for the moment contented himself with gathering information about the enemy. Schwerin set out toward Poznań only on 2 October, by the road through Pleszew [Pleschen], Jarocin, Śrem and Kórnik [^311]. At Pleszew he robbed the castellan Gorzeński, but he was reportedly not to get away with the loot any farther than Radlin[^312]. In that place, as Adam Turno describes in his memoir[^313], Captain of Cavalry (rotmistrz) Łojewski, sent by Kościuszko with an order to the Greater Poland insurrections, was at that time staying as a guest of the parish priest. Having learned that Schwerin

[105] <!-- p.105 -->

was in the village, Łojewski resolved to drive him out of there and used the following stratagem. When it had grown quite dark, he mounted his horse and rode toward the right wing of the Prussian camp, while he ordered his orderly to ride to the left. Both began to fire pistols and to give the command "squadrons forward". Schwerin, not suspecting the ruse, took such fright that he left behind everything he had robbed from Gorzeński and "fled through Witaszyce all night, so that he did not halt until Gostyń". How much truth there is in this story is not known, but it must be stated that similar alarms occurred often at that time[^314]. Of other places, Schwerin plundered the small town of Żerków [Zerkow] and the villages of Miniszew and Panienka[^315], and in the course of his further march, on 6 October, he stripped Eleonora Zabłocka, née Bogacka, at Śrem completely[^316]; her husband Jan was in the insurrection. He took her food, all her wardrobe, and all her livestock, and so laden with spoil he finally reached Poznań 3 days later. The number of Prussian soldiers in the city was now considerable, for it amounted to 6 battalions and 20 squadrons[^317].

Bielamowski followed Schwerin's movements. On 5 October Dąbrowski sent him from Bydgoszcz an order[^318] to march with his detachment at once to where the column of Major-General Schwerin was, with the instruction that he should start no engagement and should spread the word "that our army is leaving this region and is to attack General Schwerin". Bielamowski carried out the task entrusted to him conscientiously and with great skill. In a report of 12 October[^319] he gave Dąbrowski precise information on Schwerin's movements from the moment of his leaving Kalisz up to his entry into Poznań. He wrote that a detachment[^320] had set out from Poznań toward the small town of Kłecko, whither he too had been obliged to go, but that it afterwards

[106] <!-- p.106 -->

withdrew to Murowana Goślina, and he to Rogowo, for "both" were afraid of each other. The report ended with the words: "among the Prussians I have the reputation of marching in the vanguard before His Excellency's column, and therefore they fear me, for they think that the column is always following behind me..."

The rising in Greater Poland, which could not be so easily crushed by force of arms, which checked the plans of the Prussian king and hampered the movements of his troops, caused the Prussian king to try to master the situation not only by arms but also by means of proclamations, now threatening the Greater Poles, now, for a change, containing gentler turns of phrase.

Accordingly a patent[^321] appeared, dated 24 September at Wrocław, issued during the stay there of Frederick William II on his way to Berlin, in which this monarch stated "with just displeasure" (mit gerechtem Unwillen) that in carrying out the intention of bringing happiness to Greater Poland, from the moment of the occupation of this province, "various restless heads " had stood in the way. For the breaking of the "sacred bonds of fidelity and obedience" the patent threatened the participants in the insurrection with imprisonment and even death, and the nobility moreover with the loss of noble status and of estates[^322] that were to fall to the share of "faithful and deserving vassals". Finally the king expressed regret for those of his South Prussian subjects who, in fear for their lives and property, had taken their places in the ranks of the "troublemakers", causing, together with them, alarm among the peaceful inhabitants of the province; he refuted the rumours of persecution of Catholics and of an increase in taxes, and, wishing to win over the peasant estate, he abolished the tax on the so-called "Musikzettli"[^a106].

Despite the threats with which the patent menaced the insurgents, the armed movement of the Greater Poles did not abate. Soon there

[107] <!-- p.107 -->

came the days of the greatest triumph of the insurgent arms in this region, crowned by the capture of Bydgoszcz. It then seemed to the Prussian king that the troops of Generals Elsner, Manstein and Schwerin, shut up in the capital of Greater Poland, were unable to withstand the power of Dąbrowski's corps, which threatened at once Poznań, Toruń and even Gdańsk. True, from his Potsdam residence the king surveyed the situation in Poland with a critical eye and could rightly suppose that, in view of the rapid marches of Suvorov's Russian army, the Polish troops before Warsaw were passing through very hard moments; but on the other hand he could not make light of events in Greater Poland, all the more because the fate of the Prussian officials seized at Bydgoszcz lay in Dąbrowski's hands. For that reason, in the edict of 11 October issued at Berlin he spoke to the "rebels" in an already more gracious tone. He promised not to punish the insurgents without first examining their guilt, but only to place them in a safe place and afterwards to try them according to law before the civil authorities; he promised all citizens freedom of conscience, equality before the law and in tax obligations, and above all the paternal care of the Prussian government[^323].

The edict appeared just in time, for ominous clouds were already gathering over the insurrection. The guarantee of cardinal civil rights contained in it was in future to shield the inhabitants of Greater Poland, at least in part, from the arbitrariness of the victors[^324].

## Chapter IV

### THE FALL OF THE INSURRECTION — DĄBROWSKI'S RETREAT AND THE LAST ARMED EFFORTS OF THE GREATER POLES

[108] <!-- p.108 -->

While the armed movement in Greater Poland was developing favourably, to the glory of Polish arms, the situation in the other theatres of insurrectionary fighting was growing ever more grave. True, on the Narew the Poles were still holding their ground against the enemy, even gaining certain successes over the Prussians, but the Lithuanian troops, whose morale had deteriorated considerably after the fall of Vilnius, were no longer capable of resisting the pressure of the Russians. One after another the corps withdrew towards Grodno, abandoning even Samogitia, the holding of which Kościuszko had recommended. Meanwhile Suvorov was approaching Brest-Litovsk with a corps of 18,000 and 60 guns. To check this column Kościuszko sent Józef Sierakowski with a division numbering some 8,000 soldiers[^325], but fortune did not favour Sierakowski. On 19 September he suffered a great defeat near Brest; Suvorov took all 28 of his guns and scattered his division, all but annihilating it. The Russian General Fersen, who had until then been stationed in the vicinity of Kozienice[^326], deceived the vigilance of General Poniński, whose task it was to watch him and not to let him pass to the right bank of the Vistula, and on 4 October, with the whole corps[^327] that

[109] <!-- p.109 -->

had taken part in the siege of Warsaw, he crossed the Vistula. This step had unpleasant consequences. First, Fersen was cutting one of the main routes by which Warsaw was supplied with food, and besides, there was the fear that he would join Suvorov, who was holding the line of the Bug[^328].

In these circumstances Kościuszko considered it necessary to destroy Fersen's corps, and on 7 October he set out from the Mokotów camp. When, on that same day, a halt was made at Korytnica, Major Molski arrived in the evening, sent by Dąbrowski with a report on the capture of Bydgoszcz[^329]. The favourable news, proclaimed at once to the soldiers, raised their spirits. It was, however, the last ray of joy. Three days later, on 10 October, a catastrophe occurred unexpectedly. At Maciejowice, where an engagement with Fersen took place, Kościuszko's corps of 7,000[^330] was almost completely destroyed and 22 guns were lost. Kościuszko himself, gravely wounded, together with his adjutant Fiszer and his secretary Julian Ursyn Niemcewicz, and Generals Kamieński, Kniaziewicz and Sierakowski, fell into captivity[^331].

On 12 October the news of this rout reached Warsaw, causing great agitation and dejection. The new Commander chosen that same day at a session of the Supreme National Council was Tomasz Wawrzecki. He was a man personally worthy and a great patriot, a former deputy to the Great Sejm, lately distinguished by his part in the rising in Lithuania, but otherwise "in knowledge not exceeding the intellectual sphere of a Lithuanian landowner". Wawrzecki himself was also aware that he lacked the qualifications of a leader, and for four days he declined to accept the honourable office, but in the end he yielded to the entreaties and urgings of all[^332].

[110] <!-- p.110 -->

In order not to allow despair to take hold, the Supreme National Council issued appeals, encouraging the citizens to further strenuous action. On 16 October it addressed a separate proclamation to the Greater Poles, calling upon them not to bury "the hopes of the Fatherland through the loss of a man, albeit a most estimable one, whose fate cannot and ought not to determine the fate of the Fatherland". On the same day a letter also appeared, directed to the citizens of the Sieradz voivodeship and Wieluń Land, urging them to apply themselves to the rising and to support the work begun "most valiantly and most eagerly". The Supreme National Council appointed for them a new plenipotentiary in the person of citizen Okołów, because the plenipotentiary Józef Wybicki "is not in a position to fulfil the duties entrusted to him with regard to the Sieradz voivodeship and Wieluń Land"[^333].

These efforts to sustain the spirit of the nation were, however, in vain. Most Poles already regarded the defeat of 10 October as the end of the insurrection and all further exertion as pointless[^334]. For the catastrophe of Maciejowice had checked "the impetus of the revolution"[^335].

Dąbrowski, having learned on 14 October, on his return from the march on Toruń, of the defeat at Maciejowice, as well as of the severing of communication with Warsaw and of the planned destruction of his division by the Prussian forces, considered it necessary, without waiting for an order[^336], to leave Bydgoszcz and return to Warsaw. He began the preparations for the march back at once, and on the following day set out from Bydgoszcz with the main corps in the direction of Gniewkowo. He took with him more than 400 prisoners, among them also the arrested Prussian officials: the councillor Grabowski, Broskovius, the director of posts Tschepius and the mayor Radzibor[^337].

[111] <!-- p.111 -->

Behind the army trailed an enormous baggage train consisting of nearly 200 provision and field wagons, loaded with cloth for 4,000 men and with many other military supplies[^338]. The Greater Poland cavalry detachments sent to Nakło, Łabiszyn, Barcin [Bartschin] and Żnin received orders likewise to begin their retreat on 15 October and, advancing slowly towards Konin, to destroy all the bridges on the Noteć; their task was to cover Dąbrowski's right flank. Bielamowski was instructed first to observe the enemy in Poznań, and then to go by way of Pyzdry and Konin and join the main corps. Colonel Sokolnicki with his riflemen and Dąbrowski's cavalry brigade closed off the left bank of the Vistula opposite Toruń from Zielona Woda to Czerniewice, in order to make it difficult for Colonel Ledivary, encamped at Podgórz, to observe the movements of the corps[^339].

The march proceeded at first at a slow pace, since the roads were sandy and the soldiers tired quickly. For this reason Dąbrowski encamped near Gniewkowo on 15 October itself and rested there the whole of the following day. On 16 and 17 October Colonel Sokolnicki, under cover of a thick autumn fog, attacked Ledivary's outposts, after which on 18 October, not even pursued, he set off towards Nieszawa. Dąbrowski with the corps marched out of Gniewkowo on 17 October and the next day reached Włocławek, while the baggage train together with the prisoners reached Brześć Kujawski[^340]. At Włocławek Dąbrowski learned from Major Molski, and from citizen Miaskowski, whom he had sent out from near Gniezno, further details of the battle of Maciejowice. The former brought him, moreover, a commission as lieutenant-general and "a very fine broadsword for his successful action"; the latter brought exact news "of the enemy's strong vigilance[^341]".

[112] <!-- p.112 -->

And indeed the enemy was vigilant. On 13 October old Schwerin learned of the defeat at Maciejowice from the lips of an eyewitness of those events, Lieutenant von Heiligenstadt, and from then on his attention was occupied by one thought only. He was certain that Dąbrowski would have to fall back, and he began to ponder how to set up a barrier against this enemy, who could be dangerous even in retreat. He knew that Dąbrowski had three roads to choose from. He could withdraw by the old road across the Bzura, by which he had set out from Warsaw, or by new ones, of which one led across the Warta in the direction of Wieluń and Sieradz, and further between Radomsko and Częstochowa and through the Sandomierz voivodeship, while the other ran along the right bank of the Vistula, in which case he would have to cross above Toruń and cut through Favrat's corps on the Narew. Schwerin tried to prepare for all these possibilities, and even took into account a march by Dąbrowski on Gdańsk. At first, as late as 16 October, he thought that Dąbrowski would go by the right bank of the Vistula, and therefore intended to reinforce Favrat's corps, wishing to leave to it the merit of destroying so elusive an enemy. This rather strange supposition Schwerin formed before he had received news of Dąbrowski's return from Toruń to Bydgoszcz. Only when on the following day he learned that Dąbrowski had returned to Bydgoszcz did he himself intend to deal with him[^342].

To this end he made the following dispositions. From Rawa he moved a magazine to Piotrków, and from there in turn he ordered the siege artillery to be transported to Silesia, so as to be able to act freely and not to scatter his forces among numerous garrisons. Beyond the little river Rawka he dispatched 3 battalions and 5 squadrons with guns, from where, in case of need, they were to be directed to Łowicz or Piotrków. Now, having

![Antoni Madaliński. Z miniatury w Muzeum XX. Czartoryskich](../2026-10-28-0060_2R.jpg)

*Antoni Madaliński. From a miniature in the Princes Czartoryski Museum*

[113] <!-- p.113 -->

with him still 7 battalions and 12 squadrons, he was waiting only for news from Toruń and from the commanders of the remaining corps sent to Greater Poland, so as to know in which direction to march for the destruction of Dąbrowski's division. In this action he was to be assisted, should the encounter with the enemy take place near the Bzura, by General Klinkowström, as well as by Colonel Ledivary, who was following in the enemy's tracks from Toruń, and by Generals Pollitz and Schwerin the Younger, once they had united their corps. The forces thus combined, drawn up on the line from Kamionna to Sochaczew together with a powerful artillery, would then have amounted to 15,000 men[^343].

Dąbrowski meanwhile left Włocławek on 19 October and directed his march towards the lower course of the Bzura, to Gąbin, while Madaliński, to cover his right flank, went with his brigade and Sokolnicki's riflemen in the direction of Kiernozia by way of Gostynin and Slesin[^344]. Only now did Colonel Ledivary rouse himself and set out from Podgórz to pursue the enemy in retreat. He still caught up with the Poles' rear guard at Służewo[^345], dispersed it and took some baggage, but just two days later, on 21 October, he lost all contact with his opponent[^346].

Dąbrowski was by then already at Gąbin, and there he formed what was almost a new plan of action. He wished to wait "for relief or for the making of a communication across the Bzura, and then to strike at Ledivary and force his way into Greater Poland again". There was, however, no time for waiting. Patrols reported that the enemy stood close by, and "willy-nilly it was necessary to cross the Bzura" and abandon Greater Poland[^347].

Meanwhile the insurgent supreme command in Warsaw spared no effort to make it possible for Dąbrowski to cross the Bzura[^348]. The corps of General Kołysko, stationed at Błonie,

[114] <!-- p.114 -->

was reinforced to 3,000 men and sent to attack the Prussian post at Sochaczew. Prince Józef Poniatowski set out from Warsaw towards Kamionna with the same strength. On 18 October both corps drew near the Bzura, crossing the exceedingly dense forests of those parts, which made their march wholly unobserved. The next day Sochaczew and Kamionna were attacked, but the Prussians repulsed the attack. General Klinkowström, alarmed by the roar of the guns, marched out of the camp at Potoki, reached both bombarded places, restored communication between them, but on the second day returned to Potoki, fearing for the magazines at Łowicz. On 20 October Kołysko, reinforced with troops from Warsaw, renewed the attack on Sochaczew, received two days later the help of a detachment of Prince Józef's corps, and on the 23rd of that month attacked the town again "unluckily but with stubborn courage"[^349]. At the same time the Poles also shelled Kamionna, though from a respectable distance[^350].

The events that began to unfold on the Bzura moved Lieutenant-General Schwerin to take new steps. He understood that the decisive moment was approaching. He therefore sent off as quickly as possible 2 battalions to reinforce General Klinkowström's corps, and himself resolved, with the rest of his army, to leave at last the camp at Chrzonowice and take up position in the proper theatre of war. Before the march he issued final orders: he sent 3 battalions of Bonin, 5 squadrons of Bieberstein and 1 of Trenck in the direction of Rawa, and the battalion of Pfau and 2 squadrons of Bieberstein's regiment to reinforce the garrison of Piotrków, and, delaying no longer, set out on 20 October with 5 battalions and 11 squadrons for Skierniewice, and then for Łowicz and Kiernozia. He halted at Kiernozia on 22 October, and there learned,

[115] <!-- p.115 -->

that Dąbrowski had marched out of Gąbin on the previous day in the direction of Mistrzewice on the Bzura. It was Schwerin's duty to occupy at once this point, which, as could be foreseen, was to serve the Greater Poland corps as its crossing place; there a mortal blow was to be dealt the opponent. Schwerin was now 25 km away from Mistrzewice and could have arrived in time, but he did not hurry. On 23 October, in view of the wooded country and of soldiers weakened by incessant marching, he advanced only a little and pitched camp at Kocierzów, south-east of Kiernozia, at a distance of 2 miles from Mistrzewice[^351].

The consequences of this slowness soon became apparent. At the moment when the Prussian commander was only setting out from Kiernozia, Dąbrowski had already reached the Bzura and at 7 o'clock in the morning, under cover of a thick fog, began to cross to the other bank of the river. Amid the roar of the guns at Sochaczew, which was being attacked at that time by Kołysko, one detachment of troops after another entered the river bed and took up position on the heights of Brochów; at last the wagons with the booty came down, and at about 4 o'clock in the afternoon the crossing was completed, without the slightest hindrance from "even one Prussian[^352]": In the evening Madaliński arrived with the rear guard, still later the Łęczyca rising, and the night from 23 to 24 passed quietly, although word of the enemy came from all sides[^353].

This enemy had, however, his reasons for not venturing to engage his opponent at so decisive a moment. The troops remained unconcentrated, and thereby the hopes vanished that it would be possible to surround Dąbrowski's division and force it to lay down its arms almost without bloodshed. After all, the junction of the corps of Generals Pollitz and Schwerin at Słupca[^354], planned for

[116] <!-- p.116 -->

19 October, did not come about. On that day General Pollitz did reach Słupca by way of Łęczyca, Koło and Kazimierz, but waited there in vain for the arrival of Major-General Schwerin, who seemed to be acting contrary to orders. The latter was still in Poznań, where on 17 October news of the defeat at Maciejowice was brought to him also by Lt. von Heiligenstadt[^355], and only on 21 October, on the express order of his monarch, did he leave the city, to join General Pollitz at last, four days later[^356], at Słupca. The reason for his long stay in the capital of Greater Poland was fear of the might of an enemy standing nearby, which in reality was only Bielamowski's elusive raiding party[^357].

Since it was impossible to surround Dąbrowski's corps, there remained now only the possibility of fighting an open battle with the enemy, but to this way of extricating himself from an awkward situation, which only incompetent commanders resorted to, Lieutenant-General Schwerin would not consent. After all, he knew neither the enemy's manner of fighting nor his strength, supposing it to amount to about 10,000 regular soldiers. In such conditions he feared a defeat, which would draw after it a strengthening of the insurgent movement, and perhaps even the loss of Greater Poland. For that reason, when on 23 October, at 5 o'clock in the afternoon, General Klinkowström appeared at Kocierzów with the news of the attack on Sochaczew and of Dąbrowski's possible simultaneous crossing, Schwerin pretended not to believe it, or perhaps really did not believe it, "as one long refuses to believe in misfortune"[^358]. He did set out at last from Kocierzów towards Mistrzewice, and, having joined Klinkowström's corps at Sochaczew, arrived on the spot at 8 o'clock in the morning of the 24th. He at once ordered the firing of

[117] <!-- p.117 -->

cannon, to which the Polish guns replied from the opposite bank, but it soon became clear that "on both sides it was a vain waste of powder and shot[^359]". When the pointless cannonade died down, Schwerin ordered a rest, but did not go in pursuit of the Polish army, even when towards evening Colonel Ledivary arrived at Witkowice[^360].

Meanwhile on the other side of the Bzura Dąbrowski, having first sent the prisoners and the booty off to Warsaw, began to entrench himself at Brochów, in order "to operate further from there both offensive and deffensive[^361]". On 24 October he received from Warsaw the order to storm Kamionna and Sochaczew anew together with Prince Józef and to clear the Bzura of Prussian troops, but he did not obey the summons, just as earlier he had not obeyed the instruction to support Poniatowski in his uncertain attack on Kamionna. Surveying the situation with a keen eye, he realised clearly that it was impossible to withstand the mighty army of Lieutenant-General Schwerin, once the latter should unite all his corps. He therefore handed over the command to General Rymkiewicz on 27 October and set out for Warsaw, summoned there to a council of war[^362].

By leading the Greater Poland division to a place of safety, Dąbrowski fulfilled his duty. Of all the commanders of the Polish forces, he alone rose to the height of the task entrusted to him. During the 40 days that the expedition lasted he always operated successfully, though he had arrayed against him a Prussian army seasoned in battle. He himself led an army that was far inferior to its opponent in numbers and in quality, yet he was able to work a miracle with it. He crossed the Bzura, the Warta, the Noteć and the Vistula "always in the face of a superior enemy". He took Bydgoszcz and aroused in the Prussians anxiety for such towns as Poznań, Toruń and Gdańsk.

[118] <!-- p.118 -->

There was a period when, in view of the defeats on the other theatres of the insurrectionary struggle, favourable news of the movements of his corps was the one thing that cheered the hearts of the inhabitants of the capital and sustained faith in the future of Poland among the army and at those foreign courts that were well disposed towards the Polish nation. It was clear that he could not venture upon the destruction of an enemy who surpassed him in every respect, and that he was often compelled to avoid battle and confine himself rather to guerrilla warfare[^363]; yet he absorbed all the enemy's forces and, together with the Greater Poland insurgents, managed to bear almost the entire burden of the war west of the Vistula. When he could no longer expect further successes owing to the fatal turn of events on the main theatre of war, he set out on the return march and withdrew successfully with his whole corps to Warsaw, although the entire effort of the Prussian commander was directed against him, and the king repeatedly sent him orders to destroy the Greater Poland division by every means at his disposal[^364]. Dąbrowski's expedition also had considerable significance abroad. It undermined the position of the Prussian king in the partition negotiations, deprived him of part of the land in the Sandomierz and Kraków regions, discouraged him altogether from waging a simultaneous war in the west, showed the world that the famous army of Frederick the Great had incompetent commanders, and proved that "above the most seasoned routine there always prevails a spirit of civic self-sacrifice and zeal for a good cause[^365]".

In the annexed territories, meanwhile, after the departure of Dąbrowski's troops, Colonel Hinrichs, the worthy successor[^366] of Székely, set about restoring the old order. And all the gains recently achieved by the arms of the Polish army went for nothing. At Świecie

[119] <!-- p.119 -->

Hinrichs removed the insurgent commission and put up the Prussian emblems again. On 25 October he arrived at Bydgoszcz and restored Prussian authority there. On the following day the people of Bydgoszcz were already swearing a renewed oath of fidelity and obedience to the Prussian king, and soon, at the summons of the town's president Korkwitz, the Prussian officials returned to their official duties. Cavalry detachments sent out by Hinrichs removed the traces of the insurrection at Nakło, Żnin, Barcin, Kcynia and Gołańcz, while Hinrichs himself soon moved from Bydgoszcz to Inowrocław. There, on 2 November, he issued an order to seize insurgents and to notify the nearest posts of them, and for every insurgent captured by the population he set a monetary reward. From Inowrocław Hinrichs proceeded by way of Gniewkowo towards Nieszawa, in order to suppress the armed movement completely in that quarter as well[^367].

General Beyern pursued the same aim. On 23 October he was at Czarnków [Czarnikau], on the 25th at Rogoźno, and finally on the 27th he reached Gniezno by way of Kiszkowo and remained there for several days[^368].

Major-General Schwerin alone continued to show little enterprise in hunting down the enemy. Having no clear grasp of the situation because communications were hindered, he set out only on 26 October from Słupca by way of Kazimierz and Kliszewo to attack Dąbrowski's returning column near Włocławek. On the way he learned that 1,200 insurgents had occupied Pyzdry[^369], but he advanced slowly, until at last a patrol sent towards Brześć Kujawski reported to him that Dąbrowski had left that district a week before. He therefore had no reason to go to Włocławek, turned back from the road, and directed his march to Koło, where he arrived on 30 October[^370].

[120] <!-- p.120 -->

At the same time as the actions of the Prussian generals, the insurgents won a number of successes, which show that the armed movement in Greater Poland, although it did not grow in scale, nevertheless did not die out after all. Bands of partisans continued to roam there, paralysing the offices, attacking Prussian posts, seizing treasuries and magazines, destroying state emblems and cutting communications. In numbers these detachments were still quite considerable. In the neighbourhood of Gąbin as many as 3,000 confederates from the Poznań and Kuyavian voivodeships had halted, having separated from Dąbrowski's division in order to continue the fight against the enemy on their own. On the afternoon of 25 October 500 horsemen from this corps attacked Włocławek and soon sold off the entire stock of salt, collected 6,820 thalers of war contribution, and on the night of 25 to 26 October fired upon the weak Prussian post at Bobrownik, until finally the whole band made off in the direction of Brześć Kujawski. On 27 October Mniewski seized Nieszawa with an armed detachment. The insurgents, moreover, were busy in the Sieradz and Łęczyca regions. In the vicinity of the Warta, south of Uniejów, the intrepid Bielamowski was manoeuvring with part of Dąbrowski's brigade; General Pollitz's troops prevented him from joining the main corps at Gąbin on 18 October[^371].

The armed movement in Greater Poland continued to disturb Lieutenant-General Schwerin and to restrict the freedom of his movements on the main theatre. When he received Suvorov's summons to bring the capital of Poland to its fall by joint forces, he replied that for the moment he could not heed it. He feared an attack by the Greater Poland insurgents on the rear of his army, and it was with them above all that he now wished to deal. His anxiety grew when Ledivary's reconnaissance, sent in the direction of Błonie, reported to him

[121] <!-- p.121 -->

that new corps were allegedly intending to break through into Greater Poland. In order to be able to cooperate with Suvorov "most effectively in forcing Warsaw to surrender and in bringing the war to an end[^372]", he devised a new plan, according to which a suitable disposition of the troops was to protect South Prussia from a fresh invasion on the one hand, and on the other to blockade Warsaw and force it by hunger to surrender. The plan was to come into force on 11 or 12 November. But when, in accordance with the new plan, the troops had already occupied the positions assigned to them, when General Pollitz had occupied the passage between Gąbin and Iłów, where a breakthrough of the enemy was to be expected; Major-General Schwerin had taken up positions inside Greater Poland on the Warta, resting his right wing on Szadek and keeping in communication with General Pollitz; General Beyern, after occupying Włocławek, had reached the Vistula with his left wing and extended his right towards the Warta, in order to cover Poznań, Gniezno and Toruń; and Schwerin himself had at last gone from Łowicz to Skierniewice in order to secure the area from Łowicz to Rawa, it was then, on 12 November, that Favrat arrived to take over the command in place of Schwerin, whom the king was removing, dissatisfied with his lack of activity[^373].

Meanwhile the heart of the Commonwealth, its capital, had already ceased to beat.

Amid the general confusion, as the front on the Narew was breaking up (it had, among other merits, that of holding back part of the Prussian forces from striking at Dąbrowski's division), as the Lithuanian army was disintegrating, and as enormous desertion took hold of the whole army and entire corps surrendered, Suvorov was approaching Warsaw by forced marches at the end of October[^374].

At such a moment Wybicki's report on the successful expedition to Greater Poland was received "as

[122] <!-- p.122 -->

in a violent conflagration where everyone bustles about out of his senses[^375]". The Supreme National Council, in this almost last hour of the Commonwealth's existence, sought ways of rescue in new military combinations, though of little realism[^376]; it offered a large reward to the corps that should be able to "clear the river Bzura of the enemy and open up Greater Poland"[^377]. At the council of war in Warsaw on 27 October, Dąbrowski put forward, as yet another way of rescue, the abandonment of Praga and Warsaw, which in his opinion were not capable of defending themselves. He advised "marching into Greater Poland with the whole strength of the Commonwealth and with numerous artillery", with the king and the Supreme Council, and there, despite the fall of the capital, to see the winter through with the surviving army and government, awaiting French intervention or a falling-out on the part of one of the neighbouring states[^378].

The premises of the plan were sound and rested on rational foundations. For winter was approaching, desertion was beginning to creep into the Prussian ranks, diseases were rampant, and there was considerable "discontent" there on account of the unsuccessful campaign. The corps left behind in Greater Poland by Dąbrowski were "growing immensely". Major Bielamowski's detachment numbered 600 horse, and the Łęczyca, Sieradz and Kuyavian risings were already 3,000 strong. If, therefore, the main army, advancing westwards, had taken the line of the Bzura, the Prussians would have been caught between two fires, and it would then have been easy to cut all their communications together with the supply of provisions. Pressed from two sides, with an army partly disorganised, they would have retreated behind the Oder, or at least behind the Warta. The Russians' pursuit of the Polish army would have been very difficult and slow owing to the lack of means of subsistence[^379].

Dąbrowski, however, did not find a majority among those deliberating on the fate of the fatherland who were willing to support his

[123] <!-- p.123 -->

project. Two days thus passed, and the Commander and the Council took no decisive step. Only on 29 October, when Suvorov had already begun to bombard Praga, did an order come that the corps of Dąbrowski and Giedroyć should move towards Rawa in order to observe the Prussians. There they stood idle for several days[^380].

Meanwhile, on 4 November, Suvorov took the entrenchments of Praga after a brief attack; they had been thrown up without any plan and served only for a reflexive defence, and he inflicted a bloody slaughter on the inhabitants of Praga. The capital, fearing a similar fate, surrendered on 6 November, with the proviso that troops who did not lay down their arms might leave it. The Supreme National Council dissolved itself. On 9 November the victorious Suvorov already rode ceremonially into Warsaw[^381].

After the fall of the capital, complete disorder reigned in the army. The soldiers, abandoned by their commanders, threw down their arms in crowds, heedless of the fate of the country or of their comrades; they wished only to save themselves. Dąbrowski alone in this general confusion did not lose his head. With his corps, not yet touched by the ferment, he began a march to the south. His intention was "to hold out in the Sandomierz and Kraków regions, and from there to extend operations into the Sieradz region and farther into Greater Poland[^382]". He also spun plans that, given favourable circumstances, he might perhaps realise his cherished idea of breaking through to France by way of Silesia, Moravia, Bohemia, Bavaria and Swabia, "straight to Landau, or else to Switzerland towards the Rhine"[^383]. During the march, however, "the misfortune of Praga and Warsaw"[^384] made itself felt in his ranks as well. When he encamped at Gostomia, "generals, officers and soldiers from both places"[^385] began to arrive, bringing fear and dread with them. Dąbrowski sought a way of sustaining the spirit of his soldiers

[124] <!-- p.124 -->

and found none other than to assure them[^386] that within three days he would lead them to Greater Poland, where he would begin a new campaign[^387]. In order not to deprive the soldiers of this hope, and also in order not to be cut off from the banks of the Pilica, he disobeyed Wawrzecki's order to withdraw from Gostomia towards Tarczyn or else to Grójec[^388].

Meanwhile the situation worsened with every moment. Denisov and Fersen, having crossed to the left bank of the Vistula, began to pursue the Polish corps withdrawing from Warsaw. The Austrians had already taken Radom and cut off the supply of provisions and forage. There was moreover the fear that the Prussians too would move from Rawa, Łowicz and Piotrków to destroy the remnants of the Polish army, which dwindled with each day. The division of Prince Józef Poniatowski had already ceased to exist, capitulating on 7 November before the troops of General Klinkowström, and Generals Zajączek, Mokronowski, Wyszkowski, Kołysko and Ożarowski had left their corps, which themselves dispersed. Even Madaliński, "that ever well-meaning Pole, deluded by base men", left his detachment at Białobrzegi; his gallant brigade arrived at Gostomia on its own. There too took refuge the Commander Wawrzecki, President Zakrzewski, and Generals Giełgud and Niesiołowski; soon Major Bielamowski also arrived with his cavalry, returning from Greater Poland[^389].

On 12 November, on the strength of Wawrzecki's order to march in haste into the Kraków region, Dąbrowski left Gostomia. On the same day a Russian officer sent by Suvorov, who was carrying the terms of capitulation for the army, was stopped on the road. Wawrzecki sent a letter to the king through General Gorzeński, urging him, in accordance with the earlier resolution of the Supreme Council, to negotiate with the Russian general. In the evening a Prussian trumpeter arrived with a letter to Dąbrowski from the Prussian

[125] <!-- p.125 -->

General Kleist. General Kleist[^390] asked "most humbly" on what terms Dąbrowski would be willing to "enter and join the army of His Prussian Majesty the King"[^391] together with his regular troops. Dąbrowski read the letter and translated it for the Commander in the presence of all the generals. There was much dispute over the reply, until at last it was decided to give an evasive answer, although Dąbrowski himself was of the opinion that matters should have been handled otherwise. He considered that in so grave a position, when there was no hope left, when Russian and Austrian troops surrounded the insurgents on all sides, and when the soldiers, "the infantry above all, for a thousand reasons neither wished nor was able to fight", Kleist ought to be answered with points: 1) a general amnesty in Greater Poland; 2) the placing of all officers in the Prussian army according to their commissions; 3) payment of 4 years' salary to officers unwilling to serve. The Greater Poles, Dąbrowski writes in his memoir on the end of the insurrection, liked the proposal, but Wawrzecki and Zakrzewski opposed it. Dąbrowski did not press the matter, yet "the greater part of the Greater Poles" begged him not to abandon the proposal. He therefore put it forward again, and, so that it should not be supposed that he was seeking "a decent place of maintenance" for himself, he assured them "on his honour" that he would take his dismissal. He hoped that the Greater Poles would not refuse him 6,000 Polish zlotys a year until such time as he might again serve the country. This time the declaration was received fairly well; nevertheless the Commander had weighty reasons for "not approving Dąbrowski's opinion"[^392]

The question of negotiating with the Prussians arose a second time in the course of the further march, at Opoczno, where some citizen came from Major Kloch to confer with Dąbrowski concerning the proposal of General

[126] <!-- p.126 -->

Kleist. Dąbrowski, however, no longer had any "inclination" to surrender his corps of regular troops to the Prussians; he wished only to treat on behalf of the "unfortunate citizens of Greater Poland". Colonel Gorzeński of the Greater Poland rising, who knew German, was therefore appointed to him, to be a witness at the talks with the Prussians; but the meeting did not take place. Meanwhile a courier arrived with word from King Stanisław August[^393] that General Suvorov was "bloodily" crushing those who would not comply with his demands, and had ordered a warning that the rest should not suffer a like misfortune. The last attempt at an understanding with Dąbrowski, which the Prussians made during the march of the Polish troops towards Radoszyce, likewise came to nothing, because "it had already been decided not to engage with the Prussians"[^394]. Thus the securing of the fate of the Greater Poles, who above all could be called "rebels" and who for that reason were threatened with repression by the Prussian government, was not accomplished[^395].

Faithful to the national cause to the last, the Greater Poland insurgents accompanying Dąbrowski did not desert their ranks. Towards the end, another 1,000 horsemen from the Sieradz and Wieluń risings joined, and these, together with the other Greater Poland risings, under the command of General Skórzewski, and with the brigades of Dąbrowski and Madaliński, represented, after the dispersal of all the regiments that had joined Dąbrowski on the way, the Polish army by themselves[^396]. They saw how Dąbrowski, after the unfavourable reply of Suvorov brought by the dispatched Gorzeński, in utmost despair, not knowing what to do next, wished to reach the vicinity of the Galician border, burn the weapons there, and disband Madaliński and his own brigade. They were witnesses when on 18 November General Denisov arrived at Radoszyce

[127] <!-- p.127 -->

with several officers and took with him to Warsaw the Commander Wawrzecki, General Dąbrowski, Giedroyć, Niesiołowski and Giełgud; while President Zakrzewski and General Rymkiewicz, not wishing to share a like fate, had already escaped earlier to Galicia[^397]. And before they themselves, after this de facto capitulation[^398], went with General Skórzewski across the Prussian cordon, they were bidden farewell in tears by Wybicki and Mniewski, who addressed them in these words: "May God keep you in His care; we shall go to seek protection, and when we find it we shall return to you and shall not forget you"[^399].

In Greater Poland the flame of rebellion smouldered throughout November and even into December. The ranks of the insurgents had, it is true, shrunk, since many of them had returned home, but the rest, mostly people of no means who had nothing to lose, went on roaming the forests in more or less numerous bands and carefully avoided contact with the Prussian army[^400].

The last armed efforts of the Greater Poles deserve all the more attention because they had help from nowhere, since already at the beginning of November the enemy had won the victory in the main theatre, and afterwards the new commander, Lieutenant-General Favrat[^401], moved against them with redoubled energy in order to establish peace as quickly as possible in South Prussia. Colonel Hinrichs, who on 6 November had taken up position at Włocławek and was crushing insurgents in the forests between Płock and Włocławek, he ordered to remain in the Vistula region and to watch over the security of communications on the Vistula. He then divided the corps of General Beyern in such a way that 3 battalions and 4 squadrons, operating in the area Gostynin, Gąbin, Kutno, passed under the command of General Elsner, who also commanded the corps of Major-General Schwerin, who for his passivity was sentenced to 2 years in a fortress[^402]; the parts

[128] <!-- p.128 -->

of Beyern's corps that had been left in the vicinity of Gniezno (2 battalions and 3 squadrons) he directed to the Warta, to guard the crossings of that river from Pyzdry to Sieradz and, by sending out "flying columns" (fliegende Kolonne), to attack the insurgents at their assembly points[^403].

Writing of the unceasing disturbances in Greater Poland, the newspaper of the day[^404] called the insurgents "deaf to the voice of reason," since instead of trying to earn the favour of the victor, lest in time it should be too late, they continued to roam the country with arms in hand.

Numerous mentions of the armed movement of the Greater Poles in November and December have been preserved in the sources.

In Kuyavia at that time Ksawery Dąbrowski held out at the head of 500 insurgents. On 6 November he was selling off in Włocławek a large stock of salt taken from the Prussian magazines, but Colonel Hinrichs, arriving unexpectedly, soon took the salt back, thinned out his detachment and drove it from the town. Of the other insurgent commanders, Colonel Sokołowski stood for some time near Brześć Kujawski with a force of about 1,200 men. Near Dobiegniew, opposite Dobrzyń, was Colonel Wolski with 300 armed men[^405].

Driven by Hinrichs from the Vistula districts, the Kuyavian insurgents withdrew to the west, towards Radziejów and Piotrków Kujawski, in order to undertake guerrilla expeditions from there even in the second half of December. Hidden in the dense forests and marshes of that region and making use of byways, they became elusive, and establishing their numbers was not easy. The Kruszwica [Kruschwitz] domain (Amt) estimated these Kuyavian insurgents at several thousand. Their smaller detachments reached as far as Kruszwica, collecting food and forage from the neighbouring villages. News also arrived of a so-called "Kuyavian

The soldiers of this regiment took horses, forage and rents from the district, imposed contributions, collected recruits, and forced some of the nobility by violence to join the rising. On the night of 21 to 22 November three insurgents of the Kuyavian regiment rode into Strzelno, two of them fully armed and the third with only a scythe; they announced that a larger detachment of insurgents would arrive in the town within the next few days, took 300 thalers from the cash box, and then departed[^406].

In view of such events the Prussian officials in Kuyavia did not feel secure. The most various and contradictory reports heightened their anxiety. They expected a new invasion. Indeed, leaflets distributed in the villages as far as Podgórz near Toruń announced plainly the early arrival of a corps already on the march under General Mokronowski, for which forage and provisions were to be collected. In Inowrocław the Germans addressed a request to the commandant of Toruń to reinforce the town's garrison as quickly as possible. Hundt then sent 106 infantry and 7 hussars, so that the garrison grew to 133 men. In Gniewkowo the tax collector sent money to Toruń every day so that it should not fall as booty to the insurgents[^407].

Nor was there yet peace in the neighbourhood of Szubin. In the forests there the indefatigable Krupecki roamed throughout November together with the leaseholder Śminowski. Their detachment numbered 60 armed men, and grain for its maintenance was supplied by a certain clerk from the surrounding estates. On 12 November 2 insurgents from Krupecki's band came to Szubin, where a fair was then being held, and gathered information about the stationing of the Prussian troops[^408].

Insurgents also still held out in the neighbourhood of Gniezno. According to the *Südpreussische Zeitung*[^409] small detachments of them were still roaming there at the end of November. On the 10th of that month a single insurgent, armed with 4 pistols and a broadsword, fell between Trzemeszno and Wilatowo[^410] upon two couriers dispatched from Gniezno and destroyed them completely[^411].

Throughout November the insurgents were active around Wieluń and Sieradz, despite the defeat that Colonel Dossow had inflicted on them there at the beginning of that month, and also in the Kalisz region, reaching as far as Częstochowa and Rawa. Their bands numbered for the most part 50 or 100 men. By day they hid in the forests, and by night they left their hiding places and carried out raids to the detriment of the Prussian government.

In the Sieradz region especially their "escapades" caused much trouble. On the night of 8 to 9 November the insurgents burst into the customs office at Panke(?) in the Krzepice district[^412] and took the cash box from it. They were commanded by Major Pągowski and Captain of Cavalry Karski. The Prussian lieutenant von Lüttwitz, hastening to help from Kłobuck[^413] with 20 cuirassiers of Görtz's regiment and as many infantrymen of Lichnowski's regiment, was attacked by an insurgent detachment consisting of 300 foot and 300 horse. Lüttwitz could not withstand such superiority and was taken prisoner together with 21 soldiers. On 9 November Generals Lipski and Stokowski took up position at Krzepice with the intention of gathering more insurgents and attacking the weakly garrisoned Częstochowa[^414]. It went no further than a plan, however. At about this time the insurgents came to Kłobuck, removed the Prussian emblems and soon departed. According to news of 23 November, given in the *Südpreussische Zeitung*, there stood near Wieluń and in the Ostrzeszów district 2,000 insurgents, supplied with two guns. They spread unpunished from there in a westerly direction as far as

[131] <!-- p.131 -->

Jutrosin [Jutroschin] and Sarnowa [Sarne], taking money from the cash boxes. The Germans consoled themselves that perhaps Major-General Elsner, who at this time with his corps ought to be in the neighbourhood of Sieradz, would be able to curb their "wilfulness[^415]."

In November insurgents were still wandering in the western part of the Poznań voivodeship, in the neighbourhood of Grodzisk. On the 9th of that month 20 armed men came to the manor at Karczewo[^416] and the following night halted in some other village near Grodzisk. A somewhat larger detachment of 30 men came at this time to Grodzisk, where with drawn broadswords they rode through the town, announcing that "the insurrection is rising anew from the ruins" and would soon be drawing peasants into the insurgent ranks. The owners of the village of Turkowo[^417] at once informed their peasants of this, adding that 2,000 insurgents were assembling near Kościan, ready to join those who would once more seize arms and try a better fortune in the struggle with the Prussians. The peasants near Grodzisk were said to have been urged to insurrection by Faustyn Zakrzewski of Rakoniewice [Rakwitz][^418], a member of the criminal court for the Poznań voivodeship, who at the beginning of the rising had attacked the Prussian post office[^419].

The surrounding peasants were also called to arms at the beginning of November by the mayor of the town of Krzywiń [Kriewen][^420], who was at the same time steward of the Konarzewo estates, with the intention of marching with them to seize Poznań. The Prussian detachment at Leszno, however, learned of the whole affair and sent out Captain of Cavalry Kottuliński with 30 horse. Finding the peasants assembled on the occasion of his arrival, Kottuliński sought to win them over to the Prussian government. He therefore set before them into what misery they would fall by following the voice of the nobility, which under Polish rule had alone enjoyed freedom while oppressing the peasants; he

[132] <!-- p.132 -->

assured them that freedom could be given to the villagers only by the Prussian government. Having finished his speech, he distributed a certain sum of money among those assembled, so that they might drink the king's health with it. The effect was extraordinary. The peasants promised to follow his advice, and out of gratitude revealed to him the hiding place of that steward and mayor. Kottuliński seized him and sent him to the justice commission at Wschowa[^421].

For a long time, until as late as 7 December, the partisan Ksawery Łukomski harassed the Prussians. With a detachment that by some accounts numbered 5 or 8 men, by others 30, he ranged over the neighbourhood of Książ [Xions], Środa [Schroda] and Zaniemyśl. He was marked, moreover, by great recklessness. The Prussians also set a reward of 50 ducats on his head. On 7 December Łukomski was captured and handed over to the investigative commission in Poznań[^422].

The events described here, fragmentary as they are, give a picture of the last insurgent struggles in Greater Poland. After the fall of the uprising in the main theatre of the fighting, further efforts by the Greater Poles could not avail much; they were not able to save the national cause. It is true that when the Polish army was making its last march towards the Pilica, the Greater Poland insurgents still sustained themselves with the hope that the armed movement might revive once more, if the soldiers leaving Warsaw were to set foot on their soil[^423]. This hope was not groundless. Dąbrowski, as we know, long insisted on his plan of transferring the war to Greater Poland. But from the moment the Polish troops fell into disarray and the Greater Poles could expect rescue from nowhere, they finally had to regard the continuation of the struggle as pointless. So they returned slowly from all sides, nobles and peasants, to their homes, to resume their former occupations. The scythes, so recently turned into weapons,

[133] <!-- p.133 -->

changed back again into tools useful at harvest time[^424]. After the Radoszyce capitulation Stokowski, Lipski and Lenartowicz disbanded their detachments[^425]. Deprived now of guiding aims, the insurgents, insofar as they had not laid down their arms, acted on their own account. The Prussian offices in small towns without garrisons continued to be exposed to danger from them, and communications were uncertain. Yet even these most persistent men were unable to resist the victorious Prussians for long. The year had not yet run out, and the insurrection in Greater Poland already belonged to the past. The advancing Prussian troops met with no resistance from the inhabitants, and "only neighbourly jealousies, mutual accusations and anxious politeness towards the officers"[^426] recalled the times so lately past.

The activity of the Greater Poles during those few months of the insurrection in this region may be characterised as follows[^427]: those to whose credit the kindling of the revolt belongs were the nobility and the clergy; the Prussian government, too, regarded them as its greatest enemies. It was above all the petty nobility, who had little to lose, that took up arms willingly. Owners of larger fortunes, fearing damage to their property, often kept away from the uprising and frequently had to be forced into it. There were even some who, stripped of patriotic feeling, fled from the insurgent ranks to the protection of the Prussian government. The burghers, too, made common cause with the national movement, but only in small towns where the garrisons were weak or absent altogether; and always at the instigation of the nobility and clergy. The principal towns, such as Poznań, Łęczyca and Piotrków, where Germans formed a large percentage of the population, remained quiet, although cases of anti-Prussian agitation occurred there as well. Thus in Poznań the investigative

[134] <!-- p.134 -->

commission had to issue a proclamation[^428] refuting rumours of an alleged slaughter of Catholics by Protestants. The peasant, although not opposed to the insurrection, if only because he regarded the colonists sent by the Prussian government as heretics and despoilers of the land, nevertheless did not march in a mass into the insurgent ranks; only a few went there of their own free will. The Prussian government, for its part, from the very beginning of the insurrection let slip no opportunity to win over this very class of the population, the least nationally conscious. In its proclamations[^429] it warned the peasants not to let themselves be seduced by the nobility's "lying" promises of a reduction of labour dues; it compared their former position to bondage and assured them that only the Prussian king could confer benefits on them.

The strength at the insurrection's disposal was small. The creators of the voivodeship militias, despite a period of five months, did not know how to organise it properly, and later it proved impossible even to concentrate these risings of the individual lands, so that they could effectively resist the detachments of Prussian troops which, however small their numbers, advanced against them from Pomerania, from Silesia and from the vicinity of Warsaw. It happened that discord among the leaders of the risings, or a lack in them of the abilities needed to direct the movement, made unanimity of action impossible. For just this reason, at the agreed signal to unite all forces at Gniezno, only the insurgents from the Kalisz and Poznań regions appeared, under Niemojewski, that is about 1,300 men. When Dąbrowski halted at Słupca, 4,000 Greater Poles joined him. At first the commander had little satisfaction from them, for they lacked military training and a knowledge of the elementary rules of the art of war, but in time even this could be remedied. At Łabiszyn and Bydgoszcz the Greater Poles won for themselves

[135] <!-- p.135 -->

a glorious page of remembrance. Nor were the small detachments of the Greater Poland rising, which acted on their own account, idle. They cut direct communication with the centre of the Prussian lands, hindered transports and paralysed the civil authorities. It is chiefly to the credit of the Greater Poles that the Prussian king suspended the siege of the capital, for their armed movement was a successful diversion in the rear of the Prussian forces besieging Warsaw. By their participation in the insurrection the Greater Poles proved that they did not reconcile themselves to the new state of affairs, and that in defence of the fatherland they too were ready for sacrifices and self-denial. This circumstance, together with the incompetence of the Prussian generals, rendered a great service to Dąbrowski's military operations.

## Chapter V

### AFTER THE FALL OF THE INSURRECTION

[136] <!-- p.136 -->

With the fall of the insurrection the fate of the Polish nation came to depend entirely on the will of the triumphant partitioning powers. Nothing was any longer able to hold back the final partition. The insurrection, that armed reflex of a nation being quartered, which was meant to tear it from the hands of its butchers, in the end brought it political death.

The superior force ranged against it, however, was great. Hopes of help from other states proved vain. France did not grant the 12-million loan on the mortgage of the starostwa, which Potocki and Kołłątaj had expected from that quarter[^430]. All the efforts made in this matter by the Polish diplomatic agent to the revolutionary government, Franciszek Barss, were futile. The Committee of Public Safety objected that he had no official standing and that the rising in Poland "had been made by the nobility"[^431], and in the end it announced that "France will not let go the smallest particle of gold, nor expose the life of a single man for the sake of consolidating the revolution in Poland, if it is aiming at an aristocratic or royal government, or at a change of the reigning dynasty, or at a change of the form of government to another no less bad[^432]". This unfavourable stance of the French government of the time is significant,

[137] <!-- p.137 -->

since the insurrection in Poland was a diversion in France's favour, holding back part of the enemy forces from striking at the revolutionary armies[^433]. When in November the new Committee of Public Safety took an interest in foreign policy, the Polish cause again became topical. The old project of a Swedish-Danish-Polish-Turkish coalition was then brought forward; Parandier, appointed provisional agent to the provisional Polish government, was instructed to establish relations with the insurrectionary government based on trust and friendship and to declare that the French Republic would not refuse Poland help, so far as its own situation permitted, provided only that it had a guarantee that this help would serve the cause of liberty. Yet this help would have been belated and unnecessary. For it was not known that Kościuszko had already been a month in Russian captivity, and that the cause of the liberty of the Polish nation had been decided a few days earlier[^434].

The insurgents also counted on the service that Turkey was to render their cause. They hoped that, under French influence, it would declare war on Russia or Austria in order to make good the losses suffered two years before. These calculations too, however, were to fail completely. In Constantinople the former French envoy in Poland, Descorches, did indeed work in this direction, but his influence carried little weight. Forgotten by the terrorist government, he waited in vain for those 4 million francs so indispensable for setting the project of a Swedish-Danish-Polish-Turkish coalition on a real footing[^435].

Sweden, which was to join the coalition, although it regarded the insurgents with sympathy and had established diplomatic relations with the insurrectionary government, gave no help whatever[^436].

[138] <!-- p.138 -->

Efforts to secure the neutrality of Austria, which Kościuszko, as we know, considered the best of the three neighbouring states, likewise failed[^437].

The machinations concerning the final partition of Poland had begun as early as the middle of 1794. On 4 June the Austrian minister Thugut received a dispatch from Austria's representative in St Petersburg, Count Cobenzl, that Catherine regarded a new partition of Poland as unavoidable and promised to support Austria's aspirations in this respect[^438]. Thugut, who had dreamed of a third partition of Poland even earlier, succeeded in winning the emperor over completely to his plans of partition. Francis II therefore laid the duty of wrestling with the revolution on the shoulders of his generals and on 19 June returned to Vienna to watch, first and foremost, over his interest in Poland[^439].

In August the final partition negotiations began, to the immense joy of the Prussian king, who had for months been awaiting that moment with impatience[^440]. The new Prussian envoy in St Petersburg, Count Tauentzien, then demanded, in accordance with the instructions he had received, so extensive a share for Prussia that he provoked the decisive protest of his Austrian colleague Cobenzl, likewise supported by Russia. For he demanded not only the areas on the Narew and the Niemen and the lands west of the Pilica and the Vistula with Warsaw, but also the Kraków and Sandomierz voivodeships. In order to avoid bordering on Russia, he proposed carving out of Mazovia and Samogitia two separate duchies for Platon Zubov and Prince Nassau-Siegen. The Empress had no thought of agreeing to such far-reaching claims by Prussia. She assigned the Kraków and Sandomierz regions to Austria and Samogitia to herself. Prussia, however, did not give way, with the result that relations between it and Austria became so strained that they even threatened a breakdown of the

[139] <!-- p.139 -->

negotiations. Both states were by then ready rather to see in Poland the state of affairs of 1793 than to yield[^441].

Russia took a decided stand on the side of Austria. Between her and Austria a partition convention was concluded on 3 January 1795[^442]. It assigned to Russia an area of 120,000 sq. km with 1,200,000 inhabitants, namely: the remainder of the Vilnius, Nowogródek and Brest-Litovsk voivodeships, part of Volhynia and of Chełm Land, and almost the whole of Samogitia; to Austria, a territory amounting to 47,100 sq. km with a population of about 1½ million, that is, the remainder of the Kraków voivodeship (with the exception of a small strip which fell to Prussia), the Sandomierz, Lublin and Bełz voivodeships, part of Chełm Land, a considerable part of the Podlasie voivodeship, Liw Land from the Mazovia voivodeship, half of Warsaw Land and of Czersk Land, and a piece of the Volhynia voivodeship. The remaining lands of the Commonwealth were to fall to Prussia, that is, the remainder of the Rawa and Mazovia voivodeships with Warsaw (with the exception of those lands which fell to Austria), a fair part of the Troki voivodeship and a small piece of Samogitian land, and a small part of the Kraków voivodeship with the Duchy of Siewierz, together 48,000 sq. km with a population of almost 1 million[^443].

For some time Prussia did not consent to the area assigned to it. Above all it began negotiations with the Committee of Public Safety in order to conclude peace with France. Prince Henry of Prussia, the brother of Frederick the Great, pushed it onto this road. He saw in the war with France "the suicide of Prussia for the benefit of Austria[^444]", and he regarded peace as the more necessary the more he feared that the victorious French might later make the conclusion of peace with Prussia conditional on its renouncing the Polish provinces[^445]. And indeed peace was concluded at Basel on 5 April

[140] <!-- p.140 -->

1795, and Prussia gained a free hand to settle the matter of its acquisitions in Poland[^446]. Its resistance was, however, soon broken by the Empress. Ostermann declared to Count Tauentzien that the Polish question must be brought to an end, and that if Prussia intended to attack Austria, the Empress was determined to support the latter with all her strength, while the emperor would make peace with France and himself take the field against Prussia[^447]. The threat had its effect. On 3 September 1795 Tauentzien held the first joint conference with the representatives of Russia and Austria, Markov and Cobenzl, and on 24 October the partition treaty between Prussia and Russia, with the participation of Austria, was concluded, in which Prussia agreed to the provisions of the convention of 3 January 1795[^448].

The Prusso-Russian partition convention was supplemented by an additional one of 26 January 1797, accepted by Austria. A separate and secret article of this convention erased from memory for all time the name of the Polish Commonwealth (Rzeczpospolita Polska)[^449].

Of the lands which Prussia received in the last partition, the area on the left bank of the Vistula with Warsaw was incorporated into South Prussia. The area on the right bank of the Vistula and north of the Bug formed, together with the Płock voivodeship and the small piece of the Warsaw voivodeship which Prussia had gained in 1793, New East Prussia [Neuostpreußen]; and part of the new lands in the south, namely the Duchy of Siewierz and a piece of the Kraków voivodeship, formed New Silesia [Neuschlesien]. South Prussia was in time divided into the departments of Poznań, Kalisz and Warsaw; New East Prussia into those of Płock and Białystok[^450].

The loss of its own state was the consequence of the fall of the insurrection for the whole nation. What, apart from this, were the consequences of the collapse of the uprising, in particular for the Greater Poles, and what was the situation in their country?

[141] <!-- p.141 -->

In Greater Poland, alongside the restoration of the old order, trials of the insurgents now began. Their tracking down and punishment was undertaken by special commissions, which had been appointed while the insurrection was still in progress. For East Prussia [Translator's note: so printed; South Prussia is evidently meant: the commissions at Wrocław, Brzeg and Głogów, in Silesia, dealt with the insurgents of South Prussia (see p. 143).] they were installed in Wrocław, Brzeg and Głogów; for West Prussia, in Grudziądz and Toruń. The former were presided over by Minister Danckelmann, the latter by Minister v. Carmer. With the establishment of the West Prussian commissions, the commission set up in Toruń by Székely, under the chairmanship of the judicial councillor (Justizrat) Kühze, ceased to function. On 26 January 1795 an order was issued that there was to be no appeal from the verdicts of all five commissions, but that for a verdict to become valid royal confirmation was required[^451].

From 1 February 1795 Count Hoym[^452], who after the recall of Voss in September 1794 had become chief minister of South Prussia[^453], was appointed to supervise the commissions in Silesia. His intention was to bring the trial of the insurgents to an end as quickly as possible, and in accordance with the royal instruction[^454] fines and confiscation of property were to replace prison sentences. Hoym meant to devote the fund thus collected to the reforms in South Prussia that had been announced in part in the Wrocław patent of 24 September 1794, probably drafted by himself[^455]. For while on the one hand he had resolved to teach the Greater Poles obedience by punishing the rebels, so as to deter them in future from stirring up any disturbances, on the other hand he intended to introduce certain reforms, to remedy the lack of credit and cash, to put the mortgage registers in order and to lower the rate of interest, in order to inspire confidence in the new ruler among the inhabitants. Observing social relations in his province, he had come to the conviction that since its seizure by the Prussians

[142] <!-- p.142 -->

the magnate had lost his influential position and had become a mere landholder, that as a consequence the petty nobility, whose voice had formerly been needed by the lords at the sejmiks, had lost its means of livelihood, that the burgher was oppressed by the obligation to quarter Prussian troops, and that the material position of the peasants had deteriorated considerably because of the requisitions by the insurgents and by the Prussian army, which out of hatred for the insurgents had utterly ruined some estates. He held that the Prussian soldier in Greater Poland should change his hostile attitude towards the population, cease abuses and strive to win its affection[^456].

According to a plan adopted in advance, the whole enormous trial of the insurgents, embracing more than 2,000 accused[^457], was to be completed by 1 June 1795. After that date the cases of the accused not yet judged were to be taken up by a special commission. All the others were to be dissolved. Minister Hoym made the establishment of this commission known in an announcement of 24 April 1795[^458]. Poznań was designated as its seat, and it was subordinated to the former Prussian envoy in Warsaw, Buchholtz, who since September 1794 had held the dignity of Oberpräsident of South Prussia. The members of the commission were: the president of the Płock Chamber, Privy Councillor v. Harlem; the president of the Piotrków Regierung, Count Reuss; and the director of the Piotrków Chamber, Privy Councillor v. Zitzewitz; the assistant was Lt. v. Klebowsky[^459].

Patents issued from time to time called upon the insurgents to appear voluntarily before the commissions, promising those who proved obedient to the summons a remission or reduction of punishment. The patent for the inhabitants of West Prussia, issued while the insurrection was still in progress, on 23 October

[143] <!-- p.143 -->

1794, went furthest in this respect. It promised all participants in the uprising release "from every and any penalty, whether of life or of body, or of confiscation of estates and other penalties on estates", as well as complete amnesty, "every pardon and remission", if within a period of two weeks from the date of publication of the patent they laid down their arms and returned to their homes; those who would not comply with the appeal were threatened by the government with severe penalties[^460]. The Landrat of the Bydgoszcz district, Twardowski, who had been an active insurgent at the time of Dąbrowski's expedition, placed his trust in this appeal, but was nevertheless arrested on 25 November 1794[^461]. For in the meantime the king had issued to the West Prussian commissions an order to mete out punishments to the "rebels" with complete ruthlessness[^462]. On 10 December 1794 a proclamation appeared concerning the treatment of the insurgents of South Prussia. It ordered the release of Polish prisoners. Former officers were to give a written undertaking that they would not enter service in any army that might take the field against Prussia. The common soldiers were to be incorporated in part into the Prussian army, or else sent out into the province as settlers. The nobility and clergy who had taken part in the uprising were threatened by the appeal with the loss of their estates and dignities if they did not present themselves before the appropriate authorities by 1 January 1795; the principal "rebels" and leaders of the insurrection it ordered to be arrested and delivered for interrogation before the commissions in Wrocław, Głogów or Brzeg[^463]. A publicandum (public notice) of 10 February 1795 extended the term for the appearance of the insurgents from South Prussia to 1 June 1795, threatening otherwise punishments as before. Finally, the patent issued in Wrocław on 24 April 1795, concerning the treatment of the South Prussian

[144] <!-- p.144 -->

insurgents, announced the execution of severe penalties only on a small number of the principal culprits; it sentenced those starostas who had taken part in the uprising to the loss of their starostwa, and the remainder of the accused to fines, with the intention of turning these to the benefit of the country[^464].

Few former insurgents, however, presented themselves voluntarily to the authorities[^465]. The most guilty, those accused of *crimen perduellionis* (treason against the state), did not return.

In vain did the edictal citation (summons) of the Royal Regierung of South Prussia of 12 September 1795 call for the return of Józef Wybicki, of Generals Lipski and Niemojewski, and of the Rev. Canon Prolewicz of Nietrzanowo near Środa. Of all these, however, Niemojewski was arrested and confined in Magdeburg. His companions in misfortune there were Madaliński[^466], Jerzy Grabowski, Giełgud and others[^467].

The commissions conducted the investigation of the former insurgents with great thoroughness. Officials in West Prussia and South Prussia submitted lists of persons compromised in the events just past, specifying who had gone into the insurgent ranks of his own will and who under compulsion[^468]. Participants in the insurrection were punished with penalties on their liberty, such as detention in a fortress or imprisonment; estates were sequestrated; noble as well as church and monastic estates were confiscated[^469]; non-castle starostwa were taken away; finally, the "rebels" were removed from the offices they sometimes held, and sentenced to pay sums of money that were at times very high. No insurgent was sentenced to death, although the royal instruction to the West Prussian commissions recommended its execution in the case of very aggravating circumstances[^470].

[145] <!-- p.145 -->

How many Greater Poles underwent fortress imprisonment is hard to establish. It is known, however, that at the beginning of 1795 the Prussians were holding in Toruń 42 citizens from West Prussia and 60 from South Prussia; in Grudziądz, 48 from West Prussia and 25 from South Prussia[^471]. As to the names of these prisoners, we can give barely a few. Thus in the fortress of Grudziądz were confined, for a period of 2 years, Piotr Trzebuchowski, captain of cavalry in the Kuyavian uprising[^472], and the monks of the Benedictine monastery at Mogilno, Kajetan Smoliński and Izydor Domagalski, for 3 and 4 years, because they could not pay the fine imposed on them, in the amount of 6,000–8,000 thalers[^473]. In Toruń, Jan Żmichowski of Rawicz, private secretary of the starosta Mycielski, spent nearly half a year in detention[^474]. There are no closer data which would allow us to establish how many, and which, of the Greater Poles were held in the Silesian fortresses. We know only that in Głogów a citizen of Gniezno, Kłosowski, was kept in detention for 6 weeks[^475]. Those released had to swear the oath of allegiance anew[^476].

In South Prussia the Prussian government placed under sequestration the estates of the following Greater Poles: Wojciech Węgorzewski of Objezierze[^477], Antoni Chłapowski of Dąbrówka[^478], Celestyn Sokolnicki of Jarogniewice[^479], Mateusz Rosen of Kruchów[^480], Biskupski of Kuchary[^481], Jan Zembrzuski, owner of Great and Little Siekierki[^482], Antoni Grudziński, lord of Popowo, Kukulin, Rybkowo, Łagiewniki, Jagniewice, Sarbia and Podlesie[^483], Ksawery Łukomski, lord of Drzazgowo, Wysławice and Sokolniczki[^484], Florian Borzęcki, leaseholder of Porażyn[^485], the starosta Leszczyński of Grabów[^486], who died of wounds received in the uprising, the Landrat Józef

[146] <!-- p.146 -->

Koszutski, Józef Lipski, Władysław Gurowski, Józef Niemojewski, starosta of Śrem, whose estate was finally confiscated as that of one of the principal leaders of the insurrection[^487], Emanuel Polster, Józef Roseroni, Piotr Dapfontaine and Neopold, former bookkeeper of the Poznań banker Klug[^488]. We do not know which of the Greater Poles resident in West Prussia had their estates sequestrated.

Nor can we say which of the numerous estates that the Prussian government generously distributed to Germans over the next few years came from confiscation, or had been taken from starostas as punishment for their part in the uprising, or who their previous owners had been. We know only that of the estates lying in the most fertile area of the former Poznań voivodeship, namely in the districts of Śrem, Kościan, Krobia [Kröben] and Wschowa, which were received by the former royal adjutant, Major-General Zastrow, a part had been confiscated and had previously belonged to Józef Wybicki[^489].

The former Greater Poland insurgents whom the Prussians removed from office included Twardowski, Landrat of the Bydgoszcz district, and Józef Koszutski, Landrat of the Gniezno district. In place of those removed, veteran Prussian officers were appointed[^490].

Most often the "rebels" were assessed fines, which also frequently took the place of imprisonment. A sum of 2–6 thousand thalers was equivalent to a penalty of one year in a fortress, depending on the insurgent's financial standing[^491]. From the former insurgents of South Prussia the Prussians expected to collect 300,000 thalers. King Frederick William II assigned one third of this sum to his discretionary fund (Dispositionskasse), one third to the building of a fortress at Łęczyca,

[147] <!-- p.147 -->

winter uniforms for the soldiers, compensation and the costs of the insurgents' trials, and the remainder to the purposes of reform in South Prussia. Despite the strenuous efforts of Minister Hoym, the collection of the fines proceeded very sluggishly; in addition, some had to be partly remitted and a few even entirely. By 1 June 1798 a total of 1,707,168 Polish zloty, that is 284,528 thalers, had been collected from the whole of South Prussia[^492]. In all, 1,195,506 Polish zloty, that is 199,251 thalers, flowed into the Prussian treasuries in the Poznań department. As appears from numerous receipts found in the files of the State Archive in Poznań[^493], those sentenced paid the fine imposed on them in instalments. The largest sums, that is, those amounting to 2,000 thalers and more, were paid by: Bieńkowski Sebastian — 5,000 thalers, the heirs of Bolesza — 8,000 thalers, Gliszczyński Antoni — 7,000 t (remitted — 1,000 t); Gurowski Władysław — 6,000 t; Gorzeński Nicefor — 4,000 t (remitted — 6,000 t); Jaraczewski Hieronim — 3,000 t; Koszutski Józef — 4,500 t (remitted — 1,500 t), Gen. Korytowski — 3,000 t (remitted — 9,000 t); Lipski Józef — 7,000 t (remitted — 1,000 t): Radoliński Piotr — 5,000 t; Rogaliński Hipolit — 6,000 t; Rosen Mateusz — 2,000 t; Sczaniecki Józef — 6,000 t; Sczaniecki Łukasz — 3,000 t; Sokolnicki Nepomucen — 2,000 t; Sokolnicki Piotr — 4,000 t; Swinarski Wincenty — 5,000 t; Taroni Jan — 2,500 t; Węgorzewski — 2,000 t; Wyganowski Antoni — 4,000 t (remitted — 14,000 t); Zakrzewski Hiac. — 4,000 t (remitted — 2,000 t)[^494].

When in 1807 the lands of the Second and Third Partitions (with the exception of the Białystok district, which Russia received) formed the Duchy of Warsaw, the citizens of South Prussia who had been wronged by the Prussians were ordered to submit their claims to the new government. In the Poznań department this matter was handled by

[148] <!-- p.148 -->

Rożnowski, councillor of the Administrative Chamber of that department[^495].

We do not know how large a sum the insurgents of West Prussia had to pay by way of penalty. In any case it must have been small, since it did not suffice even for repairing the damage caused in that area by the insurrection. For that reason the authorities there made representations to the king that he should assign to West Prussia at least a part of the sum collected in South Prussia. The representations, however, foundered on the resistance of Minister Hoym. By virtue of a royal rescript of 17 October 1796 the injured parties of West Prussia could only claim compensation for damages from those who had caused them. In this way the matter was referred to private proceedings. Since, however, the guilt of the defendants often could not be proved, and many of them were abroad or were altogether poor, even by this route some compensation could be obtained only in a few cases[^496]. The small sum that came in from the insurgents in West Prussia is explained, in general, by the fact that the area affected by the revolt was small and was confined chiefly to certain parts of the Netze District. Of 138 accused, only 63 inhabitants were found guilty. Of these, 26 came from West Prussia proper, the rest from the Netze District. What fine was imposed on these 63 is not known, since no closer data are available[^497].

The punishment of the insurgents, which was intended to deter the Greater Poles from starting revolts in the future and to teach them obedience, evidently did not produce that effect, since the Prussian king in the patent of 9 August 1796 warned against a new uprising. He threatened the owners of estates with responsibility for subjects

[149] <!-- p.149 -->

who rebelled, and ordered the peasants to report to the nearest authority the revolutionary intentions of their lords. The numerous landless nobility, residing with the more powerful landowners or loitering about the towns, was regarded as an element especially susceptible to revolution; the Prussian government accordingly extended watchful control over it. Within six weeks of the date of publication of the said patent, every individual of this kind was obliged to report to the district authority, present a certificate of origin, name his settled acquaintances, friends and relatives, and declare whether he intended to remain in the country or to go abroad. The emigration of this nobility was highly desirable to the government, which therefore announced that "permission for it will be neither refused nor made difficult"[^498]; on return, however, a passport and various credentials were demanded, and every effort was made to make it as difficult as possible[^499].

The Prussian government likewise paid close attention to the clergy, for the clergy had "in the last unfortunate insurrection justly drawn upon itself the suspicion" of being guilty of "genuine revolts, of personal partiality and active support for harmful conspiracies, and even of secret associations and agreements aimed at the overthrow of the government of the country, and of other culpable undertakings from which every feeling of respect for law and moral order recoils[^500]." The patent of 9 August 1796 contained four points concerning the clergy: 1) bishops, officials (diocesan judges), deans and heads of monasteries are liable, for the conspiracies of those subordinate to them, to loss of office and sequestration of revenues; 2) for concealing rebellious intentions, priests will incur expulsion from the country, and for active participation in a conspiracy, the penalty for lèse-majesté and treason; 3) in the event of the complicity of several members of a chapter, monastery or other ecclesiastical community, besides the punishment of the guilty,

[150] <!-- p.150 -->

the cathedral or convent will be abolished, their estates confiscated, and their buildings converted to public purposes; 4) the same penalty threatens the concealment, within ecclesiastical buildings or outside them but with the knowledge of the clergy, of gunpowder, lead or firearms. Appeal to the duty of not revealing confidences entrusted to them did not release priests from responsibility. A conspirator who informed could expect a mitigation of his penalty, and one who took no part in it, a reward for denunciation. The patent assured informers of discretion and threatened with punishment those who knowingly made false accusations[^501].

Simultaneously with the punishment of the former insurgents the Prussian government set about improving anew the whole administrative and judicial apparatus introduced in Greater Poland before the uprising, introduced further innovations, and spared no effort to turn the seized lands into a Prussian province.

On 5 January 1795 the South Prussian courts resumed their interrupted activity[^502]. In the judiciary the German language came to reign supreme, and Prussian procedure was introduced. This caused great discontent among Polish citizens, and the bolder among them even addressed the king on the matter. As a result of their efforts a publicandum appeared, dated 16 November 1795, intended to adapt judicial proceedings "as far as possible to the character and way of thinking of the nation". German was not, however, removed from the judiciary; it was merely promised that extracts from the general Prussian law (das allgemeine Landrecht, the General Land Law for the Prussian States) and the procedure would be published in Polish. The Regierungen were instructed to translate judgments and summonses into Polish, in whole or in abridged form. In 1797 Polish laws lost their binding force.

[151] <!-- p.151 -->

For Prussian legislation, the so-called Landrecht, was introduced[^503].

The Greater Poles were removed from participation in public affairs; the Prussian government extended over them the tutelage of the German bureaucracy. Special regulations concerning the clergy, the nobility, the peasants, the burghers and the Jews governed the manner of ruling the population by means of the administrative organs[^504]. The clergy was placed wholly under the authority of the state. Through its commissioners the Chamber directed the elections of bishops, prelates and the superiors of religious congregations; from priests it received the oath of allegiance as subjects. In civil and criminal matters the clergy were subject to the jurisdiction of the Regierung. Only matters of a purely ecclesiastical nature belonged to the competence of the ecclesiastical courts. In 1796 the ecclesiastical estates were seized for the benefit of the treasury. For the maintenance of the clergy the treasury set aside a part of the revenues from the church properties it had taken and the interest on the capital belonging to ecclesiastical corporations and lent out privately; it allowed the clergy to collect fees for religious services according to an official tariff[^506].

The nobility retained the privilege of owning landed estates, but with certain restrictions. Those among the nobility who had held no landed property at the time of the fall of the Commonwealth could now acquire it only after obtaining the incolat (indigenat, the right of a native noble to hold land). If anyone who had neither any right to landed property nor the incolat wished to acquire an estate, he had first to secure a special permission from the Prussian king. Inheritance, bequest by will and gift did not confer the right to hold landed estates without the incolat or royal permission. A contract of purchase or sale of noble estates was not legally valid without the approval of

[152] <!-- p.152 -->

the Regierung, which had first to obtain information about the new purchaser from the police organs. The owner of an estate had supreme authority over the peasant, but did not exercise it directly. For the administration of justice he had to maintain a justiciary (Justitiar), and he exercised police authority by means of the village head (sołtys; German Schulze); he appointed these officials after consultation with the Landrat. The justiciary and the village head sowed discord between the lord and his subjects[^507].

The royal towns lost the right to elect their officials. In the private towns in the territory of South Prussia the government did, it is true, assure the hereditary owners and the commoners, by a patent of 18 April 1795, the right to nominate or elect the town councils, but it reserved to itself the appointment of the mayor. It controlled the municipal revenues, devised ways of repaying debts, and so on[^508].

The Prussian government paid close attention to the Jews. In the annexed lands only those Jews could live who had settled there in the time of the Commonwealth; those who arrived later had to leave the country. A Jew entitled to reside in the country possessed a so-called letter of protection (Schutzbrief), which meant the same as a passport. The district offices imposed German surnames on the Jews, often ridiculous ones. Without the authorisation of the Chamber, Jews were forbidden to change their place of residence and their occupation[^509].

In organising social relations the Prussian government was guided not by the good of the inhabitants but by the aims of the state. It made officials of the priests. The nobility it now favoured, now extended police control over. In order to curry favour with the nobility, it undertook no improvement of peasant conditions and did not abolish labour dues, despite announcements to that effect[^510]. The restriction of the self-government

[153] <!-- p.153 -->

of the towns and the manner of treating the Jews likewise served the aims of the state[^511].

In order to safeguard the Greater Poles against undesirable influences, a severe censorship was introduced. The author was answerable for the content of a book even after it had been censored. Publication of a work without censorship entailed a fine[^512].

Simultaneously with issuing new regulations, the Prussian government sought to extract from Greater Poland the greatest possible material benefits. It paid particular attention to agriculture and forestry, since the main part of its revenues flowed from them[^513]. It forbade the division of the so-called knightly estates (Rittergüter) of no more than 10 włóki (the włóka being c. 17 ha), which, as a result of the incessant partitioning among the members of expanding families, were falling more and more into decay[^514]. In addition to the ecclesiastical estates, in 1796 it also seized for the treasury the non-castle starostwa[^515]. Both kinds formed the so-called domains, whose administration was directed by the Chambers. The government raised taxes, increasing the ofiara to 24 groszy, and the hearth tax by one half. To improve communications it cleared the Warta and made it navigable[^516].

The Prussian government likewise spared neither effort nor expense in order to entrench the German element in Greater Poland. For this purpose it brought in a multitude of officials, sent in colonists and peasants for public works, and leased the domains only to Germans. In South Prussia alone, within 4 years under the administration of Minister Hoym, 241 estates were distributed among 52 grantees. The starostwo of Międzyrzecz, with a number of picturesquely situated manor farms, was received, as a token of the royal "satisfaction" for services rendered to Prussia over many years, by Lucchesini[^517]. The Prussian government also took up a plan to dispossess the Greater Poland nobility

[154] <!-- p.154 -->

in favour of the German element. It was assisted in this by the thoughtlessness of the landowners, such as living beyond their means, readily availing themselves of easy credit, and squandering[^518].

Schooling also served the aims of Germanisation. In the elementary schools a German primer was introduced. The German language was the main subject of instruction in the newly founded cadet school in Kalisz. In order to force the youth of Greater Poland to enter German universities, the government founded no institutions of higher learning on Polish lands. Only in 1804 did it establish the Warsaw Lyceum[^519].

The efforts at Germanisation, undertaken already at the beginning of Prussian rule and continued later with ever greater ruthlessness, nevertheless did not succeed in killing the Polish spirit in the nation. The idea of independence was always alive. The Greater Poles longed for freedom, like the whole Polish nation. Polish blood was shed in cruelly suppressed uprisings. The Greater Poles watered the fields of their land abundantly with it in 1848, until at last, after more than a century of bondage, the sun of freedom shone for the oppressed nation. In vain, 20 years later, did the rampaging Hitlerite beast strive, during more than 5 years of occupation of the Polish lands, amid murders and the bloodiest persecutions known to history, to erase the name of Poland for ever; in vain was Greater Poland, Germanised at lightning speed, to be turned into a province German from time immemorial. From the martyr blood of the best sons of the Fatherland Poland rose up free, to prove to the world the truth of Staszic's words that "even a great nation may fall; only a worthless one can perish."

[^373]: Skałkowski, Mon. IV, 150, 151, 372; cf. Treskow, 323—325. Wilhelm, Fryderyk, Karol, Count Schwerin, was born on 11 December 1739 in Berlin. In 1753 he entered the Prussian army, serving in the regiment of his paternal uncle. At the outbreak of the Seven Years' War in 1756 he was adjutant to General Winterfeld, and after the latter's death (1757), adjutant to the king. In 1758 he was wounded at Zorndorf and taken prisoner by the Russians. There he managed to win over the Grand Duke Peter, and in 1760, exchanged for a Russian prisoner, he was sent back to Prussia. After the conclusion of peace at Hubertusburg in 1763 he returned to the army, later became a lieutenant-general, commander of an infantry regiment for the district of West Prussia, and governor of Toruń (Allgemeine deutsche Biographie (Leipzig 1891, vol. 33, p. 427)). Of the supreme command of the Prussian forces on the left bank of the Vistula, entrusted to him in mid-September 1794, he acquitted himself as badly as possible. According to the opinion of Prussian military men (Treskow, 282 ff.; Boyen, 79 ff.; Knoll, 156 ff.), Schwerin ought, immediately after taking over the command, to have concentrated his forces and attacked the opponent standing between him and Warsaw. The Polish troops equalled the Prussian neither in numbers nor in quality. During the attack mentioned above it was necessary for Generals Lattorff and Lichnowski to occupy Kraków and Sandomierz and to guard the regions of Piotrków and Łowicz against intrusion by the Greater Poland insurgents, while the corps of Elsner, of Schwerin the Younger and of Székely, joined together, were to strike at Dąbrowski's division. Later still, after the defeat at Maciejowice, when Suvorov was approaching Warsaw, Schwerin could have attacked the Polish troops stationed before his front and beaten them separately. Even if he had merely succeeded in pushing the opponent back towards Warsaw, he would already have gained freedom of action, and then he could have turned with the greater part of his army against Dąbrowski. But Schwerin remained indecisive even then, frittering away his forces from day to day, detaching more and more units against the insurgents; in the end he set out with 5 battalions to destroy the returning Greater Poland column, but as a result of false combinations he chose the wrong road. He allowed Dąbrowski's troops, together with a considerable baggage train, to cross the Bzura in peace, and afterwards took no step to support the Russians at Warsaw. He lacked, as indeed throughout the whole campaign, a clear judgment of events and decisiveness. He had set against him a very able opponent in the person of Dąbrowski, and by that measure he assessed the rest of the Polish army and its commanders (Skałkowski, Mon. IV, 151). For all the oversights and errors committed in his responsible post he answered with the loss of his command. The king took it from him under the pretext of weakened health and sent him back to his former governorship at Toruń. Schwerin, being certain that an injustice had befallen him, asked for an inquiry to be opened. His demand was granted. The inquiry was conducted by Lieutenant-General Brüneck at Königsberg. On 9 May 1795 a court martial assembled in Berlin under the presidency of General Rohdich and pronounced a sentence by virtue of which Schwerin was condemned to lose his regiment, to resign the governorship of Toruń, to one year's detention in a fortress, and to repay the costs of the investigations. He served the sentence at Grudziądz. After the death of Frederick William II he made efforts to return to the Prussian army, but the new king, Frederick William III, opposed this; he was permitted only to enter foreign service. On 17 August 1802, during a journey to Hamburg, Schwerin died. In his own justification he wrote "Wahre u. mit Aktenstücke belegte Darstellung der Veranlassung auf welche ich nach 43 Dienstjahren aus dem Königlichen Preuss. Militärdienste entlassen worden bin" (Leipzig 1798). The title of the second edition (Leipzig 1799) read: "Muster für Stabsoffiziere Rapporte zu machen". In the above treatise Schwerin accused Lieutenant-General Klinkowström as the one who in October 1794 had allowed Dąbrowski's division to slip away across the Bzura (Allgemeine Biographie. vol. 33, 428). Klinkowström rejected the charges made against him by Schwerin in the work: "Berichtigungen einiger Angaben, welche in dem vom General-Lieutenant Grafen v. Schwerin herausgegebenen Buche... enthalten sind" (Berlin 1799). The blame that he attributed to Schwerin during the campaign of 1794 he set out in points: 1) the unnecessary drawing in of General Lattorff's corps from the Sandomierz region and the consequent loss of that province; 2) the pointless formation of the corps of Pollitz and Ledivary and their unsuitable use for holding back the opponent in West Prussia; 3) remaining idle in the camp at Chrzonowice, on the strength of false assumptions, until 20 October 1794; 4) the undertaking on 22 October of a march from Łowicz to Kiernozia and Osmalin that was pointless in the highest degree, as a result of which the enemy was able to cross the Bzura with impunity; 5) the failure to undertake, jointly with the Russian troops, an offensive against Warsaw, contrary to the royal orders and to the very nature of things, and instead remaining idle at Łowicz from 26 October until 10 November (according to Klinkowström, Berichtigungen.... 11, 12).
