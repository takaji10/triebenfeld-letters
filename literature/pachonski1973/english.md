THE POLES IN THE REPUBLIC OF VENICE

(1794-1797)

The beginnings of the Risorgimento coincide with a period of Polish struggles for independence and social progress. One little-known stage of Polish-Venetian relations, which preceded the formation of General J. H. Dąbrowski's Polish Legions in Italy, deserves in particular a careful examination[^1].

But the Poles did not think of surrendering: on the territory dismembered into three parts a clandestine network began to work, directed by the Lwów Centre. Some of the leaders of the Insurrection managed to get abroad, from where they meant to direct the struggle to come.

Venice – centre of action

At first it was rather difficult to find a seat from which the struggle would be directed. Saxony, close at hand, refused asylum under pressure from Prussia. The fighting on German territory made contact between Paris or Frankfurt on Main and occupied Poland difficult. In Hamburg the influence of the Russians and of the Targowica[^3] prevailed. The French agent for Polish affairs, Pierre Parandier, proposed Altona, in the duchy of Holstein, as the centre.

The main current of the emigration went south, however, towards Vienna and Prague, but the many vexations the emigrés suffered opened their eyes to Austria's want of goodwill. Their eyes then turned to the Republic of Venice, which lay between Paris, Vienna and Constantinople, and whose trade gave it the freshest news and easy communications. Its neutrality made it, besides, a meeting ground between the camp of the coalition and that of the revolution, and gave the Polish emigrés a certain freedom of movement.

The Republic of Venice comprised, besides territories in the north of Italy, possessions on the Adriatic coast, the whole inhabited by three million people, 130,000 of them in the capital. The Republic was going through an economic and commercial crisis, together with the decay of its army and fleet[^4].

Salvation could come only from radical reforms, to which the ruling oligarchy, selfish and idle, would not consent. To keep Venice from invasion this oligarchy relied on the Republic's former glory and on its neutrality, which had just been proclaimed. The tragic fate of Poland taught it nothing. It feared only a revolutionary upheaval, and above all kept a vigilant watch on foreigners.

The make-up of the Venetian "Polonia"

As early as the summer of 1794 the Prince and Princess Charles and Charlotte of Nassau-Siegen (born Godzka)[^5] came from Poland to the lagoons. Deprived of his inheritance by the Prince of Orange, Prince Charles had sought protectors among the monarchs for military and diplomatic services (he was, among other things, a Russian admiral), and had travelled in Africa and Australia. A rich marriage made in Poland (1780) had brought him connections and the privileges attached to the titles of Polish citizen and deputy.

After the proclamation of the Kościuszko Insurrection he declared his solidarity with Poland in arms by giving up his Russian dignities; but as a monarchist he would not join the insurgents, who were growing more and more radical, and he emigrated with his wife to Venice. In the palace they rented on the Rio Foscari (near the Grand Canal) they set up a royalist centre: the prince and princess were partisans of Stanisław August and gave money to the Bourbons. Soon they were helped in this by the King's nephew, Lieutenant-General Stanislas Mokronowski, an active fighter of the Insurrection.

From October 1794 the first emigrés arrived from the territories where the fighting of the insurrection had taken place. According to the reports of the Venetian police, "the Polish adherents of French maxims" met at D. Petrillo's inn, beside the Rialto Bridge and the Malibran theatre[^6].

Towards the end of 1794 there were some fifty Polish emigrés in Venice, among them six members of the insurrectionary government[^7], a senator, seven deputies to the Diet[^8], three diplomats[^9], three starosts[^10], a few landowners and, among the military, eight generals[^11], several staff officers and others of lower rank.

In the emigration it was not only former offices and merits that counted: one also needed a certain fortune, connections, foreign languages and enterprise. Pierre Potocki, Michel Ogiński, Charles Prozor, Denise Mniewski and Stanislas Sołtyk excelled in this.

Three main parties formed quite quickly: the partisans of a strong monarchy, the adherents of constitutional monarchy, and the republicans. The first were represented by the Prince and Princess of Nassau-Siegen and General Mokronowski. At the head of the second party were diplomats, S. Sołtyk, P. Potocki and M. Ogiński, commonly called the moderates. In their view the Constitution of 3 May had the nation's favour and, abroad, would reduce the number of the opponents of Poland's restoration. This party was the strongest in Venice, without being united, for its progressive wing, with Charles Prozor and Joseph Kociełł at its head, looked to deep changes in the Constitution once independence had been recovered, and fought Stanisław August.

François Dmochowski, General François Łaźniński and Major Clement Liberadzki[^12], on the other hand, were won over by the French revolutionary régime.

Making contact with the minister Lallement

In the second half of December 1794 the closer watch of the Venetian police alarmed the Poles[^13]. Fearing complications, they sent a delegation to the French Embassy made up of S. Sołtyk, M. Ogiński and F. Dmochowski[^14]. The French ambassador at Venice at the time was Lallement, a man of about sixty[^15]. He knew the situation in Italy very well, having spent some thirty years in diplomatic posts at Ragusa, Messina and Naples.

The minister Lallement and his son, secretary of the Legation, received the Polish delegation very kindly, promised their help, and put the rooms of the Embassy at the Poles' disposal for their meetings. The minister assured them that France held to the restoration of Poland, whatever its future régime, and that the emigrés could count on its full support. This stand was bound to be disavowed rather quickly by the French government. But for the moment, encouraged by this declaration, the Polish emigrés addressed a memorandum to the French government (31 December), in which they stressed the ideological kinship of the French and the Polish Revolutions, and the unequal effort of a struggle waged for eight months against some two hundred thousand invaders, which had drawn off substantial Austrian and Prussian forces fighting revolutionary France on the Rhine. The Poles now asked for a return for this service. They explained that it was in France's interest to defend the cause of Poland, Turkey, Sweden and Denmark, or else the riches of those countries might serve to crush the French Revolution; a liberated Poland, on the other hand, would be its devoted ally in the east of Europe.

The memorandum put forward the idea of a great coalition of progressive states and of forming a "restricted" Diet abroad, and declared that if the French army attacked from the Rhine towards the Vistula, Galicia would take up arms and go to meet it. It also proposed raising a Legion of four thousand Poles who had been forced to serve in the Austrian army, and it was hoped that this Legion

The Polish Committee at Venice

In January 1795 the Poles, trusting in the minister Lallement's promise and encouraged by the news of French successes in Holland, decided to form a provisional government under the name of the Polish Committee at Venice. M. Ogiński, S. Sołtyk, K. Prozor and, of the republicans, F. Dmochowski entered it; the right was reserved to co-opt representatives of the Altona centre, should that be wanted.

The appeal to patriotic generosity secured considerable funds. Straight afterwards Ogiński and Dmochowski were sent to the French agent Cacault at Florence, Major Thomas Maruszewski to Basel, to the minister Barthélemy[^18], who had great influence there, and Michel Skrzetuski to Constantinople, to watch over the Polish-Turkish alliance and the forming of Polish armed forces.

For the Paris mission, the most important, Charles Prozor was chosen; he left for Paris by way of Florence on 7 February 1795. He was to take over the duties of François Barss, the representative of the Insurrection, tolerated by the French government but blamed for his want of energy. Prozor thus became the Polish spokesman to the French government.

In Paris Prozor met many Poles ready to recognise the authority of the Venice Committee[^19], but he soon came himself to the conclusion that the Polish government in emigration should have its seat not in distant Venice but near the revolutionary government, in Paris itself.

The importance of the Peace of Basel

The treaty of Basel concluded between France and Prussia (5 April 1795) was an unpleasant surprise for the Poles and a proof of France's ingratitude. The French assured them that the treaty was precarious, and urged the Poles to turn to Prussia in their efforts for independence. This view brought together Barss, Prozor and Joseph Wybicki, who were inclined to support it[^21]. The last two, on 15 April, addressed a manifesto to their compatriots in Venice, recommending patience and the steps to be taken to make the Diet work abroad, and advocating a Franco-Prusso-Turco-Swedo-Danish coalition.

Changes in the balance of forces within the Venetian "Polonia"

Meanwhile the situation in Venice had changed appreciably. The royalist centre suspended its activity, because the Prince of Nassau-Siegen had to leave for Spain and Austria in search of funds, which he found unexpectedly through settlements and inheritances. At the same time General Mokronowski went to settle in Florence.

From May 1795 the palace of S. Luca ceased to be the main centre of activity of the Poles of Venice, after the disappointments that the minister Lallement had caused the "hotheads"; he had moved into the Palazzo Rosso, larger still than the one before.

The Poles now met at Joachim Durante's inn in the Calle del Forno, near St Mark's Square and the La Fenice theatre. Current news was gathered from those who arrived from the occupied country, from those who travelled for pleasure or on business, and also from the emigrés, who kept on coming because they could no longer live under the occupier's terror.

So although many Poles left Venice for Paris or scattered to other Italian cities, their number in Venice itself only grew. Among the eighty entered in the registers are the names of Thomas Wyssogierd, a member of the insurrectionary government of Lithuania; Onufry Morski, castellan of Kamieniec; five Potockis, among them Georges, a former ambassador to Sweden; and among the generals, General Xavier Jezierski, seven colonels and lieutenant-colonels, etc. But Pierre Parandier deplored the discord that reigned among them.

In April 1795 the republicans broke with the others, sending F. Dmochowski to Paris to strengthen the Paris left. Dmochowski had just finished his "Mémoires pour servir à l'histoires des révolutions de Pologne, particulièrement à celle de 1794", in which he condemned not only the invaders and the King but above all the Poles' want of faith in themselves.

The receipt of 2,000 ducats, deposited after the failure of the rising in Galicia, gave the republicans more freedom of action; the sum had been sent to be at General Łaźniński's disposal, and he used it to strengthen the republican party.

S. Sołtyk, discouraged by the situation, decided to move nearer to Poland, seeking to save his family, who were interned at Głogów. He left Venice on 12 June 1795[^23], but fell ill at Dresden. It was then that the Lwów Centre entrusted him (in July) with the responsible office of plenipotentiary delegate for Italy, with his seat at Venice, and assured him the subsidy his work for independence required.

Under the sign of the Deputation

Thanks to easier contact with Poland, several important emigrés came to Paris within a fairly short time (many of them from Venice). On 22 August 1795 they formed a government in emigration under the name of the Deputation, on the model of the French Directory. The emigrés from Venice held the majority in it (3 of 5)[^24]. Although the idea of forming this Deputation came from them, since it was meant to replace the Venice Committee, which the French government had not recognised, they did not manage to bring about unanimity. Indeed, the majority of the emigrés from Venice was at once replaced by a republican majority. Mniewski and Dmochowski allied themselves with General Gabriel Taszycki of Little Poland and General Romuald Giedroyć of Lithuania, while Prozor, outvoted, had to withdraw from the Deputation (18 November 1795), and with F. Barss and J. Wybicki formed the opposition group of the Polish Emigrés, supporters of the Constitution of 3 May. This made it difficult to use the "Palace of the Emigration", which had been mortgaged in Prozor's name. For a time both parties used it, but in the end the republicans had to leave it.

The influence of the two other emigrés from Venice is shown by the fact that Mniewski was regularly elected president, and Dmochowski put in charge of the most important questions of foreign policy. When he had met with setbacks in France and in Prussia, there was talk of replacing him, and Prozor too, with Generals Kołyszko and Łaźniński from Venice.

M. Oginski was sent from Venice as agent to Turkey, charged with obtaining help to create a Polish army able to attack Galicia. His efforts, begun on 21 April 1796, came to nothing; they even ended by setting him at odds with his former supporters in Venice, since he was bound to express the republican line of the Deputation. Besides, the final break between the moderate camp and the republicans in Paris on 20 February 1796 put the corresponding groups in Venice on a war footing.

The supreme command of the Venetian army in the hands of Poles

In 1796 the Prince of Nassau-Siegen came back to Venice with considerable funds. His royalist attitude was so well regarded by the Signoria that in April 1796, when the time came to award the supreme command of the Venetian army, it was offered to him. The other candidates were the Prince de Broglie, a French emigré living at Turin, and the Polish general Jean Komarzewski, called back from Dresden.

The Emperor's first minister, Baron François Thugut, was hostile to the candidacy of the Prince of Nassau-Siegen, and made this known (in June) to the Venetian ambassador in Vienna, Agostino Garzoni. Thereupon it was decided to offer the supreme command to General Komarzewski, whose chief rival was the former colonel of the French Swiss Guards, de Salis[^25]. General Komarzewski stayed at the Albergo Reale from 6 June to the beginning of August, negotiating with the Signoria. The Poles of Venice and the Legions

A new situation arose after the arrival in Paris from Warsaw of Lieutenant-General Jean-Henri Dąbrowski, renowned for the victories he had won in the Insurrection, whom the French government authorised to form Polish Legions on Italian territory[^30].

The Deputation, through Joseph Sułkowski, Bonaparte's aide-de-camp, and General Łaźniński, leader of the Polish republicans at Venice, naturally tried to make this attempt fail. Nevertheless General Dąbrowski left for Milan at Sołtyk's expense; and when the negotiations with the commander-in-chief Bonaparte dragged on and threatened to break down as the money ran out, the generosity of Sołtyk and Potocki made possible the conclusion of a convention with Lombardy (9 January 1797), which decided the creation of the Polish Legions, later known for their feats of arms.

Conclusion

Thus, in the first three years after the Insurrection, Venice gave hospitality to a hundred and thirty eminent emigrés from Poland. The oligarchy in power particularly favoured the royalists, offering in turn, in 1796, the supreme command of its armies to an honorary citizen of Poland, Prince Charles of Nassau-Siegen, and then to General Jean Komarzewski.

Other emigrés, by contrast, were kept under strict police watch, which is why we have so many details of their stay in Venice, preserved in the police reports to the Inquisitors.

The supporters of the Constitution of 3 May and the republicans set up there their first government in emigration (the Polish Emigration Committee), but having failed to obtain its recognition by France, they soon after formed the Deputation in Paris, led by the Poles who had come from Venice; it had its seat in the "Palace of the Emigration" and sent its plenipotentiaries abroad.

Those who directed the clandestine struggle inside Poland in the Lwów Centre set up at Venice their representation, which spread news and issued appeals for the

[p. 253]

continuation of the struggle in occupied Poland. With the funds collected in Poland, independence movements and the forming of Polish armed forces abroad were subsidised, both the Legions formed in Turkey and those of General J. H. Dąbrowski in Italy, which later became justly famous. Their relations with Venice form a separate chapter in the history of Polish-Italian relations. This question has been studied in one of my works printed in Polish[^31]. I hope that it will soon be published in Italian translation.

APPENDIX

(added by the author after the discussion)

Thanks to Professors Angelo Tamborra and Salvo Mastellone we have learned of several problems of the political ideology of Italy in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Personally, what interests me most are the questions of the beginnings of the Italian Risorgimento that coincide with the Polish struggles for independence, namely the Kościuszko insurrection and Dąbrowski's Polish Legions in Italy. The fact that towards the end of the eighteenth century 25,000 Poles fought for the democratisation and the republican régime of Italy, divided into several dozen small states, and that half of them died in these struggles, speaks for itself.

The Poles conspired for the unification of the peninsula, in order to give it a progressive régime; the Italians for their part gave asylum to the Polish emigrés. It was in Venice, in January 1795, that the first government of the Polish emigration was set up – "the Polish Committee at Venice" – until the establishment of the Polish Deputation in Paris.

Of course the Polish emigrés differed in their ideas: there were also monarchists among them, who stood for the maintenance of absolute power in Europe. To two of them the Venetian Signoria offered the high command of its armies in the struggle against the republicans. I mean

[p. 254]

Prince Karol of Nassau-Siegen, who after his marriage to Karolina Godzka obtained the indigenat (title of nobility) and became a Polish deputy, and who, settled in Venice, supported the Bourbons with considerable sums; and General Jan Komarzewski, a close collaborator of King Stanisław August. Among civilians a considerable part was played by the Polish magnates settled in Italy: by Stanisław Poniatowski, the King's nephew, former treasurer of Lithuania, who had a palace in Rome and the admirable estate of Villa Leoni near Florence; and by the former "scripteur" of the army of Lithuania (then the chief commissary) François Michel Rzewuski, who owned a palace in Rome and another in Pisa, a friend of the Ronconi of Florence.

The main current of the Polish emigration, however, wished for the restoration of the Constitution of 3 May 1791, and the Polish Legions of General Jean Henri Dąbrowski, as auxiliary troops of the Cisalpine Republic, guarded its frontiers and later supported the Roman, Neapolitan and Ligurian Republics. This party wanted to convene the prorogued Polish Diet at Milan; it also put great effort into education in the emigration, and founded among other things two Military Schools, at Rome and at Mantua, so that every soldier, on returning to his country, should at the same time spread progress. The glorious watchwords of the Legions deserve to be stressed, GLI UOMINI LIBERI – SONO FRATELLI, as does their principle that as long as the Poles had not recovered their own freedom they would fight for the freedom of others, and in this case for that of the Italians. Besides, the Poles were among those who understood the need for the unification of Italy, and it must be said that they contributed a little to it. The brightest note of this period is the Song of the Legions, the famous Dąbrowski Mazurka, composed in July 1797 at Reggio, which is to this day the Polish national anthem.

Among the emigrés there was also a very strong group of Jacobins, eager to introduce all the achievements of the French Revolution. Of course, not everything that was good for France was necessarily good for Poland or Italy. As early as 1797 the Polish Jacobins were conspiring together with the Italian Jacobins against Bonaparte. It was they who in 1798 found themselves in the newly formed Roman republican army. General George Grabowski, made famous by the defence

[p. 255]

of Wilno in 1794, was appointed its commander-in-chief in the course of the Neapolitan war, and some twenty other Polish officers held high commands. Curiously, there was more material on this question in the Polish archives than in the Roman archives, destroyed by the reaction. At Mantua two Jacobins, General F. Rymkiewicz and Captain C. Godebski, edited the first Polish military periodical, La Décade des Légions.

The Polish Legions, which had from 10,000 to 15,000 soldiers under arms, perfectly trained and ready for battle, aroused hopes and fears in Italy: the hope that they would protect the country against Austria, and the fear that, making use of their military strength, they would create their own state on the territory of Tuscany or Apulia. It is true that in 1798 the Consulate of Rome itself proposed to give land in the neighbourhood of Rome to 3,000 Polish legionaries, so that, as military colonists, they would support the new régime. General Dąbrowski laughed at these suspicions and proposals and declared that, beautiful as Italy was, the Poles wanted to live only in their own country. Only the Ionian Islands were at one moment considered, as an independent base from which to set out for occupied Poland by way of Greece.

What remains of this period are the rich official archives of the emigrés and the legionaries, and also private archives, for many legionaries married in Italy. It is enough to recall that the son of the commander of the Legions, in time a general himself, Jean Michel Dąbrowski, married a Genoese, Emilia di Negro. The daughter of General Jean Henri Dąbrowski, Karolina, married a Neapolitan who later became famous, General Joseph Prince Palombini. General Joseph Grabiński married Marie Broglie d'Amera and, as a Bolognese landowner, was appointed commander-in-chief of the anti-papal rising of Bologna in 1830. The examples multiply. Some dozens of memoirs that deal with Italian questions in the years 1794 to 1807 have survived. They throw valuable light on this period. During several stays in Italy I have found, in talking with my colleagues, Italian professors, that these papers were unknown to them. I have therefore taken the trouble to make use of everything that might help to throw light on the Polish-Italian relations

[p. 256]

of that period, in the recently published work Les Légions Polonaises - La vérité et la légende 1794-1807.

I should like to stress that we know Italian scholarship above all through the journeys we make to Italy, for only a small part of the printed work reaches us.

Yet the training of a group of Italian scholars in the Polish language seems to me indispensable, and it is with pleasure that we see scholarship holders coming to Cracow. This is indispensable, because there is a rich archival material on Italy in Poland. Several historians publish serious contributions to Italian history which are not always translated into foreign languages, and so do not come to the knowledge of Italian historiography.

I consider conferences of this kind very valuable. They allow us to get to know one another, and they give rise to an exchange of ideas.
