## V. THE WARTHE MARSHES

### Landscape

The Berlin-Warsaw glacial valley (Urstromtal), in which the Warthe now rolls its waters sluggishly, is in places hardly wider than 3 kilometres. The river valley is mostly open. Only in the area between the towns of Koło and Konin do dense stands of alder and poplar cover the valley floor, which is fertile, though in places also sandy. As little as 150 years ago the glacial valley was ground hostile to settlement. Backwaters, bogs and high sandbanks, covered with impenetrable bushes and climbing plants, teemed with water birds. Then as now, in spring murky floods poured over the whole valley, though not with such devastating effect as on the Vistula. Here as there the spring water leaves behind the silt that is wanted for the growth of the meadows. So the wanderer today sees splendid meadows in the "Bruch", as the landscape of the Warthe valley is generally called. The cheerful twittering of the rich bird life enlivens the stillness of this peculiar landscape.

### Settlements

Over a stretch of 130 kilometres, from the former Prussian border to Leśnik south of the town of Uniejów, 63 larger German villages, with a population of 7000 souls, are strung one after another, with greater and smaller breaks. In addition, some 4000 Germans live in 105 Polish marsh villages or in isolated small German settlements. As little as 40 years ago some villages still had their old German names, such as Sophiental, Friedrichsfeld, Ludwigslust, Ingelfingen, Adelhof.

The conditions of ownership are good in most of the marsh villages. The most prosperous German settlements lie west of the town of Konin. In the foreground of farming are the raising of cattle and poultry. Large pieces of meadow are let for good money. On the higher-lying fields grain and potatoes are grown, and there too, mostly scattered, the farmsteads stand. Sometimes the villages take a position along the bank, as in Daniszew, Leszcze, Paprot Hol., Młyny, Piekarskie and Stawki. The silt soil, or "Letten" (clay), as the "Brücher" (the marsh people, also called in mockery "Wasserpatscher", water-splashers) call it, needs strong horses for ploughing. The yields of the harvest are high accordingly. The cattle are grazed here in "Koppeln" (fenced pastures) and not in "Hocken", as the Vistula German would say.

[p. 36]

In the building of houses, brick has lately gained the upper hand. The half-timbered barns of the Leśnik language island bear witness, together with the dialect, to the Pomeranian origin of the settlers. The villages near Konin have old barns of planks or modern board cladding.

### History of Settlement

The German settlement of the Warthe marshes of central Poland began around 1750. The first marsh settlement that can be shown by documents is Wenglewer Holland, which was laid out in 1767 on the lands of the estate of Sławsk, whose owner was the district judge (Landrichter) S. Leszczyc-Zielonawski. At the same time the Holländereien (Olęder colonies) of Rumin, Kolno and Sławsk arose within the lordship of Sławsk. The marsh villages lying further east, Briesener Hol., Paprot Hol., Drążyn Hol. and Tury Hol., were founded around 1775. In 1781 King Stanislaus Augustus Poniatowski confirmed the rights of the Holländerei of Majdany, lying in the Chełmno estate group (Schlüssel), the property of the Archbishop of Gniezno. The German villages opposite Majdany, Police, Daniszew and Leszcze, arose around 1785. The great Leśnik language island south of Uniejów was founded, according to its charter, in 1796. On taking over 10 Hufen of forest land the colonists paid the estate owner Milewski 189 Thaler of ground money. In 1792 German farmers settled in Czyste, Kaczka and Machnówka.

In 1845 the German villages of Józefow, Młyny, Łęg, Nowa Kaczka and Rosengarten arose in the Leśnik language island on swampy, sandy soil. All the villages were "laid out in the forest, on overgrown places, on moor soil, pastures and waste land", as a court judgement puts it. In the 19th century new villages were founded now and then, such as Neu-Czarkow in 1835. The emigration to Volhynia depopulated many villages, especially those set on poor soil.

The cloth-makers and weavers who came into the towns of Zagórów, Konin and Koło after 1818, and who managed to hold their own after a fashion until 1870, went over to other trades under the pressure of circumstances. Most of them have wholly shed their inherited nationality (Volksart), and have at times become eager standard-bearers of Polishness, so carrying a deep split into what decades ago was a closed community of church and people.

### Origin

The settlers of the Warthe marshes of central Poland came chiefly from the marshes of the Oder, the Netze and the western Warthe. The country round Landsberg an der Warthe, Sonnenburg and Driesen is often named in the church books. The "Brücher" were therefore people who knew how to deal with swamp forest and marshy meadow land, men "skilled in water works". Before the war there were among them experienced boatmen and boat-builders, who carried Polish grain from the area of Koło and Konin to Germany in Oder barges. Even now they speak a Low German (Platt) coloured by the Mark. In many villages a popular High German has already taken hold.

The inhabitants of the Leśnik language island come from the Netze district and speak the Pomeranian dialect.

[p. 38]

### Churches

The first Evangelical parish was founded in Konin in 1826, after long negotiations with the government offices. Brave Lutherans of Konin turned for legal support even to the then viceroy of Poland, Grand Duke Konstantin Konstantinowitsch. The first preacher, A. Kegler, came from Eckartsberg in Thuringia. The congregation of Zagórów was raised in 1858 from a branch (Filial) founded in 1833 to an independent parish. The branch of *Lissewo-Peisern*, lying in the Warthe marshes, arose in 1858. The congregation of Koło, at first a branch of Turek, became independent in 1903. It has had its own church since 1882.

## VI. THE KALISZ COUNTRY

### Landscape

South of the towns of Zagórów and Konin stretches a swampy, sandy plain, broken in strips by meagre pine woods. Alder carrs and rows of pollard willows along drainage ditches surround thin, peaty meadows. Sand hills, poor fields and stretches of drifting sand alternate. The field of view in this half-open landscape is narrow. Light, fine-grained sand is the most striking mark of this secluded, quiet, dreamy country. The names of the villages also point to the sandy nature of the landscape: Białe Błoto, Bielawki, Biała, Biała Panieńska, Białobrzegi, Łazy, Borowiec, Grądy, Mokre, Bagno[^3]. In earlier centuries the wealth of forest gave rise to glassworks and iron hammers, as can be seen from the village names Huta Stara, Huta Nowa, Huta Trąbczyńska, Huta Lukomska, Huta, Prażuchy, Kużnica, Huta bei Prażuchy.

20 kilometres north of Kalisz the picture of the landscape changes almost suddenly. A fertile plain, thickly set with manor farms and wholly without forest, opens before the wanderer's eyes. Here, apart from two villages that arose in the 20th century, we find no trace of German settlements.

### Settlements

The German villages of the Kalisz country lie over an area of a good 1000 km². They form a relatively closed area of settlement, in which some 25,000 Germans of Silesian stock live. Unlike other regions, we find here some large villages with a population of over 500 souls. The village of Łazińsk has 757 souls, Borowiec 668, Obory 635, Białobłoty 588. 9500 Germans live in 26 large villages, some 8000 in a further 74 smaller ones.

The quality of the soil, as already said, is far below middling. In dry years the yields of grain and root crops are small. It is said then that bread is eaten only at Christmas. On market days the German farmers bring, instead of

Over-population is heavy. In most cases it leads to the division of farms among heirs (Realteilung) and to the growth of a large rural proletariat. Seasonal work in Germany (Sachsengängerei) was widespread for many decades before the war. Even after the war hundreds of young men and girls went to Germany in spring for farm work. For three years this almost only source of earnings has run dry. Economic need has therefore reached an unbearable degree

[p. 41]

in many German villages. The conditions of ownership are fairly dismal. Of the 805 who pay church dues in the congregation of Grodziec, 101 families, or 13 in a hundred, are landless; 183 families, or 23 in a hundred, have typical dwarf holdings of 1 to 6 Morgen. In the congregations of Zagórów, Stawiszyn and Prażuchy things are by no means better.

In *house building* building with planks prevails everywhere, which is natural given the great wealth of forest. Since the shallow peaty meadows hold bog iron ore in most cases, this stone is much used for building farm buildings, even dwelling houses. Lately Jewish traders have been buying up these "iron stones" and taking them to the factories of Upper Silesia, where artificial fertiliser is made from them. In the Silesian settlements the dwelling house always stands apart. The farm buildings are often surrounded by birch groves and patches of wood. Because of the marshy, sandy ground, scattered settlement prevails with only few exceptions. The farmsteads lie off the village street, each with its own access road.

Now and then the last traces of the old folk costume are still to be met. Folk art is well kept among the Silesians. Only among them are the finely carved and tastefully decorated grave boards to be seen. Folk dance and folk song are still alive. Folk life always received new impulses from the seasonal work in Germany, though not always in a good sense. There is almost no link with the Łódź industrial area.

### History of Settlement

Around 1740 the German settler made his way with axe and spade into the tracts of the Kalisz country, remote from men and untilled since time immemorial. In 1746 the owner of the estate of Trąbczyn, Paul von Kolno-Prusiński, concluded with honest Hollanders a contract of hereditary lease (Erbpachtvertrag), by which the woods and waste lands south of the estate of Trąbczyn were given over to be brought under cultivation. The village of Łazińsk Holland arose. The castellan of Sieradz, J. Wężyk, settled four German villages on his estates at Grodziec in 1772: Borowiec, Konary, Grunde and Wielołęka. On the lands of the estate of Ceków a whole series of Holländereien arose after 1770, among them the large village of Prażuchy, which by 1799 already counted 34 farmers on 24 Hufen; in 1775 Poroże was founded; in 1775 Zbiersk Holland and Ciświca-Holland, in 1778 Lipnica and Jarantów, in 1780 Jaszczury and Zakrzyn. The owner of the landed estates of Piegonisko and Stoki, the steward (Truchsess) of Smolensk, M. Wągrowski, who had extensive woods at his disposal, laid out in 1782 the Hollän-

[p. 42]

dereien of Piegonisko, Sobiesęki and Stoki. The German village of Kazimierka-Stara has a founding charter of 1782; eight years later Kazimierka-Nowa arose. In 1784 the lord of Biskupice settled the village of Adlerholland, or Orlin, in his woods lying west of the estate. The owner of the lordship of Gadow, the noble Peter von Sokoła-Gałczyński, concluded a contract with worthy Hollanders in 1784, since his woods "promise no use whatever, neither now nor in the future, the same with regard to the proven uses which Hauländer bring to all hereditary estates". The new village is called Gadow-Holland. In 1786 Zamęty and Danowiec were founded. The starost of Odolanów (Adelnau), F. Stadnicki, settled the Holländerei of Białobłoty "on the green forest" in 1787. The hereditary lord of the estate of Chocz, the prelate K. Lipski, in 1790 gave the hard-working George Müller forest for Holländereien on the boundary of Obory, granting seven free years. The taker bound himself to settle twenty Hollanders in the new colony of Józefów. So, even before the last partition of Poland, the whole forest area north of Kalisz between the Prosna and the Teleszyna had been opened up by German settlers. Numerous clearing islands, larger and smaller, studded the forest land thickly.

In the 19th century the work of clearing went on. Around the old villages a ring of daughter settlements arose here and there. So near Prażuchy the German villages of Celestyny, Feliksów, Kotwasice, Niedżwiady, Annopol, Wygoda; in the neighbourhood of Grodziec in 1845, from the estate of Grabieniec, the colony of Wojciechowo, later Wycinki, Boberfeld, Michalinów, Łagiewniki, Sołomina. From the emancipation of the peasants on, the work of settlement rested entirely. The space free for settlement [sielungsfreie] is now used up. The nearness of the Prussian border gave the young people of the villages chances of earning in the Reich; seasonal work in Germany slowly developed, which in summer wholly emptied many German settlements. The emigration to Volhynia did not reshape the Kalisz country as strongly as other German areas. No village was noticeably weakened by it.

In the Kalisz country lies the first and oldest German industrial town in central Poland. The castellan (Burgherr) of Gniezno, M. H. von Górowo-Górowski, founded the town of *Władysławow* in 1738. In the same year arose "the praiseworthy craft of the ticking-weavers, fustian-weavers and linen-weavers in Władysławow". To the present day the German community, though small in numbers, has held out bravely in spite of every economic hardship.

German Catholic weavers from Bohemia came to *Turek* around 1826, for whose religious needs a Catholic priest from Bohemia was even appointed. At the same time weavers came from Saxony, West Prussia and Silesia. In all, 56 in a hundred came from German Bohemia, 26 in a hundred from Saxony, 12 in a hundred from West Prussia and 6 in a hundred from Prussian Silesia. In 1828 a weavers' guild was founded in Turek, which exists to the present, although the German members of the guild are already very much in the minority.

[p. 44]

The town of *Kalisz* was rebuilt by the Prussian government after the fire of 1793 and settled with many Prussian officials. German craftsmen, merchants and tradesmen joined them. After 1831 many German entrepreneurs left Kalisz; even so it counted 2405 Germans in 1860, 2500 Evangelicals in 1913, and in 1921 only 1297 Evangelicals. The Kalisz cloth factory of Repphahn, founded in 1816, was known far beyond the borders of central Poland. Not far from Kalisz, in Opatówek, Fiedler founded a fine-cloth factory in 1823.

### Origin

Apart from the villages of Józefów, Wielopole and part of Białobłoty, in which Pomeranian farmers settled, the German population of the Kalisz country is of *Silesian* origin. The Silesian dialect, with certain gradations, prevails without exception. Custom and usage, folk art and folk song are of a truly Silesian stamp. In the church books of the Evangelical congregations of Grodziec, Stawiszyn and Zagórów the places of origin given for the immigrants are above all villages in the neighbourhood of: Bentschen, Neutomischl, Grätz, Wollstein, Tirschtiegel, Zduny, Wreschen, Pudewitz, Krotoschin, Kirch-Boruj, Militsch, Heinzenburg, Parchwitz, Wohlau, Brieg. All these places lie within the area of the Silesian dialect, in southern Posen and the neighbouring Lower Silesia. The settlers came from the new settlements that had arisen in the 17th and 18th centuries. In the wooded, swampy areas of the Kalisz country they found soil like the soil they were used to at home around Grätz, Neutomischl and Wollstein. The growing of hops, carried on in many Kalisz colonies until the World War, also points to the Neutomischl area.

Only a few of the stated origins point to other dialect areas. Only from the neighbouring Oder marshes did a limited number of immigrants come, from the neighbourhood of Landsberg an der Warthe, Sonnenburg and Driesen.

### Schools

A report written in 1797 by Pastor Grimm of Stawiszyn for the consistory in Posen gives German elementary schools in the following Hollander villages: Zbiersk, Prażuchy, Kosmowo, Dannowitz, Zamenty, Zakrzyń, Przespolew, Czachulec, Grojetz and Mycielin. With the schools in Obory, Łazińsk, Białobłoty, Gadowskie Hol. and Wierzchy, which also already existed then, this gives a total of 15 German elementary schools. The way of working and the conditions of pay

[p. 45]

of the "school-keepers" (Schulhalter) were like those in other German areas of settlement. Some schools, as in Adler-Holland, Łazińsk and Obory, had 15, 18 and 22 Morgen of land respectively. Besides teaching, the teacher's most important duties were reading the sermon every Sunday, baptising and burying. In the first half of the 19th century religious schools were founded in the newly laid-out daughter settlements. The teachers' level of education remained very low as before. Around 1870, after the German teachers' seminary in Warsaw put its pupils at the disposal of the German elementary schools, the state of the elementary schools slowly began to rise. Even so, in 1898 the cantorate schools made up 74 in a hundred of all German schools.

### Churches

The mother congregation of the Kalisz country is that of *Władysławow*, founded in 1776. The first pastor, Martin Marggraf, travelled from Władysławow not only through the Kalisz country but through almost the whole of Kuyavia. Four years later the parish of *Stawiszyn* was founded. In 1795 the Lutherans of Kalisz asked the town authorities to appoint an Evangelical preacher. Pastor Karl Michael Sinell, who took up the office in the same year, became the real founder of the congregation. In Prussian times the congregation of *Grodziec* arose, with the lively part of the lord of the manor, Count Stadnicki. The first pastor, D. Bergmann, came from Fraustadt. In Prażuchy, which Pastor Grimm of Stawiszyn had served until then, a parish of its own arose in 1808 with the active help of the lord of the manor, Celiński, owner of the lordship of Ceków. In *Iwanowice* the estate owner Wężyk founded an Evangelical congregation in 1808. In 1818, since lightning had burnt down the church in Iwanowice, it was moved to *Sobiesęki*. In the weavers' town of *Turek* a branch was founded in 1827, followed in 1845 by an independent congregation. That the great landowners took a direct part in the founding of four Evangelical congregations shows how important the German settlers in town and country were to the Polish nobility.
