III. Marianne Countess of Hoym and Friedrich Ludwig Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen

Amalie Louise Marianne Charlotte Countess of Hoym was counted one of the wittiest and most charming young ladies of her time. She knew how to move in society with an elegance that was yet natural, and not only this: as the future sole heiress of the extensive possessions in Saxony and Silesia she was one of the richest women in the duchy of Sachsen-Weimar-Eisenach and in Prussian Silesia.[^p33-1] Her circumstances nonetheless did not permit her the free choice of her future husband. In noble circles as among the peasantry the contracting of a marriage had for a long time, quite independently of the literary impulses of Sturm und Drang, the character of a contract of sale, "in which the bride was nothing but the object being sold - without an opinion of her own".[^p33-2] In contrast to her future husband, whom the young woman had even been commended to by Frederick the Great, she, as a well-brought-up daughter of a good house, can hardly have had the chance at nineteen to make enquiries about the hereditary prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, eighteen years her senior. What course the marriage contracted on 8 April 1782 at Gleina in Saxony would take, the young countess could not have foreseen with the best will.

The hereditary prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, born on 31 January 1746 at Ingelfingen, seemed destined for a military career. At ten the young hereditary prince was already yearning for his first military deployment, for Prussia had just begun the Third Silesian War, which it waged against Austria, Russia, France, Sweden and the majority of the princes of the Empire. At barely thirteen he received a captaincy in a Franconian circle regiment, but by the time he might have taken up the post at sixteen the war, now seven years old, was almost over. Frederick the Great, however, was and remained his great model, which he strove to emulate. He therefore did not seek military renown among the Imperial troops either. He soon stood in the particular favour of the Prussian king Frederick II. As a major he entered Prussian service in 1766. In 1775 Friedrich Ludwig received the patent of lieutenant colonel. In 1778 he became colonel of infantry in an Upper Silesian corps. In the War of the Bavarian Succession he could not however prove his military talent, for the Prussian

The Prussian roi philosophe Frederick II with his strong affinity for an Enlightenment of French provenance, and Karl August von Hardenberg - a friend of Prince Friedrich zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, who in Ansbach-Bayreuth had already set an example in the enforcement of necessary reforms[^p34-4] - may also have served him as models in his efforts at reform in the Hohenlohe heartland. Until the French Revolution the reigning princes of Hohenlohe had lived on in an absolute political dead calm, in the conviction that their hallowed by tradition sense of right would outlast the coming centuries as well.[^p34-5]

The research of the historians Hansmartin Decker-Hauff and Gerd Wunder establishes the origins of the house of Hohenlohe in the year 1095 at Weikersheim in the Tauber district. There Konrad von Wikartesheim from 1207 first used the name "de Hohenloch" in his seal. His sons, Heinrich and Albert, bore this name from then on and added to the seal a coat of arms - two leopardised lions arranged one above the other.[^p34-6] Emperor Frederick II, in whose campaigns they served faithfully, rewarded them with the lordship of Langenburg, when the Staufer was at odds with his son Henry VI about 1231.

In these regions, remote for the house of Hohenlohe at the time, it could not indeed hold its ground against the landgraves of Hesse, yet the comital title remained to it, which for the consolidation of the territory immediately surrounding it mattered in so far as it helped to stabilise and extend the territorial supremacy in the Hohenlohe heartland.[^p35-8]

Toward the Reformation, which elsewhere had not only split the faithful into two camps but had also become a matter of politics, the house of Hohenlohe behaved rather hesitantly and wait-and-see. An impulse to take "sides" came first from a petition of the town of Oehringen directed against the frivolous goings-on of the canons.[^p35-9] With the church order of 1553 the Reformation was sealed. Two years later the counts Ludwig Casimir and Eberhard resolved the partition of the land into the two main lines Hohenlohe-Neuenstein and Hohenlohe-Waldenburg. In the course of the seventeenth century further splittings of the land followed from rules of inheritance, and with them the arising of numerous side lines. From the main line of Neuenstein the lines of Langenburg, later Oehringen and Weikersheim, branched off in 1610. From the line of Langenburg there formed in 1701 the lines of Ingelfingen and Kirchberg. The main line Hohenlohe-Waldenburg divided once more in 1688 into the lines of Schillingsfürst, Bartenstein and Jagstberg. Already in October 1667 the sons of Count Georg Friedrich II of the line Waldenburg-Schillingsfürst, Count Christian (1627–1675), progenitor of the house of Bartenstein, and Count Ludwig Gustav (1634–1697), founder of the house of Schillingsfürst, had returned to the Catholic faith.[^p35-10] Confessional differences and notorious disputes of inheritance weighed on the relations of the various lines the more also because the Catholic counts had already been raised to the rank of prince in 1744, while the counts of the Neuenstein line were raised only in 1764.[^p35-11]

Though branded by many of his peers as a betrayal of emperor and empire, he had seen to it that Hohenlohe was included in the Peace of Basel. The neutrality of Hohenlohe prevented not only the danger of becoming a theatre of war; the prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen moreover achieved that it was freed from the payment of 300,000 livres of contributions, which would have been laid upon the principality of the Franconian circle of the Empire. Nonetheless the quarterings of Austrian and French troops beginning in 1798 weighed heavily on the budget of Hohenlohe[^p36-13].

Even before his marriage with the rich heiress Marianne Countess of Hoym the prince's finances had been in disorder. Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen cherished the pious belief that with the revenues of the estates lying in Upper Silesia he could set new projects on the way in his heartland. After the peace congress of Rastatt he tried to attach to the Hohenlohe territory the lands of the monastery of Schöntal, the Teutonic Order's district around Mergentheim and parts of the Palatinate as well as of the archbishopric of Mainz. After a then following new ordering of the German Empire he would have wished for himself the dignity of a duke of Franconia.[^p36-14] But of this nothing came, on account of the political development. Still, Friedrich Ludwig von Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had in his heartland been able to streamline the house authorities by the creation of a central instance and to renew the police administration. To the economy he sought to help with the building up and extension of commercial undertakings. Thus in 1794 he had conceived the plan of founding a joint-stock company for Hohenlohe coal mining. As the diggings brought no hard coal to light, this undertaking had to be dropped, as had the reactivation of the princely salt works at Weißbach, existing still from before the Thirty Years War. Encroaching Kocher floodwater and the influence of carbonic acid gas had prevented a profitable operation.[^p36-15] Profit from these undertakings had been drawn above all by Hofrat Glenk as his adviser, who had counselled him to invest ever larger sums and to demand a high salary for himself, which was also granted him. The good will of Friedrich Ludwig von Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen to advance his heartland economically cannot be denied him, all the more as he could look with pride on a numerous posterity which it befitted his rank to provide for. Even before the beginning of the French Revolution four of six children had been born to him by his wife. The youngest child, Prince Ludwig Wilhelm Eduard, however died a few years later on 9 February 1790. There followed the trio named so by the princess of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen: Friedrich August Carl, born on 27 November 1784 at Breslau, the future heir, then Adelaide (Adelheid), (1787–1858), who later married Ludwig of the line Hohenlohe-Kirchberg, and on 20 November 1788 Emilie, who was later wedded to the count of Erbach-Fürstenau († 1859).[^p37-18] On 16 November 1793 Auguste saw the light of the world, and on 29 January 1797 Prince Adolph. It is natural that Prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen as a loving and solicitous father worried about the future of his children. The debts heaped up additionally in the course of the years therefore weighed on him urgently. A possibility of securing his family's livelihood in the long term he believed he could accomplish with the purchase of the Rittergut Bytkow in the Upper Silesian district of Beuthen, which he acquired on 24 April 1801 for a consideration of 43,000 Reichsthaler. To this Rittergut belonged a zinc smeltery in the wood of Bytkow and two hard-coal pits at Ignazhütte. After the purchase the complex was renamed "Hohenlohe-Hütte".[^p38-22]

With the engagement of the engineer John Baildon, who came from Carron near Edinburgh, the prince had drawn to himself a first-class adviser. The director of the Silesian mining office, Friedrich Wilhelm Graf von Reden, had been made aware of Baildon on an instruction journey in 1793 and had first taken him into the Prussian state service. With his technical knowledge Graf Reden succeeded in laying the foundations for an enormous industrial upswing in Upper Silesia. Still in 1790 the annual cut of wood had amounted to 200,000 Festmeter. The region embraced, by estimation, nearly 28 million cubic metres, but by the usual charring the wood was badly used, and experts complained of the increasing ravaging of the woods.[^p38-23] In the nearer surroundings of Beuthen mighty seams of hard coal had been discovered, and the task had fallen to Baildon to erect at Gleiwitz the first coke blast furnace on the mainland.[^p38-24]

"It is hard to judge from afar", the privy councillor Johann Wolfgang von Goethe had once observed. The duties of service which Prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had to perform as Prussian general and governor of Breslau prevented both a regular presence in his residence at Ingelfingen and in the immediate neighbourhood of the Upper Silesian industrial works. In his nature the prince was already rather like the people of the post-industrial age. Having to be present, by his profession, at constantly changing places of deployment brought with it a flightiness of presence that had especially fatal consequences for his economic interests. In the summer of 1806 the loss of the independence of the Empire struck him, joined with the annexation of his lordship by king Friedrich I of Württemberg.[^p39-26]

Of his military capacities, despite strong short-sightedness and violent fits of gout that plagued him at times, still entirely convinced, he had once on the occasion of a banquet in the Hohenlohe heartland announced to the representative of the French military of his "greatest" political antagonist, Napoleon: "J'estimerai le jour pour le plus heureux de ma vie, ou je pourrai me trouver vis-à-vis de lui, pour le combattre." [I shall esteem the day the happiest of my life on which I may find myself face to face with him, to fight him.][^p39-27] In the following autumn of 1806 the exact opposite came to pass. By Napoleon and his troops the Prussian field commander was taught the most painful defeat in the double battle of Jena and Auerstedt, which finally put an end to his military career.

From the battle, which had been fought by the Prussian main army with 58000 soldiers and 46500 soldiers of the Saxon division as well as by Rüchel's reinforcement corps with 15260 men, the prince could send home only a remnant troop of 12000 survivors.[^p39-28] Napoleon's victory meant not only the prince's greatest personal military defeat, it was the complete collapse of Prussia. Not least because of the extremely hard terms of peace which the French emperor, out of his own grace, dictated to the Prussian king Friedrich Wilhelm III already on 25 October 1806: beside the recognition of the Confederation of the Rhine he demanded a war indemnity of 100 million francs and Prussia's renunciation of the land west of the Elbe, but also the greater part of the Polish territories occupied by Prussia with the town of Danzig. This was altogether 190,000 square kilometres with 3.8 million inhabitants.[^p39-29]

When Prince Friedrich von Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen after the end of the battle wanted to withdraw to his Upper Silesian possessions, to look after the smelting works that had run into trouble, this was at first denied him. He was brought to Oehringen as a prisoner. The engineer John Baildon, with whom he stood in correspondence to keep him informed of the state of the works, he could no longer help out of his own strength.[^p40-30]

The only person who was in a position to halt the collapse of the works was the princess Sacken. At first she was not ready to do so, because Prince zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had carried out the purchase of the Rittergut Bytkow without consulting her, and she was of the opinion that the estate could have been brought to the house on more favourable conditions than it had been. Of the acute misery in which the prince found himself she had in the end nonetheless received knowledge. She now wanted to await the bankruptcy, in order then to acquire the Rittergut Bytkow. Only the earnest plea of Prince Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen to preserve the estate "durch ihre Fürsorge auf ehrenvolle Art der Familie zu erhalten" [through her care, in an honourable way, for the family] changed her attitude.[^p40-31] On 13 October 1808 she therefore wrote to him:

We would both now have a large portion less of sorrow if you had earlier granted me the trust that I deserve so completely. Through want of trust you have harmed the good cause, and it can now no longer be carried out so well as it could have been done before.[^p40-32]

After the prince had thus appealed to her sense of family duty, she had the possession transferred to herself, paid the debts resting on it, and united Bytkow with Schlawentzitz into a Fideikommiß.[^p40-33] Although she was no longer willing to make money available for the completion of the blast furnace building begun by John Baildon, because she herself had no reserve of money left, John Baildon succeeded under the most difficult conditions in building at Blechhammer a first plate-rolling works after English plans, which could begin operation in November 1807.[^p40-34]

This changed nothing however in the disastrous financial situation of the prince, who, although he no longer had the means for it, kept up great house as before. Adolf Fischer, the historiographer of the house of Hohenlohe, described him thus:

In matters of money he was like a child that does not know the worth of money. As often as he received any, existing debts were paid, wherefore he did not long want for credit. Even after the catastrophe of 1806 he could not for a long time accustom himself to the necessary restriction. (…) When money arrived, a pressing creditor was satisfied, even if nothing remained over.[^p40-35]

With the consent of the daughter Marianne, provision had already been made in the marriage contracts concluded at Leipzig on 9 October 1781 and 8 August 1787 that aimed at clearly restricting any claims of possession and the enjoyment of the Hoym estates on the part of the future husband Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen. As guardian of her daughter the princess Sacken reserved to herself the right of the administration of the estates of her first husband, count Hoym, for as long as she wished. According to the marriage contract which she had concluded with count Hoym while still Freiin von Dieskau, half of the whole revenues from the Silesian and Saxon estates was due to her, that is, about 8250 thalers. She would however have renounced 2250 thalers of the revenues if that money had been applied to the extinguishing of the debts still resting on the estates. The sum was then to be invested, with the prince's share, in a money-Fideikommiß:

As has been observed as law until now, to lay up yearly four thousand Reichsthaler of the revenues for the increase of capital, so it shall also remain in the future with this useful institution, and the capitals arising from it shall be regarded and treated as a money-Fideicommiß entirely in the same spirit and according to the same rules as the estates themselves.

As a further precaution it was expressly declared,

that His Serene Highness has no authority to alter the natural or legal substance of the estates and capitals and ever to burden them with a debt or other charge,

in order to preserve them "zum Flor beiderseitiger künftiger Descendenz" [for the flourishing of the future descendants of both sides].[^p42-42] Rather, for the lifetime of the princess it was to remain unalterably with the "Commun-Administration introduced as expedient vermöge Abkommens Breslau, den 8. August 1787" [by agreement of Breslau of 8 August 1787].[^p42-43] Because of the prince's incapacity to manage economically, all the more as his creditors constantly pressed for the settlement of the debts accumulated with them, he, to come by "ready means", sold on again far below their worth considerable family possessions he had acquired, such as the lordships of Betsche, Zogorowo, Trobczyn, Botkowo and Winiec. With the transfer of the Rittergut Bytkow into the existing family Fideikommiß of Schlawentzitz the princess Sacken had at least in this case put a bar before the complete selling-off of the estates.

Appendix 8

"Ein Wort des Vertrauens" [A Word of Trust]

In a very personal writing[^p325-5] of countess Marianne von der Osten-Sacken, who had been married in a first marriage to the prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, this countess set out her motives for the separation from her first husband:

A word of trust to my two sons, the prince August von Hohenlohe Oehringen and the prince Adolph von Hohenlohe, and for the present in his stead to His Excellency the justice minister von Kircheisen, the privy councillor of justice von Sack and the chief medical councillor Cosmar.

With my son, the prince August von Hohenlohe, and with the aforementioned gentlemen guardians of my remaining children, I permit myself, with the enclosed declaration over the testament of my departed good mother, to speak yet a word of trust. Until these named persons, and my children still under age, have reached the years of their Vollblütigkeit [full maturity], these confidential lines are for no one. I beg therefore that they not be laid among the papers that can fall into the hands of any third party outside this small circle.

It is now twelve years since I left prince Hohenlohe, - in my view I had to take this step, to save my children's fortune. More than once I had had to sign sums of enormous amounts for the rescue of the prince, against my mother's wish, and yet his mass of debt grew again like the crop of the rooster. Had I remained in relation with him these 12 years, then, especially in the last time, when every household, even economically conducted, was falling into money troubles anyway - the splintering of my fortune would have been certain.

I therefore renounced the splendour of my station, I renounced the advantage of my rank - but I renounced at the same time also the renown and the greatest happiness of my life, in that I tore myself out of the midst of my children and fled into a foreign land, given over to the vengeance of the world, to the momentary displeasure of my mother, beloved by me above all, so inwardly honoured and worthy - who at the time was less informed of some of the occasions of my step than she later became. In the first upsurge of her displeasure she demanded of me, perhaps excited by other impulses, a multitude of sacrifices, which I brought to her all willingly for her sake. I marvel now myself, when I read through the Transact of 19 November 1799 and the revers and declarations that followed it, which I had to sign within a short space of time, over the demands that were made of me. As mistress, lawful heiress of the most beautiful estates in Saxony and in Silesia, I was not left even a little place of my paternal ancestors on which I might have lived quiet and withdrawn from the world that misjudged me, but I was thrust out with a cold hand beyond the borders of the possessions of my fathers.

I know, and I knew it then just as well as now, that no power in the world could have compelled me to sign all these documents which were to be the legal necessity of my temporal welfare; I know that it would have been easy for me to obtain far more favourable conditions

[^p325-4]: 4 HZAN K 172, F1, F2, Brief von John Baildon an Fürst Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen vom 8. März 1808
[^p325-5]: 5 APK Hohenlohe Koscz. 480, Brief der Gräfin Osten-Sacken vom 12. Oktober 1811

[p. 326]

- but I signed with mute obedience all these papers. Good transfigured mother! they did not flow from your heart, these cold demands of those documents - therefore they shall not rest on your coffin; I alone will bear all their consequences for myself! I signed all those papers partly to reconcile my kind mother with my unconditional submission, partly because I recognised with gratitude that the welfare of my dear children was secured by them. In this great pledge my own welfare was given away.

What should have been sacrificed of the princess Hohenlohe, of the heiress of the Hoym stock, for me, the mother gave up with loving intent, for she gave it to the dearest thing in the world, her children.

The feast of reconciliation that I celebrated with my kind mother in a quiet corner soon led me back to the warm mother-heart, and I went, now reconciled with myself, into a foreign land, there - so indeed Convenience willed it - separated from my dear ones, to spend my life if possible in quiet.

The mother travelled into the land that unlocked not etiquette and calculation but love and regard - heaven gave me two children - the care of their upbringing - the untiring diligence for their education, was the only compensation - the greatest joy of the burnt mother-heart separated from my other children - and I doubled this diligence for their education, since it is the only treasure I could gather for these poor creatures. Yet my motherly duty commands me to stand up for the peace of these my younger children with the same warmth with which I sacrificed myself for the welfare of the children of my first marriage. You all! have lain on my heart, you all! are to my heart alike dear, alike precious!

The welfare of my youngest children suffers from the dispositions of the maternal testament, if I die earlier, so is my husband[^p326-6] hardly able to bring them up according to their station.

My son August knows his sisters - He received them with love and that brotherly goodwill which His good, noble and feeling heart only marks the more; and I am convinced also of my son Adolph - they are both too good, too noble, for a stepbrotherly streak against their sisters, in every respect harshly set back, to be feared from them, and certainly, you cannot wish the misfortune of your poor innocent sisters. I demand no generosity - and beg I will not for my children - least of all from their siblings - I therefore only commend to my beloved sons my two youngest daughters and repeat, for the satisfaction of all parties concerned, those fair proposals of settlement regarding the purchase of Bellin which I set out, dated the 22nd of September, in my letter to Herr O.M.R. Cosmar and detailed there more closely, and to which I refer in all points.

If this proposal is approved, then I declare, this time not after prescribed formulas but - with a completely free and happy heart, that I then regard myself and my children as fully satisfied, and preserve the love of my sons for your sisters and for me in tender motherly care - and acknowledge the co-representation of the gentlemen curators with warm gratitude.

Berlin 12. October 1811

Marianne countess of Osten-Sacken

née countess Hoym

Appendix 10

Clarification of the debt relations of the daughter Marianne countess of Osten-Sacken

Beside the debt obligations that Marianne countess of Osten-Sacken had signed for her first husband, the prince Friedrich Ludwig zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, she had also, in her second marriage with the count Friedrich Ludwig von der Osten-Sacken, got into a debt situation which she could hardly still manage without her mother's help. Her mother therefore attempted to reach a settlement with the creditors. For this purpose she had charged her confidant, the medical councillor Cosmar, to travel to Güstrow, to examine the situation there. The agreements reached there were taken up into her codicil of 6 November 1807:[^p327-8]

Since my lady daughter, the countess von Sacken, is so deeply entangled in debts that the opening of bankruptcy over her fortune is to be feared, I hereby charge the chief medical councillor Cosmar to betake himself to Güstrow and to regulate the debt relation of my lady daughter and her husband, the count von Sacken, as well as is possible, to come to an agreement with their creditors over their satisfaction resp. treatment according to his best judgement and conscientiousness, to examine and fix the amount of their claims, to determine whether they are satisfied entirely and completely or whether only a part is paid and in what terms the creditors are to be discharged. I give to Herr Cosmar full power and authority herein, that he, with all the creditors, both those [creditors] secured on the Gülzow estates as on the estates of Hülseburg and Bellin, or on one of these estates, as also with the ordinary credi-

[^p327-8]: 8 HZAN Kosz. 472

[p. 328]

tors, examine and conclude as well as he will and can, and regulate everything completely, so that the peace and contentment of my lady daughter is restored. I authorise Herr von Cosmar further, over the estates of Bellin and Hülseburg, to conclude every contract and settlement that may be necessary and expedient. (…) All sums which Herr von Cosmar will promise to the creditors of the two Sacken estates I will pay out of my means and from my fortune.
