# Judiciary of the nobility from the 15th to the 18th century

# Abstract

*The judiciary of the age of the feudal monarchy changed its character in a natural way into an estate one, which was bound up with the emergence of the social estates, above all the nobility, which as a result of the political processes and the social changes under way won ever greater privileges, among them judicial immunities*[^2]*. This study has sketched in general the judicial organs of the administration of justice in the Polish Commonwealth from the 15th century to the decline of the Polish state. These organs were naturally not uniform in structure or organisation, and their competences overlapped. They evolved with time, though it is a fact that these changes did not keep pace with the changes taking place in the international environment around the First Commonwealth, and were not able to answer the needs both of the reform of the state and of the social changes. At a certain stage they even became a burden on the political reforms, adding their share, as it were, to the ossification of the structures of the then Polish state and its fall at the end of the 18th century. # 1. The land law

As has already been said, in the age of the Kingdom of Poland, in the times when the noble judiciary was separating out, it rested on customary law. There was no uniform codification of the laws and procedures used by the judicial organs in the process of the administration of justice. This caused an enormous legal chaos and an uncertainty of legal dealings, and the erosion of the courts' authority and, indirectly, of the State's as well. The lack of consistent procedures was not, however, the chief cause of the courts' weakness but the dead execution of their rulings. That weakness was bound up with the nobility's fear that its privileges and liberties would be infringed by a strong central power. Through the whole existence of the First Commonwealth that state of things held, and every attempt to change it was torpedoed, which in the end led to the fall of the State. The same troubles touched the administration and the organs of the state in general, whose practice and activity, minimalist in their form and substance, was tolerated just so long as it did not enter on a collision course with the particular interests of the nobility.

[p. 228]

Customary law never saw a true codification, though as is known Casimir the Great in the Statutes of Wiślica of 1347 introduced the principle of setting aside the force of a custom that worked to the harm. This led neither then nor in the later ages to a codifying of the laws in a uniform manner, one that would have strengthened the central power and the unitary function of the state, though it should be noted that such attempts were made.

The best known of the sources of customary law was the so-called Book of Elbląg of the 13th century[^3], which owed its name to the place of its discovery, that is the town library of Elbląg in the 19th century. It held a register of the legal customs in force in the Polish lands, used by the Teutonic Knights to judge the Polish population under their rule.

Another source of the laws and procedures in the field of the law of the early age of the nobility was the Book of Henryków, called the Book of the Foundation of the Monastery of Saint Mary the Virgin at Henryków, which describes the history of the foundation and the endowment of the Cistercian abbey at Henryków, written in Latin after the year 1268. That work is a document of great historical value also because of the minute description by its author of the social relations of Poland in the age of the fragmentation of the realm, and it stands as one of the most precious monuments of letters and of the law in force in the Polish lands[^4].

Customary law was gradually extended and supplemented by the privileges and the statutes of the Polish rulers and in later times by the constitutions of the Sejm as well. It made up a tangled and not fully consistent system of land law, the basis of judicial adjudication, both in the criminal field and in the civil one. Though the west of Europe began already in the 16th century to reach for the Roman jurisprudence containing practices and institutions most useful for the judicial and legal practice of the time, that practice did not take root in Polish legislation, though certain patterns passed into judicial activity through the efforts of the reformers (e.g. the bishops Jan Konarski and Piotr Tomicki). Most useful and serviceable for judicial practice proved the Statutes of Łaski of 1507, which were a successful attempt at the codification of the law at the beginning of the 16th century undertaken by the chancellor Jan Łaski, and the Lithuanian Statutes (1529, 1566, 1588), a source

# 2. The noble courts

Once the nobility had taken shape as a separate social estate, it followed as a natural course of things that the noble judiciary won a form specific and separate from the remaining courts of the Commonwealth of the time.

At the very bottom of the structure of the judiciary stood the land courts, the municipal courts and the podkomorski courts as the courts of first instance.

The first kind of courts: the land courts descended from the structures of the starosts' courts, adjudicating in the king's name. The starost judged in the name of the king, who could not be present at the court sittings in person. Hence there took root the practice of delegating the judicial powers to the office of the starost[^8]. He heard the matters over a larger territory, which resulted in the delegating of the judicial acts to judges, who had earlier attended the starosts at the court sittings. Thus gradually the function of the judging of matters passed to the judges, who began to specialise in the acts entrusted to them. The composition of the court was filled out by the representatives of the local nobility (as assessors), which was to underline not only the solemnity of the rulings and the authority of the court, but lent the legitimacy of any possible execution as well, for the organs of execution and of police did not exist yet. The execution of the rulings went through the intervention of the nobility of the given land, and so their presence on the bench had significance beyond measure[^9]. A deputy judge and a scribe were chosen for the composition. The land courts so formed covered the individual voivodeships, which already at that time were large units of the territorial division. It was necessary, then, for the composition of the land court to move between the districts and to hear there the local matters brought to its cognisance. These sittings were called the district sessions (roczki), which as a rule took place four times a year, but it happened also, that in case of need. At the end of a district session the judge proclaimed the term of the next sitting, guided by the principle that they were held again in the same place[^10]. With the development of the infrastructure the custom of the courts sitting under the open sky, in tents, dies away. The courts begin to gather in the castles, the manors, the land offices, in the monastic rooms and in the churches. The scribe was responsible for the drawing up of the written documents of the court. The subject-matter competence of the land courts covered at first chiefly the civil matters between noblemen, and at times the criminal ones too. Curiously, they performed the functions that we today assign to the notarial offices, namely it was before them that the contracts of the purchase and sale of landed estates and buildings were concluded, the pledge agreements drawn up, the acts of settlements with the clergy over the tithes, the divisions of the estates in the process of inheritance, the marking out of the boundaries between immovables, the writing of the donations for the monasteries, the loan agreements, as well as the suits for the payment of debts and other dues, and the entries into the land books (the perpetual ones) of the titles of ownership. The land courts adjudicated the questions of the disputes over the ownership of land and of other immovables, both between two parties of the noble estate and between a nobleman and a townsman or a churchman. A distinctive feature of that kind of courts was the hearing of the matters of the settled nobility only, the landholders. These courts gradually began to lose on their importance in the 17th century, which is bound up with the fall of the land sejmiks, which did not gather and did not elect judges nor fill the offices. The abuse of the liberum veto brought the paralysis of those sejmiks, and in consequence the nobility had no judges for the disposing of the cases pending before the land courts.

Meanwhile the unsettled nobility was subject to the jurisdiction of the second type of court — namely the municipal court. That type of judiciary had different competences, composition and manner of functioning, and its first beginnings are to be sought in the 14th century in Lesser Poland, where it supplemented the land courts functionally. They were held in the castles (in Lesser Poland at Kraków, Biecz and Sącz) and their territorial competence was close to that of the land courts, yet in its subject-matter that type of courts was proper to the civil and criminal matters of the unsettled nobility. With time the criminal jurisdiction of the municipal courts was also extended to the settled nobility, along with the moving away from the armed raid and the nobility's self-help as the chief way of the meting out of justice in the so-called four municipal articles: of arson, of the attack on a noble

[p. 233]

house, of the robbery on the public road and of the rape[^11]. The municipal court was led by the castle starost, and the adjudicating composition was filled out by the deputy starost, the municipal judge, the municipal scribe and an assessor. The technical and the order matters were secured by the usher. In the 17th century the land courts entered the period of stagnation and of fall, for the land sejms did not gather for long periods, which caused their role to be taken over gradually by the municipal courts, which judged continually, and through that they gradually took over the competences of the making of the entries in the perpetual books, which before had belonged only to the land courts. The sitting of the court was opened by the castle starost, the usher called the case and the parties were called upon to present their case and the proofs in its support. Here a field for display opened for the various substitutes in procedure, who with time grew ever more active as the law grew complicated. The judgment fallen after the bench's deliberation was written into the special books, of which more will be said below, at the discussion of the working of the court chancelleries and of their role in the practice of the administration of justice.

The third kind of the courts of first instance were the podkomorski courts, arisen in the 14th century in Lesser Poland and gradually formed in the remaining voivodeships, though one must grant that their subject-matter competence was limited only to the contentious and the non-contentious matters bearing on the fixing of the boundaries between the landed immovables of the nobility and between the nobility and the royal domains, whose significance and territorial range gradually diminish, along with the grants of the royal domains made by the rulers of Poland. The bench was presided over by the podkomorzy, who was the first in the hierarchy of the land offices after the senators, in accord with the sejm constitution of the year 1611, which formally ordered the sequence of the offices in the Crown.[^12] The court of the podkomorzy had the task of the hearing of the boundary disputes, which required the personal presence of the court in the field, for the marking out of the boundary and the settling of the dispute, and thereby excluded the recourse to the commissioners, the specialists, or a relying only on the documents on which the judgment was to rest. The criminal judiciary in the period after the unification of the Kingdom rested in the hands of the court of the oprawca, that is an official of the municipal court appointed by the starost, that is an organ coming from the royal nomination. In the period before the institution of the royal instygator had taken shape, he had the task of the pursuit of the crimes threatening the safety and the public order[^13]. An office with competences so shaped gathered in its hands the investigative and the jurisdictional acts: it established, pursued and confined the perpetrator of a crime. One cannot refuse that office an exceptional effectiveness and efficiency of action for the practice of the time in the field of the execution of the laws, as the numerous complaints of the nobility against the abuses of the oprawca point to — one who, in their belief, infringed their rights and liberties. It is a fact that the court of the oprawca had an efficient and a harsh investigative apparatus, and the cases pending were judged quickly. This did not however stop the noble brethren from the criticism of the oprawca and from the fierce combating of that judicial office, which led at the end of the 15th century to the liquidation of that institution. Reminiscences of its existence function to the present day through the pejorative meaning given to the word "oprawca" (the bailiff).

In the 15th century the wiec courts keep on further, one in each voivodeship, which were in a straight line the functional heirs of the district courts of the princes. The close of the 15th century, however, is already the decline of that kind of institution, and the competences pass to the sejm court, which adjudicates in the presence of the nobility under the presidency of the voivode. The judgments fall in the sejm courts in the king's name, who is the highest judge. The most important deeds of the high officials go precisely to the king's court, though to relieve the king, a practice takes shape that in his stead a dignitary indicated by him presided over the court. In the practice of the time of the functioning of the organs of the administration of justice it was accepted that the multiplicity of the types of court had the task of the relieving of the ruler of the personal judging of the matters, which the monarch had the right to take over always to his own cognisance. That competence engaged itself always at the moment when the ruler appeared in a given land; then all the courts stopped their activity and the judge, the sub-judge and the scribe took their place at the ruler's side, to serve him with their help in the giving of the rulings. # 3. The judicial offices

A discussion of the institutions of the administration of justice of the Noble Commonwealth will not be full without a discussion of the judicial offices. As is known, the efficient functioning of the administration of justice rests on two pillars: the rules and the personnel. The institutions discussed appear in all the courts of the Noble Commonwealth.

# 3.1. The professional judges

In the functional sense at the head of the judicial offices always stood the representative of the king in a given land, so that it might be the starost, the voivode or a commissioner. In the period of the monarchy the administration of justice was exercised in the name of the sovereign, who is the king. In the exercise of that function, which demanded a specialisation and with time also an ever greater knowledge, the Polish monarchs made use of the judges and the sub-judges they appointed. The judge thus became the president of the adjudicating composition in the name of the king and came from the royal nomination, filling his function for life. After the taking of the opinion of his advisers the king named a nobleman to the post of judge, from among the candidates chosen by the land sejmiks. It was required that the candidate be a landholder, settled in the given land, meeting the requirement of worthiness, so that he enjoyed the respect, the good opinion and the trust of the local noble brethren. Requirements so laid with respect to the judge were to give a warranty of the honest exercise of the duties and to help raise the prestige of the rulings made through him, which had no small significance given the weakness of the institutions of execution and the relying in that field on the general levy. The knowledge of the specific character of the given land and of the personal [relations] of the noblemen settled there had a great significance for the effectiveness of the judging. The judges of the noble courts performed their duties without a pay, which was bound up with the fairly common conviction that as an honorary function it was to bring prestige and respect. Where the judge nominated by the monarch could not fill his function, he had recourse to his substitute — the komornik, towards whom likewise the require-

# 3.2. The lay judges – the assessors

Beside the professional judges, in the compositions of the courts there sat the lay judges, who represented the interests of the nobility as the whole of a given land, called the assessors. They were noblemen of the given land, whom the professional judge chose for himself, so that they adjudicated together with him in the judicial composition. As a rule there were six of them in the composition; they had no legal education and rather advised the judge in the giving of the judgments. As we read in the literature, "a court assessor is a person who, either by virtue of an entitlement belonging to her, or of a calling by an empowered judicial authority, or of an office she holds, assists the judge or the tribunal in the acts of the adjudication, contentious and non-contentious, being at the same time a witness of the course of the trial"[^19]. Curiously, the assessors were standing and non-standing, and the former were recruited from among the noblemen holding the offices in the given land and appointed by the king. It is thus a second function in the judicial composition of the land court nominated by the ruler, while in the municipal courts there was no requirement of their appointment, though that practice began to fix itself[^20]. "The szlachta sat not only on their own land and municipal courts, but also on the town courts; on the other hand, on the noble courts there often sat as assessors townsmen, and not only the local officials, the burgrave, the wójt, the jurymen, but also ordinary townsmen, holding no office, and also Latin clergy and even Ruthenian. (…) This admission of the various strata and estates to a share in the court sittings was a proof of a high tolerance, and at the same time it strengthened the authority of the court and the trust in its justice, regardless of the differences of estate"[^21].

# 3.4. The court chancellery

The court chancelleries were kept by the scribe, who was responsible for their efficient functioning. A curiosity is that the fairly uniform practice of the keeping of the court books fixed itself on the Polish lands in the 15th century.

The court books were destined for the entries of every kind of judicial ruling of a criminal as well as of a civil nature. With the beginning of each year new books were opened, to ease the chancellery work and the use of the data contained in them. # 3.5. The usher

The technical and the order outfitting of the courts of the time was provided by the usher, who did not have to be a nobleman, which caused that in that role there appeared many people of peasant origin. The name of the office came from the function of the carrying of the documents of the princely chancelleries as a carter. They were nominated by the voivode, and in the later period by the sejmiks; they took the oath, after which their symbolic hair-cutting was done, which symbolised the submission to the power of the voivode, who performed that rite in person. # 3.6. The instygator

The noble judiciary formed the institution of the public prosecutor — the instygator[^29], called also the delator. His role was the representing of the general interest both in the criminal and in the civil processes, e.g. defending the interests of the nobility in the processes for damages of a given land, if the neighbourhood suffered through the march-through of troops. With the shaping of the practice, that the victim of the misdeeds should be represented and in some way recompensed, and the legal order infringed by that deed restored through a just punishment, the land instygators appear gradually in the judicial practice, though not at once do they gain the functions that in the modern times are assigned to the prosecutors. They appeared before all the kinds of courts, and were chosen from among the noble landholders, of an acknowledged social position at the land sejmik. In the 16th century the instygator appears as a central royal office — the Instigator Regni. The name of that office comes from the Latin word instigare — to accuse. By the title of the exercising of his tasks the land instygators took no pay, and their function, as the judicial one, was bound with the duty towards the community and tied itself with the prestige of the holding of the post.

# 3.7. The advocates

The defenders in the processes before the land courts were called the advocates, the jurists, the palestrants, the patrons. Appearing before the court, they had no legal education, and the knowledge of the procedure and of the material law they gained in the practice of the day everyday. They were most often

# 4. The judicial process

Let us look then at how the process before a land court of the age of the Noble Commonwealth looked, on the example of a criminal process. In that time a clear division into a criminal and a civil process had not yet taken shape, and so the procedural elements interweave for both types of the proceedings. A criminal case began with the bringing of a complaint by the wronged party himself, whom with time the instygator supported before the court. Then the court chancellery draws up the document called the summons, which was to be served on the settled nobility by the usher in writing, 14 days before the term of the hearing, while to the landless nobility it was announced orally. That important document is issued by the land scribe at the court, furnished with the royal and the land seal, in the Latin language and from the mid-16th century in the Polish language. The serving of the summons was attested by the deposition of the usher in the court book, which was held as the fact of an effective service. A frequent practice, however, was the ignoring of the summonses. The noble Arnold of Kościerzyna sued Mikołaj, the castellan of Bydgoszcz, before the land court at Nakło in a process "over the fled subjects", which was a rather frequent subject of the disputes between the nobility. "The court summoned lord Mikołaj, who did not stand at all, holding in contempt the summonses brought by the usher: 'the same usher, giving his account in the face of the court, attested publicly that he called the first, the second, the third and a fourth time over that lord Libijski of Złotów to stand and explain, which lord Mikołaj neither himself, nor through a substitute, stood forth and made no effort to explain'"[^32]. The court scribe entered the case in a separate book called the wokanda or the register, from which the successive cases were called. It was technically the role of the ushers, yet

The courts gathered in the rooms called the audience chambers, where at the chief place stood the table called the "tribunal", with a cross set on it. In the middle of the table sat the judge in the surroundings of the assessors. The parties entered the hall after the calling of the case. The standing forth of both the parties was obligatory, and the absence without an important cause was punished with a fine, called also the non-standing. The procedure of that time, from the statute of the year 1578, allowed the possibility of the putting off of the case, which was called the dylacje or the puttings-off, at most four times, with the taking of the next term as a fatal one. The judge opened the hearing, which went orally and openly and was made up of three stages. At the first term every question of organisation was considered, the puttings-off, the further terms were planned. In the second stage, the plaintiff proclaimed his summons, described his claims and cited the rules on which he based his pretensions. The defendant answered the claims of the plaintiff, raised his own arguments and declared whether he accepts the judicial summons or contradicts it. The outline of the most important claims of the parties was written into the court book by the scribe. The plaintiff could no longer withdraw his complaint from that moment, unless with the agreement of the opposite party. Then followed the third part of the process: namely the evidentiary proceedings, during which the parties presented their findings, called the witnesses or delivered before the adjudicating bench the material proofs. The processes were not divided into civil and criminal, and so both the elements of those processes in the present meaning interwove. In the evidentiary proceedings[^33] before the courts of the time the rule found its application that it was the parties who had to gather and themselves present every proof before the adjudicating bench. "The court did not seek the objective truth, but contented itself with the proofs, which made the formal basis for the giving of the ruling"[^34]. The so-called propinquity of the proof was applied, which consisted in the determination that the right of the priority in the carrying out of the proof belonged to the party higher in estate. The rulings given by the land courts were called the final rulings, from which, after the introduction of the institution of the appeal, one could appeal. The bringing of the means of appeal held back the execution of the punishment. The giving of the ruling itself, however, bound itself with a ceremonial, underlining the weight of the ruling. A curious description of the giving of a ruling adjudging the punishment of death is brought by the Lublin palestrant Jan Duklan Ochocki: "When the decree condemning to death was to be read, the judges covered their heads, the presiding one called louder — 'Open the doors! The punishments meted in the period of the Noble Commonwealth answered the conviction of the time of the necessity of the laying of the stress on the general prevention, which cannot wonder, for given the weakness of the execution of the laws, the individual prevention often proved illusory. The conviction of the exceptionality and the inviolability of the nobility caused that towards the representatives of that estate no tortures were applied during the skrutynium, and even from the heaviest misdeeds one could buy oneself out.

So Wacław Matuszewicz, after a quarrel, beat and shot with a pistol his adversary, the nobleman Parowiński, who soon died of the wounds taken. The companions of the dead man caught the perpetrator, to set him down to the castle, which guaranteed a quick administration of the punishment. The body of the dead man was delivered to the municipal court at Brześć as the chief proof in the case. Into the chancellery came the manifest of the instygator and the process moved. The perpetrator had, however, an experienced palestrant, who so tangled him in the case and taught him "how to stand in the court", that it was adjudged that Wacław Matuszewicz shall take the oath and cleanse himself of the accusation of a murder wilful. The starost took into the prison the perpetrators, whom the wronged had caught and delivered at once to his office. The prisoner sat in the arrest only as long as the interested wronged party or his family delivered the means for his keep. Curiously looked the practice of the execution of the rulings against immovables. The starost's service met enormous obstacles in the carrying out of such rulings, but the situation was easier all the same than in the case of the pursuing of the criminals condemned to death. The mighty noblemen disposed of a numerous service and let no representatives of the judicial powers into their estates. In such a situation the starost organised an armed raid on the estates of the disobedient convict. The whole nobility of the district had the duty of the delivering of the victuals and the support for such an expedition. The sejm statutes of the beginning of the 17th century already clearly laid such a duty on the nobility, foreseeing the punishment of 100 marks for its not being carried out. The person against whom such a raid was organised had the status of a banite, that is of one taken out from under the law, and so his murder went unpunished. The estates of such a person could be plundered, and after their taking the feasts lasted at the banite's cost. The life of such a nobleman was not at all easy, for one condemned by a judicial ruling to banition could be killed unpunished by anyone whom he offended, caught and given to the starost for the carrying out of the punishment. He was moreover deprived of honour, which at that time bound itself with a civil death. No self-respecting nobleman would give him his hand, nor wish to enter any relations. A person held for unhonourable, and such was an *infamis*, counted for nothing socially and had no right to stand at the sejmiks.

The court acts of the age deliver numerous examples of the people of noble birth troubling themselves with the common criminal misdeeds. A rather frequent practice was the meting out of the justice on one's own hand, not believing in the effective execution of the rulings fallen, which the condemned held in contempt and ignored. The merchant was taken, beaten, bound and stolen away in a stolen coach to Łaziński's village of Siniawy in the district of Sanok. The bad example from the elders passed to the youth, which despite the harsh upbringing and the moral and the religious rules inculcated at times demoralised itself too and entered the road of the criminal misdeed. After the return home he prepared his mother a hell. He carried out the furnishings and the clothes of his siblings, which he sold. To the household trying to oppose that behaviour the youthful offender threatened with a beating, swinging at the same time a knife and an axe. The noblewoman wrote in her complaint: "my own sister, standing personally before these present acts, he beat without any cause, made the marks of bruises, and drove all the people from the manor, and tried to set the manor on fire, the windows in the manor and in the village he knocked out, and he promises certainly to beat, to kill and to burn the manor and the village. The land judiciary in the 17th and the 18th century lived through a crisis because of the estate specificity of the participants appearing in the proceedings carried on before them. In that period the municipal courts play a greater role. Only in the year 1765 does the revival of the activity of the land courts begin, which compete effectively with the municipal courts[^43]. Stanisław Staszic criticised the doubleness of the courts with reason: "The municipal and the land courts are needless, so long as these have an equal power of the judging of the same matters and their rulings an equal force. If the Commonwealth does not distinguish the matters of the kind proper to each of these courts, it will act wisely and destroy the disinclinations — the quarrels between the land and the castle — when instead of these two courts it establishes one court permanent"[^44]. The reforms of the state carried out at the end of the 18th century abolished the division of the judicial organs. The Four-Year Sejm liquidated the land, the municipal, the podkomorski courts, introducing in their place the uniform noble courts, made up of 10 judges appointed for 4-year terms and adjudicating through the whole year[^45]. In this way the chapter in the history of the Polish judiciary closed — the period of the noble administration of justice.

# Judiciary of the nobility from the 15th to the 18th century

# Abstract

*The judiciary of the feudal monarchy naturally changed its character to the state one, which was associated with the emergence of social classes, especially the nobility, which, as a result of the ongoing political processes and social changes, obtained more and more privileges, including*

[p. 253]

*judicial immunities. This study will outline in general the judicial organs of the judiciary in the Republic of Poland from the 15th century until the end of Polish statehood. Naturally, these bodies were not uniform in terms of structure and organization, and their competences coincided. They evolved over time, although the fact is that these changes did not keep up with the changes taking place in the international environment around the First Polish Republic, and were not able to meet the needs of both state reforms and social changes. At a certain stage, they even became a burden in political reforms, contributing in some way to the ossification of the structures of the then Polish state and its collapse at the end of the 18th century. This period was characterized by a multitude of types of judgments, their cognition and territorial properties, which overlapped and yet did not constitute the uniform and coherent structure to which we are used these days. The subject of this study will be a discussion of these organs with an outline of their practice and a short description of the specificity of land law.*
