The Polish researcher H. Wiercieński, in a treatise on German immigration devoted to the Germans of Congress Poland, says the following:

> *"The German settlements on Polish soil are as old as Poland itself. Numerous names of places and castles bear witness to this; the history of the country, too, and that of many Polish families speaks of it. The German settlers were bound to the native economic circles by common economic interests. Marriage strengthened the mutual relationship. From this the adopted homeland gained many deserving citizens, who often stood out from the sameness of the mass of the people and who knew how to win names of historical importance for themselves and their descendants. To this day we find many bearers of German names among the outstanding minds of the country*[^1]*."*

Over the centuries German people streamed continually, with greater or lesser interruptions, into the lands beyond the Oder. Dr. Lück, in his epoch-making work "Deutsche Aufbaukräfte in der Entwicklung Polens" (German constructive forces in the development of Poland), has shed light on this German question of fate in the East and has often given us quite bitter insights. His research covers the whole territory of the Polish state and all the centuries of the Polish nation's existence as a state. My task is not drawn so wide in space and time. I want to show that the greater part of the Germans who immigrated into central Poland in the 17th, 18th and 19th centuries came from the neighbouring eastern provinces of Germany, the old ones and above all West Prussia and Posen, acquired later through the partitions. From there German farmers, craftsmen, teachers, pastors, scholars and artists streamed to the culturally undeveloped lands of the East, to find new places to work here and to do good German work.

[^1]: "Ekonomista", year 1908, vol. II

[p. 3]

Little preliminary work exists in this field. The inquiry must therefore be built on the *sources*. These come into question:

> 1. the church books and archives in the Lutheran congregations of the area in question, in the German superintendencies and in the Consistory at Warsaw;

> 2. the main features of the dialect (Wenker's sentences);

> 3. comparison of the first names and family names;

> 4. the way the houses are built, the way of farming;

> 5. hymn books and books of sermons;

> 6. folklore.

Sometimes the form of the village and of its fields allows a conclusion about the stock and the origin of the inhabitants. Beside these general sources there are special ones for particular groups of Germans. While for the village the *Schulzenlade* (the headman's chest of village papers) in most cases supplies first-class material on the question of origin, in the town its place is taken by the *Innungsladen* (the guild chests) of the German guilds, and often by old papers in the possession of families, passports, certificates and the like.

Questioning the old people succeeded only in the rarest cases. Among the country people, who have mostly lived for 200 to 300 years outside the borders of Germany among the surrounding Polish people (Umvolk), the memory of the old homeland has almost entirely vanished. A praiseworthy exception in this respect is the Swabians settled in the Prussian period, which is partly to be ascribed to their greater intelligence.

Mapping the places of origin found sometimes met great difficulties, especially for the small villages and hamlets. In many cases neither old nor new gazetteers helped. Old topographical maps sometimes gave excellent help, but so did newer maps on a large scale.

[p. 4]

In what follows, the origin of three groups of the Germans of central Poland, by their station, is to be proved:

> 1. the rural settlers;

> 2. the German burghers and the German craftsmen;

> 3. the Lutheran clergy, the German teachers and the members of the free professions.

The first *rural settlement* on the soil of central Poland is, to our present knowledge, the Vistula village of Słońsk, founded in 1605 and called Schlonske in the popular speech[^2], not far from the well-known salt-water spa of Ciechocinek. In quick succession a whole series of new foundations followed up the Vistula, such as Alt-Bogpomoż, Lentzen, Neu-Bogpomoż, Woluszewo. The Polish historian J. Baranowski says in his treatise "Wsie holenderskie na ziemiach polskich" (Dutch villages in the Polish lands):

> *"In this way at the beginning of the 17th century both banks between Thorn and Breslau [sic - probably meant is Leslau/Włocławek - JD] were sown with Dutch colonies*[^3]*."*

The settlers of these lowland villages, who are also called "Holländer" at Lentzen, at Wolluschewo and so on in the 17th-century church books of the Lutheran congregation at Thorn, came for the greater part from the Vistula villages around Thorn, Kulm, Graudenz and Schwetz. Schmidt says in his "Deutschtum des Landes Posen" (The Germans of the Posen country) that as early as about 1600 numerous Lutheran "Holländer" were settled below and above Thorn, in the villages of Gurske, Nessau, Silno and Dybow, because the strictly orthodox town government of Thorn did not like Mennonites as settlers. The Thorn lowland villages were mostly daughter settlements of the Holländereien (the Dutch-law settlements) lower down the Vistula, which had arisen about the middle of the 16th century. The inmates of the Holländereien were by no means Dutch by stock. In this respect we agree with Schmidt's view that real Dutchmen took part only in the first decades of the clearing of the Danzig Werder. In the course of time the

German farmers of the neighbouring marsh areas also took over the Dutch way of farming. So the name "Holländer" passed to them in the legal and technical sense.

According to the Thorn church books, a small number of settlers from eastern Pomerania, from the neighbourhood of Christenberg, Schifelbein, Kolberg and Latzken, also lived in the new Vistula villages.

A comparison of the present *family names* with those of the old Thorn church books convinces us of the likeness of the names; a proof that the Vistula Germans have long been settled. Names such as Leuschner, Heise, Milke, Kersten, Gollnig, Moltzahn, Netzel, Kröning, Raths and so on bear witness to this. After 1700 the description "Holländer" disappears from the Thorn church books; it is replaced by farmers' names from Slonske, Woluschewo and so on.

About 1740 settlement in the lowland villages began to quicken. One after another numerous villages arose, up to the mouth of the river Wieprz, 100 km south of Warsaw. Of the origin of these settlers we receive reliable information from the church books of the Lutheran congregations of *Gombin, Iłow, Plozk* and *Neuhof*. Most came from the surplus population of the old settlements between Thorn and Wlozlawek. But settlers from the lowland villages *below* Thorn, Bromberg and Graudenz also took part in the clearing of the Vistula meadows; some even came from East Prussia.

Going through five years of the registers of the congregation of *Gombin*, founded in 1832, gave 45 statements of origin for the lowland villages. Of these West Prussia had 30, the old Vistula villages of central Poland 15; in detail, the neighbourhood of Thorn 12, Slonske 9, Graudenz 5, Schwetz 4, Bogpomoż and Dembe 3, Bromberg 2, and Fordon, Neuenburg, Straßburg, Riesenburg, Mewe, Mehlsack, Braunsberg and Driesen on the Warthe one family each.

The church books of the Lutheran parish of *Iłow*, founded in 1775, contain for the period 1779 to 1840 the following 80 places of origin, which we list going up the Vistula: Danzig Werder, Baldrum near Marienwerder, Neuenburg, Montau 1 settler each, Graudenz 4, the Kulm lowland 2, Bromberg, Flötenau 1 each; from Neidenburg, Amt Schoden, Bergbruch, Neuenort, Altretz 1 settler each, Altendorf (3), the neighbourhood of Thorn (11), Nischewke 19, Deutsch-Glinne 9, Slonske 9, Neu-Ciechocinek (2), Woluschewo and Rybit (1), Bogpomoż 5, Witoschiner Lengde, Liciszewo near Lipno 1 family each.

[p. 6]

In the civil registers of the congregation at *Neuhof* (Nowydwór), which arose in 1780, we find for 1782 to 1806 the following places of origin: Danzig 1, Langenau 6, Bromberg 2, Straßburg in East Prussia 2, the neighbourhood of Rypin 5, the neighbourhood of Thorn 4, Slonske and Rybit 1 each, Bogpomoż and Osieker Lengden 2.

If we sum up the statements on the origin of the German farmers in the Vistula lowland, it follows that *95 in a hundred of the first settlers came from the Vistula country around Thorn and down the Vistula*. 5 in a hundred came from the Dobrzyń country (Dobriner Land), related by stock. The Vistula villages that arose after 1750 are mainly daughter settlements; they formed a widely branched, one might say, great family of one stock. Characteristic of the Vistula lowland, too, is the exclusive use of the *Thorn* and *Rogal* (Königsberg) *hymn books*. The Mennonites of our area, whose number has now greatly shrunk, came after 1760 mostly from the lowland south of Thorn.

The German country people of the *Dobrzyń* country, who began to immigrate about 1700, came from the neighbouring counties of West Prussia, and to a lesser part also of East Prussia. Part of the town population of Lipno and Rypin also came from the Netze district (Netzegau) and from the Reich.

The baptism, marriage and death registers of the congregation of *Rypin*, 1785 to 1854, give 127 statements of origin: of these Straßburg county 41 or 31 in a hundred, Graudenz county 11 or 8 in a hundred, Thorn county 8 statements, Briesen 7, Marienwerder and Kulm 4 each, Schwetz, Soldau, Bromberg, Osterode, Königsberg 3 each, Mohrungen, Mewe, Rosenberg in West Prussia, Pr.-Holland 2 each, Angerburg, Goldap, Darkehmen, Lyck, Gilgenburg, Kalminnen, Insterburg, Pr.-Eylau, Riesenburg, Johannisburg, Hammerstein, Wohlsin 1 statement each. The townspeople came from Konitz, Tuchel, Filehne, Nakel, Lobsens, Labischin, Schubin; from the Reich came craftsmen from Berlin and Hamburg, 2 each, and from Mühlhausen in Alsace, Stuttgart and Reichenbach in Silesia, 1 each.

The church books of the Lutheran congregation of *Lipno* in the Dobrzyń country give for the period 1799 to 1815 the following places of origin: Thorn county 5 persons, Mohrungen county 4, the counties of Graudenz, Labischin and Kolmar 2 each, Osterrode, Soldau, Briesen, Schwetz, Schubin and Konitz 1 each; 2 came from Saxony and one craftsman from Silesia to Lipno. Of 26 place names, then, 13 fall to the counties neighbouring the Dobrzyń country, much as in the parish of Rypin.

The dialect of the Dobrzyń country is also spoken in the Vistula lowland, around Danzig and in the western strip of present-day East Prussia. The folklore of the colo-

[p. 7]

nists leans strongly on these areas, and the family names agree too. Finally, here as there the Rogal and in part the Thorn hymn book was in use. Characteristic of the settlement history of the Dobrzyń country is the additional name "Räumungen" (clearings) for most German villages. It is also found in Straßburg county. All these facts speak for it that *the Germans of the Dobrzyń country come from neighbouring West Prussia* and the western counties of East Prussia.

Research into the origin of the Germans has gone furthest on the *Kuyavian lake plateau* (Kujawische Seenplatte). This was treated at length in Heft 2 of the "Deutsche Monatshefte in Polen". Therefore only a short summary is to be given here. The dialect, the family names, the Alt-Stettin hymn books and prayer books, the way the houses and barns are built, and the folklore point beyond doubt to eastern Pomerania as the country of origin. The place names from the church books of the Lutheran congregations of Sompolno, Babiak, Koło, Chodecz, Przedecz and Maślaki carry special weight as proof. The 529 places of origin found for the period 1800 to 1860 are divided among the counties of the former province of Posen as follows: Mogilno 129 (27 in a hundred), Strelno 89 (19 in a hundred), Obornik 40 (8.4 in a hundred), Kolmar 24 (5 in a hundred), Witkowo 21 (4.4 in a hundred), Schubin 20 (4.1 in a hundred), Hohensalza 17 (3.6 in a hundred), Posen 15 (3.1 in a hundred), Schroda 13 (2.7 in a hundred), Bromberg 12 (2.5 in a hundred), Znin 10, Wreschen 8, Wirsitz 7, Jarocin, Kempen, Filehne 4 each, Schmiegel, Samter and Adelnau 3 each, Wollstein, Lissa, Krotoschin, Grätz, Pleschen, Neu-Tomischel, Rawitsch, Ostrowo, Fraustadt 2 each, Kosten, Gostin, Czarnikau, Meseritz and Samotschin 1 place name each[^4]. By stock, *90 in a hundred fall to the Pomeranians*, 10 in a hundred to the Silesians. This proportion shifts still further in favour of the Pomeranians if we add the rural, and in part urban, settlers who came directly from eastern Pomerania. From Jastrow came 3, and 1 settler each from: Berlinchen, Pasewalk, Alt-Stettin, Flatau, Damlug in Pomerania, Stolp in Pomerania, Bublitz, Saba in Pomerania, Alt-Glebsow, Neugardt, Sandow, Gollnow, Hammerstein; further, one each from Tiefenau in Old Prussia, Perleberg in the Prignitz, Schwerin on the Warthe, Bachwinkel, Altstadt, Stieglitz near Grünfier and from Schneidemühl.

Into the *Kalisz country* (Kalischer Land) the Germans immigrated from the areas around Neutomischel, Kirchplatz Boruj, Grätz and the western Warthe marshes [Wartebruch]. Once again the

Silesian dialect, the way of farming (the growing of hops), the customs, the dress, the way the houses are built, the hymn books, mostly of Züllichau and Breslau, and the family names give a convincing pointer to Lower Silesia, in part to the Warthe marshes [Wartebruch]. Special importance in this case, too, falls to the registers of the Lutheran congregations of Zagorow, Grodziec, Stawiszyn, Turek and Władysławow, which contain statements of origin, though not always in the number one would wish. Some of the writers of the church books gave no information at all on the place of origin for years. Sometimes, too, in the death entries, the relatives did not know the place of birth; this was mostly the case with retired farmers (Ausgedinger).

Going through the marriage and death registers of the congregation of *Zagorow* from 1843 to 1847 gave 18 place names, divided as follows: Wollstein county 1, the western Warthe marshes, Lower Silesia 1, the Silesian dialect area west of Posen, in Posen county, 2; the Netze marshes 2, the counties east of Posen 2, from the rest of the Reich 5.

From the church books of the Lutheran congregation of *Grodziec* we take for the period 1797 to 1826 137 place names: the neighbourhood of Neutomischel county 36, Meseritz 3, Wollstein 5; the western Warthe marshes 20, the Oder marshes 3; Lower Silesia 18, the Silesian dialect area in Posen county 16, the Pomeranian dialect area in the same county 8, the Netze district 15, Pomerania 8, the rest of the Reich 5.

For the colonists of the congregation of *Stawischin* we found for the period 1780 to 1830 70 statements of place; of these Neutomischel county had 16, Meseritz 1, Wollstein 3; Lower Silesia 7, the Silesian dialect area in Posen county 5, the Pomeranian one in the same county 5; the western Warthe marshes 17, the Netze district 8, the rest of the Reich 8.

The church books of the congregation of *Turek* supply for 1837 to 1847 42 statements of place: Neutomischel county 6, Lower Silesia 14, Silesian dialect areas in Posen county 3, the Pomeranian area east of Posen 7, the Netze marshes 10, the Reich 2.

For *Władysławow*, the mother congregation of the Kalisz country, we found for 1775 to 1800 the following 15 statements of place: Neutomischel county 2, Lower Silesia 5, the Silesian dialect area in Posen county 3, the eastern, Pomeranian part of the same county 2, the Netze marshes 1, from Pomerania 2.

If we sum up the statements of origin for the Kalisz country, we obtain the following distribution: *Silesians* 54 in a hundred, *Pomeranians* 21 in a hundred, *people of the Mark* (Brandenburg) 12 in a hundred; *to the rest of the Reich fall* 10 in a hundred.

This provisional reckoning is unfair to the Silesians in so far as the statements of the congregations of Prażuchy and Sobiesęki near Kalisch are still missing; they are inhabited almost exclusively by Silesian farmers. The strong showing of the Pomeranians and the people of the Mark comes from the fact that our reckoning took in mainly the northern congregations, neighbouring the Pomeranian dialect. It is natural to suppose that in the parishes lying further south the number of Pomeranians and people of the Mark will be comparatively small.

With few exceptions all the German villages of the *Łódź industrial area* are daughter settlements, mainly of the German colonies on the Kuyavian lake plateau and in the Kalisz country. On the whole, in the Łódź area the two stocks of the Silesians and the Pomeranians balance each other. The Swabian villages make up a certain percentage only in the neighbourhood of Łódź and in the Gostynin country. In the *Gostynin country*, about 1800, the wholesale merchant v. Treskow settled farmers from the western Warthe marshes, from the neighbourhood of Landsberg on the Warthe and from Driesen on his extensive woods, as we have been able to establish from the Gostynin church books.

In conclusion we state: *the German rural population in central Poland comes in each case from the German areas neighbouring it to the west. In the great majority the German villages of central Poland are daughter settlements of the mother provinces of Silesia, Posen, West and East Prussia. This finding also holds for the German farmers of the Chełm and Lublin country, and likewise of Volhynia. For the settlers of these areas come, with few exceptions, from the German districts (Gaue) of central Poland.* They moved away from here between 1855 and 1885; many thousands of colonists from central Poland found their way overseas after 1890.

The *urban Germans of central Poland* look back on a history of a good 200 years. The immigrants of the 18th century, it is true, as shows most starkly in Warsaw, were lost in the host people; only small groups have kept their nationality (Volkstum), for example in the town of Władysławów, founded in 1738. As everywhere, the urban Germans who arose from single immigrants have here too a far smaller national (völkisch) power of resistance than the German farmers settled together. In the time of the Polish kings of the

[p. 10]

Saxon house, many officials, craftsmen and soldiers came from Saxony to Warsaw. The Treaty of Warsaw of 1768, which guaranteed freedom of belief to the Dissenters, brought a stronger stream of German craftsmen and members of the free professions. In 1781 the splendid Lutheran church in Warsaw was consecrated. Among the Germans of that time many came from the neighbouring Prussian areas. It would go beyond the bounds of a short lecture to list all the names. Some outstanding representatives shall nonetheless be named. The well-known coach-maker Thomas Michael *Dangel*, raised to the Polish nobility in 1792, came from Pasewalk in Pomerania. The outstanding Warsaw architect Ephraim von *Schröger* was born in Thorn; he too was ennobled, in 1775. Another important architect, Theodor Gottlieb *Seyfried*, was born in Buk near Posen. Of the forebears of the leading Polish booksellers' firm *Gebethner* and *Wolff*, Gebethner came in 1727 from Krotoschin and Wolff from Brätz. The family of the well-known Warsaw wholesale merchant *Herse* comes from Schwiebus. About 1776 the forebears of the Warsaw factory owners *Schlenker* came from Thorn. The dyer *Werner* in Warsaw, whose descendants now own extensive landed estates [Goßgrundbesitz] in the neighbourhood of Łęczyca, came from Obersitzko. The list could, as I said, be continued at will.

Let us now turn to the urban Germans who arose from settlement in closed groups. The second *German town settlement* after Władysławów, which the Germans called Rostarschütz, is the small craftsmen's town of *Neuhof*, laid out in 1782 by the Archbishop of Gniezno. According to the church books of the local Lutheran congregation, the cloth-makers came from Schwersenz, Czarnikau, Schönlanke, Bütow in Pomerania, Braunsberg [Brausnsberg] in Silesia, Fraustadt, Neudamm and Gilgenburg in East Prussia. The stocking-knitters, shoemakers, locksmiths and tailors came from Sonnenburg, Jastrow, Pasewalk, Johannisburg in East Prussia, Löbau, Thorn, Straßburg in West Prussia, Bischofswerder and from Ohlau in Silesia, all of them, then, from the neighbouring Prussian provinces.

When, by the second and third partitions of Poland, considerable territories of the Polish Republic fell to Prussia, German craftsmen and merchants streamed into the depopulated Polish towns together with the Prussian officials. A fine example of this is the voivodeship town of *Kalisch*, which had fallen victim to a terrible fire in 1793. The first concern of the Prussian authorities was the rebuilding of the town, which lay well placed for trade and administration. From the neighbouring areas of Silesia numerous building craftsmen immigrated, who within a few years raised the town out of rubble and

[p. 11]

ruins. As early as 1795 the Prussian authorities installed the first Lutheran preacher, Karl Michael Christian *Sinell*, from Stolzenhagen in Pomerania. From his entries of the couples married in the period 1795 to 1815 we take the following places of origin: of 298, the Silesian dialect area has 192, the Pomeranian dialect area 36, East Prussia 14, the rest of the Reich 36. That gives 69.3 per cent Silesians, 13 per cent Pomeranians, 5 per cent East Prussians and 13 per cent from the rest of the Reich. Among the Silesian towns Zduny stands first with 30 immigrants; there follow Breslau with 14, Ostrowo 12, Groß-Wartenberg 11, Rawitsch and Militsch 7 each, Kempen, Suhlau and Namslau 5, Festenberg, Krotoschin, Guhrau and Oels 4. From Bromberg came 8 persons, from Saxony 20, from Württemberg 8. By trade there were: bakers 17, joiners and masons 15 each, tailors 12, carpenters 9, hatters and shoemakers 8 each, windmillers and coachmen 6 each, merchants 5, saddlers and butchers 4 each, doctors, teachers, printers and soap-boilers 3 each, hairdressers, glaziers, girdlers, rope-makers, innkeepers, turners, confectioners, gardeners, wheelwrights and coopers 2 each. Of trades certainly new to the town life of that time may be named: a maker of sealing wax, a glove-maker, a stuff-maker, a fulling-miller, a maker of musical instruments, a bookbinder, a dyer, a furrier.

If the border town of Kalisch naturally received the strongest increase of German settlers from the neighbouring Silesian areas, at the county town of *Gostynin*, in the north of Congress Poland on the left bank of the Vistula, we find above all an inflow from the neighbouring Low German area. Here cloth-makers were settled from 1821 to 1828. Beside the old town a new cloth-makers' settlement grew up. On the strength of the notes of origin in the church books of the Lutheran congregation at Gostynin for the period 1825 to 1850, and of the records in the happily preserved guild books of the German cloth-makers' guild, it follows: of 88 statements of place, 68 or 78 in a hundred fall to the Netze district, the remaining 22 in a hundred to the Silesian areas. From the separate cloth-making towns of the Netze district there came: Radolin 15, Samotschin 13, Labischin 11, Schönlanke 9, neighbouring Jastrow 7, Margonin 4, Zielenzig 4, Lobsens and Rogasen 3 each, Wirsitz, Althütte, Kolmar, Ritschenwalde 1 each. From Bojanowo, Rawitsch, Breslau, Schwiebus, Schwersentz, Palmburg, Neudeck, Landeck and Schulitz came one cloth-maker each. In the first German cloth-makers' town in central Poland, in *Dombie*, 61 per cent of the cloth-makers came from the Netze district, 39 per cent from Silesia.

The statements on the origin of the cloth-makers in *Zgierz, Alexandrow* and *Blendow* are in my contribution to Lück's book "Die Aufbaukräfte". The en-

[p. 12]

tries in the church books of the congregations of Zduńska Wola, Konstantynow, Brzeziny and Pabjanice give, on the whole, the same picture. In the *northern* towns of central Poland the cloth-makers from the Netze district are in the majority, in those lying further *south* those from Silesia. On the strength of the 685 place names gathered so far, the statements from Ozorkow and in part from Tomaschow being unfortunately still missing, it follows that *66 in a hundred of the immigrant cloth-makers came from Silesia and 34 in a hundred from the Netze district*.

With the cloth-makers, German craftsmen of every kind also appeared in the newly laid-out cloth-making towns. Manifold personal and economic ties meant that they left their old home towns at the same time and sought new places of work together.

In the time that followed a strong regrouping took place in some cloth-making towns. Many have lost all their inhabitants by migration to the great industrial centres of the Łódź area, such as Dombie, Przedecz, Chodecz and Poddębice. The surrounding German villages, too, gave up their surplus population to the rising industrial towns. Their make-up by stock was thereby hardly changed much, since west and south of Łódź, Pabjanice and Zgierz the village population is mainly Silesian, while east of Łódź and south of Ozorkow live farmers of Pomeranian stock. New elements among the urban Germans are the proletarianised *Swabians*, who immigrated from the colonies founded in the Prussian period and in the decades after. Some towns, especially Tomaschow and Łódź, also have immigrants from *Bielitz* and Biala, mostly cloth-makers and weavers. So the Tomaschow cloth manufacturer Piesch is a man of Bielitz, and others.

Now something more about the origin of the *Lutheran clergy* in central Poland. The first German Lutheran congregation in central Poland is *Wengrow*, founded in 1650 by Prince Radziwiłł, who was himself a Protestant. A series of pastors from Greater Poland, such as *Tyräus, Rosentreter, Bachstrom*, served this congregation, lying lonely and much cut off, in the first century of its existence.

After the death of the first pastor of the newly founded *Warsaw* congregation, Johann Jakob *Scheidemantel*, a Silesian by birth, Pastor Gottlieb *Ringeltaube* was elected; he came from Gramtschen near Thorn, where his father was the Lutheran preacher. The afternoon preacher Friedrich *Cerulli*, whom the congregation had elected in 1779, came from Königsberg.

[p. 13]

Particular credit for the growth of the Lutheran congregation of Warsaw was earned by Pastor Karl Heinrich Wilhelm *Schmidt*, in office from 1790 to 1813, who was born in Karge. For many years he was guardian of the German Lutheran school in Warsaw and president of the Consistory. His greatest merit, however, is that in 1803 he published the *first hymn book* for the Lutheran church in the Kingdom of Poland, whose improved edition appeared only 40 years later. From the Polish national hero Th. Kościuszko Pastor Schmidt received a letter in his own hand for exemplary care of the sick and truly patriotic feeling, which he had shown in the war year 1795.

The later superintendent and pastor of the Warsaw congregation, Karl *Lauber*, whom the South Prussian consistory installed as preacher in 1803, came from Schmiegel; he was the eldest son of the pastor there. The first pastor of the congregation of Michalke, founded in 1779, Albert *Bocianowski*, was born in Lötzen in East Prussia. The first pastor of the congregation of Neuhof, founded in 1775, Gottfried *Leske*, came from Rastenburg in East Prussia; he was also active as a writer. The pastor of the congregation of Stawischin, Karl *Callmann*, was born in Bojanowo and died in Stawischin in 1794.

In the Prussian period eleven new Lutheran congregations arose in central Poland. In the mother congregation of the Vistula lowland, in Iłow, Pastor *Wittold*, born in Schadewalde near Marienburg, held office. In the congregation of Grodziec [Grodzierc] the first pastor, *Bergemann*, came from Fraustadt, where his father was a cloth manufacturer. Pastor *Herzberg* in Kalisch came from Schweidnitz. Pastor *Grimm* in Stawiszyn was born in Großstrelitz in Silesia.

In the time of the Duchy of Warsaw there immigrated: Pastor *Gieser* in Babiak, born at Wronke; Pastor *Hewelke*, later superintendent of the Płock diocese, an uncommonly able organiser of the parishes under him. On his gravestone is to be read:

> *"Death is not death, only the ennobling of mortal nature. Resting place of Johann Karl Hewelke, pastor of the Lutheran congregation at Plozk, superintendent of the Plozk diocese, born at Thorn in the year 1771, died at Plozk 1836."*

The long-serving pastor and president of the Reformed consistory in Warsaw, the court preacher Karl *Diehl*, was born in Lissa in 1765. The deserving pastor of Władysławów, the mother congregation in the Kalisz country, Karl Gottlieb *Bartsch*, who held office in that congregation from 1810 to 1852, came from Michelsdorf in Silesia.

[p. 14]

The immigration of German Lutheran cloth-makers, weavers and other "Professionisten" (tradesmen) between 1818 and 1828, which led to the founding of eleven Lutheran parishes, also brought a number of Lutheran clergymen into the country, in most cases young candidates of theology. Of the pastors appointed at that time, seven called the Silesian lands of their stock their home: Pastor *Benni* from Lissa, whose descendants were well-known linguists, *Biedermann* from Zduny, *Gerbling* and *Rother* from Breslau, *Hayn* from Oels, *Rüdiger* from Schawone and *Sachs* from Rawitsch. The fathers of some were cloth-makers. Pastors *Modl*, *Berkau* and *Jaeckel* were from Posen, Thorn and Filehne respectively. Of the pastors who came into the country after 1830: *Stiller* from Rawitsch [Rawisch], a cloth-maker's son, *Wilhelm* from Birnbaum, a pastor's son, *Jeute* from Bojanowo, a cloth-maker's son, *Kattein* from Beuthen, a burgher's son, *Hinz* from Wilhelmsburg in East Prussia, where his father was superintendent, *Sray* from Postelwitz in Silesia. After 1840 these were newly appointed: Pastor *Häßner*, born in Hermersdorf near Görlitz, *Jarnecki* from Klein-Ellgut near Oels; *Cotta* from Winzig in Silesia; *Modl* from Herrnstadt in Silesia; *Menzmann* from Langenau in Prussia; *Kruschwitz* from Jaulitz in Lusatia. Pastor *Lemon* was born in Posen, the son of a privy councillor; he is the last in time of the pastors who immigrated into Poland from the neighbouring Prussian areas. He worked in Ozorkow from 1880 to 1910.

After 1850 sons of the native German burghers and craftsmen were already choosing to study theology. In the last decades of the past century the Russian government made insuperable difficulties over confirming foreign pastors. In the Warsaw Lutheran consistorial district there was a great shortage of clergymen before the World War, which can be regarded as remedied only today, now that the Warsaw theological faculty has been working at full stretch.

In sum we state that in the period 1780 to 1850 41 pastors immigrated into central Poland from Silesia and the Posen country. If we add the pastors whose fathers came into the country as Prussian officials, namely Schwarz, Hermes, Lembke and Börner, the number comes to 45. To these brave German Lutheran men the founding and building up of the Lutheran church and school system is owed without reserve. This, unfortunately, is all too often knowingly passed over in silence by the present Lutheran clergy.

[p. 15]

Beside the clergyman, German elementary schoolteachers came into the newly arisen towns and villages, for the greater part from the neighbouring German provinces. Numerous school lists in the church archives bear witness to this.

After 1870 the stream of teachers from Germany ebbed away completely, since in 1866 the Russian authorities in Warsaw had opened a teachers' seminary, German at first and completely Russified after a few years.

It should not go unmentioned that the creator of the Polish dictionary, Samuel Gottlieb *Linde*, for many years director of the Royal Lyceum at Warsaw, founded by the Prussian authorities in 1805 as a model school, was born in Thorn in 1771. The music teacher of the well-known Polish composer Moniuszko was Joseph Xawer Elsner, born in Grottkau in Silesia. The outstanding Warsaw doctor Aug. Ferdin. Wolff, for whose fiftieth year of service the Polish doctors' association had a medal struck, was born in Lissa in 1768. Similar examples are listed in numbers in Lück's book.

With that I am at the end of my report. It is a first attempt to work new ground in scholarship. A great part of the work, admittedly, remains to be done. On the strength of what has been done so far, it may be estimated that of the 325,000 Germans in central Poland, the 20,000 of the Chełm and Lublin country and the 50,000 of Volhynia, at least 85 in a hundred immigrated over a good 300 years from the neighbouring Prussian provinces. In the old Polish time these settlers were already Germans abroad (Auslanddeutsche), who had lived for *centuries* in the western areas of Poland, had faithfully kept their nationality, and kept up schools and churches from their own means.

If the migration of the rural settlers went on quietly, unnoticed and evenly, that cannot be said of the immigration of the cloth-makers. The removal of 10,000 families to Congress Poland was a terrible bloodletting for the German border areas, from which they have hardly been able to recover. The empty houses in the former cloth-making towns had to be pulled down by order of the police, in Rawitsch for example some 55, as H. Barten reports in his work "Siedlung in Südwestposen" (Settlement in south-western Posen). But this by the way.

The guiding idea of our work is to establish as exactly as possible the make-up of the Germans by stock. Only this sure foundation makes possible, taking into account the very different colonising abilities, strengths and weaknesses of the individual stocks, a full understanding of the historical development and of the prospects of the Germans of central Poland.
