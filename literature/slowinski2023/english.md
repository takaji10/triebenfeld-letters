## Introduction

In October 2020 there came into my hands a very well-preserved collection of letters by a Wehrmacht soldier, discovered in the wall during the renovation of the former house of the cantor Gustav Mielke at Łazińsk. The successive letters, as they were translated, proved to be increasingly fascinating reading – a source of precious information, conveyed from the position of a private soldier, about little-known events and fighting on the northern wing of the Eastern Front and about the everyday life of the soldier's family in the Kraj Warty. This brief introduction should help to situate this extraordinary story in place and time.

◼

In the year 1718, fourteen German families arrive from Silesia in eastern Wielkopolska, in the vicinity of the town of Zagórów situated on the southern slope of the Warta river valley; the lord of the manor of nearby Trąbczyn leases them in perpetuity forest lands and fallows, where in time a village comes into being by the name of Łazińsk. Thus begins the history of the German settlers on this small land. Apparently the first experiences proved positive, for in 1746 the owners of the estates in Trąbczyn, Katarzyna and Paweł Prusińscy, designate places for further settlements. The newcomers take possession of the leased land under Olęder law (prawo olęderskie). They are Evangelicals (Lutherans), free people, with their own self-government, a cult of work and strict customs. It may be of interest that in the last of the villages located 
<!-- s. 8 [PDF 5] -->
 by the heirs of Trąbczyn there was also a group of Polish peasants – Catholics.

In the years 1746–1789 the heirs of neighbouring Łukomie likewise mark out new locations. The population of these villages was in its great majority of German origin. The name Olędrzy, used for a long time in the Zagórów region, was not connected with the nationality of the settlers, but with the law on the basis of which the villages were located and relations with the landowners were regulated. This law was brought into the Rzeczpospolita [the Polish Commonwealth] by Mennonites arriving from the Netherlands, who were fleeing their own homeland from persecution. The Warsaw Confederation, adopted by the Polish Sejm in January 1573, was an important act which secured for the nobility freedom of worship and guaranteed peace between religious dissenters. It was a legal guarantee of religious tolerance. The Mennonites were not a numerous group that had reached the Vistula and the Żuławy, but the law proved very useful in organizing the settlement drive and was applied for a long time – until the mid-nineteenth century. Zbigniew Chodyła, a meritorious researcher and expert on Olęder settlement, assesses that the Dutch and their descendants among the settlers located under Olęder law constituted merely about one per cent of the population. The rest were in their great majority Germans – Evangelicals, and also Poles – Catholics and Protestants. According to the research of the aforementioned Zbigniew Chodyła, in the whole of Wielkopolska, from 1721 to the years 1793/1795, 637 settlements were founded, of which in the Kalisz voivodeship – to which Zagórów belonged – 228[^1].

Peasants arriving from Prussia in the vicinity of Zagórów settle in the surrounding villages, which form a kind of ring around the town. The village of Łazińsk was the oldest and largest settlement inhabited by the German-speaking population. After the Congress of Vienna in 1815, Zagórów and the surrounding lands found

The settling Germans, as well as the Jews, lived for long years in the world of their own language (Yiddish and German) as well as of their religion. The partitioning powers regulated the social order according to their own injunctions and statutes – yet the cultural distinctness and the special legal status remained.

Contacts between Poles, Jews and Germans until the mid-nineteenth century were almost exclusively of a commercial, economic character. Also in later years it is practically impossible to note (apart from entirely incidental cases) mixed marriages: Polish–Jewish, Polish–German or Jewish–German. Nor are there cases of conversion. Russian rule lasted over a hundred years, but in none of the communities: the Polish, the Jewish or the German, are there testimonies of conversion to the faith of the rulers – Orthodoxy.

What was for the Jews and the Germans – and did it mean anything at all – the idea of a free Poland, nurtured through generations? Both German and Jewish children underwent a short education tied strictly to the religion they professed. For the German Evangelicals it was the Bible, for the Jewish children the Talmud. Mastery of the basic skills of reading and writing, of arithmetic, and knowledge of the holy books formed the foundations of this education. Setting aside the harsh regime of the partitioners' law, from the sources mentioned, even from their own teachers they could not obtain basic knowledge of the history of the country in which they lived.

When independence was regained in 1918, wealthy Jewish and German families, as well as Protestant clergymen coming from the Reich, began to leave Poznań and Wielkopolska en masse for Germany. Throughout the century the Jewish and Evangelical communities had been subordinate to the Tsarist administration. Successive generations of Poles, Jews and Germans created that community which, regardless of periodic tensions and collapses, slowly opened up to "strangers" and increasingly often was able to communicate, to build together – the best proof of which was the survival and the wise management of the Zagórów community in the times of the severe crisis of the 1930s. The cataclysm of the Second World War brutally destroyed that world. The war, its fatal consequences and the inexorably flowing time caused this old, yet close-to-us history to be slowly obscured by a "cloud of oblivion".

When we browse the pages of the internet records, we in fact have insight only into the times of the Second World War. The scholarly literature is exceptionally modest. I therefore thought that we would never come closer to sketching a fuller picture of the history of the town and of this land if we did not try to learn something more about those who lived here over two hundred years ago and whose traces time is erasing. Despite the whole tragic period of the years 1939–1945, one must remember that this is only a part of the history of this land and of many of its inhabitants, which one should attempt to tell. The German settlers located under Olęder law in the villages and forest settlements surrounding the town of Zagórów constituted a closed community which, despite two hundred years of settlement on these lands, was not susceptible to assimilation. A strong and probably decisive binding agent was the Lutheran religion and the German language. All were free people, whose relations with the landowners, who leased out for use grounds of meagre quality, were regulated by separate laws, customs and self-government. Such was the case in the eighteenth-century Rzeczpospolita, under the partitions and in the reborn Poland. As one of the inhabitants of those days in Zagórów recalls – Professor of the Poznań university Lech Słowiński: "In many villages right up to the year 1939 one heard only the German language. It must nevertheless be admitted that until that time the coexistence between the Polish and the German population was on the whole correct"[^5].

The largest settlement in this ring of homesteads was Łazińsk – situated eight kilometres to the south of Zagórów. To this day it is a place that perfectly illustrates the manner of organizing, under Olęder law, the space entrusted to the settlers: the location of the farmsteads, the building of roads and of drainage

Bogdan Karaszewski decided with his wife that the flat allocated to them required a considerable renovation. Possessing numerous skills that come in handy in such work, in 1995 he set about the job. The scope of these works must have been serious, since even the interior doors qualified for replacement. Removing a door together with its door frame – in such a way that the ceiling does not fall on one's head – requires a certain experience. The operation succeeded, and the extracted, rotted-through door frame revealed an unexpected sight. It turned out that this partition between rooms, which everyone had considered a single, monolithic wall, had been built of two walls, between which there is a considerable space. When the dust had settled, there appeared before Bogdan Karaszewski a sizable pile of papers sealed up tightly. Someone had secured them in an extraordinarily careful manner and hidden them cleverly. The first package extracted contained many letters bearing the postmarks of the Wehrmacht field post as well as a Book of Baptisms (Księga chrztów). Further down lay wrapped-up coverings of church altars and carpentry tools. When the find had been taken out, from the narrow crevice, into the empty space, books began to slide down from above… This extraordinary collection of books "descending" downwards was opened by a biography of Joseph Goebbels. In all, thirty-one volumes came into Karaszewski's hands. Apart from three gnawed at and damaged, probably by mice, the majority of the books survived in good condition, with the mark of time visible on the covers or on some of the pages.

In his free moments, the finder tried to interest various people and institutions in the materials he had discovered, but without much success. One must view with great respect the way Bogdan Karaszewski handled what he had extracted from the wall. Everything was packed

An announcement posted on the internet seeking materials for writing the history of the Zagórów Lutherans, former settlers from Germany, did not escape Karaszewski's attention. On some October day in 2020 he walked into the Zagórów Community Center and, into the hands of its director Łukasz Parus, handed over the entire collection, untouched by the years.

It is the end of the year 1944. As the east wind blows, one can hear the approaching front, the sounds of artillery salvos. Despite the assurances coming from Berlin radio, it was known that the entry of the Russians was a matter of weeks. We do not know which members of the Mielke family were in the house at that time (from various indirect information one may surmise that it was one of the older sisters, Leokadia). Preparing the hiding place in the crevice between the walls required time, technical skills, and ingenuity. The cache would surely never have been discovered had it not been for the decision to replace the doors. Most likely, the items being hidden were lowered in from above, from the attic, and then the entire crevice was carefully sealed up.

The packed collection contained, in its excellent part, letters from the front from the younger son of Julius Samuel Mielke – Bruno. But there were also letters and cards written by the father, the brother Gustav, the sisters Leokadia (Kadzia), Anni, and Olga to Bruno. Besides this, there were many loose letters, postcards, and documents from the years 1936–1939, such as, for example, a permit from the Konin starosta dated September 26, 1939 (!) for Gustav Adolf Mielke to move freely around the Konin county. He was also permitted to use a bicycle. From later reading one can infer that the siblings exchanged photographs, including those from the front. None of the mentioned photographs was in the hiding place. More interesting is the question of how, among these documents, there came to be letters from the father and siblings sent to the eastern front? Did Bruno leave them in Łazińsk while on leave? It is worth returning once more to the political part of the library collection and devoting a few words to Goebbels's biographer. Wilfrid Alfred Karl Bade (born 1906) belonged to the very active figures in Nazi propaganda. He rose to the position of government counsel in the Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda of the Third Reich. He was one of the leading lecturers and "creative interpreters" of the thought and ideas of the führer and his superior Goebbels. The Germans wanted something more. The main objective of the operation was the port of Murmansk and, first of all, cutting the route between Murmansk and Petrozavodsk and Leningrad. It is worth pausing for a moment over this last sentence. How is it to be understood? As the pride of a soldier who is told that he is fighting for a homeland free of Jews and Russian hordes? Or perhaps in this "feeling" there flows from somewhere in the subconscious the question of what, in truth, he is taking part in? He does not write about the war operations – for it is obvious that the censorship would never let such a letter through. But he reveals another enemy – nature, which in his account is more dangerous than the opponent. Or perhaps he indicates that this opponent has an extraordinarily dangerous ally? The question will always arise when reading letters from the front. To what extent should they be understood literally, and to what extent should one read what is not there or what has been "said between the lines"? In any case it is evident that Private Mielke has not yet come to know the true war machine that grinds human flesh, and with growing apprehension he awaits that encounter.

This letter is – to use the language of war – loaded with emotions. Above all he writes about his great dream, of which he thinks unceasingly. "…if I return happily and in good health, I will prepare myself for the most beautiful of professions, in order to exercise it with the greatest blessing […] My heart in any case is full of longing for a life dedicated to God – and as a pastor I know how to do that best." His thoughts also run to his future wife, who must hold views such as his. That is the first condition, about which he will ask her first of all. Does she belong to the Saviour and is she pious? Married life, as he imagines it, must be "the holiness of the Lord and blessed by the nearness of God and the Spirit".

He gives the same advice to his brother, who in a letter asks his opinion about Truthilde. "Observe her and converse with her thoroughly, how far she is sanctified by Christ." Brother Gustav is 43 years old (he had been cantor, teacher and headmaster at Łazińsk since 1925) and, as one can gather from other letters, the search for a candidate for wife has been going on with varying success for quite some time. Yet the advice of the younger Bruno is unchanging – he must know whether 
<!-- s. 45 [PDF 40] -->
 she is an inwardly seeking, deeply believing soul. It would also be good if she were not bad-looking.

His thoughts return to his school days at Łazińsk, which were the sunniest and happiest of all the periods of his life. With gratitude he thinks of his parents, who despite their weaknesses gave him what is most costly: "a healthy body and blood with the faith of our dear Evangelical Church", and finally of his wonderful siblings, from whose hearts beats only warm love. In the face of what is inevitably coming, we read a letter that is like Bruno Mielke's life credo and an attempt at an examination of conscience. It is also – or perhaps above all – an unspoken, strong reaction to the news reaching them from soldiers returning from the front line.

Letters from loved ones, such as those quoted earlier and the next one from Anna (of 22 September 1942), which I quote below, undoubtedly carried weight and significance in maintaining the psychological stability and resilience of Private Mielke. "I am so proud that you are fighting in this decisive battle. […] I believe that when a man like you stands at the front and looks danger straight in the eye, the petty and insignificant things flee from the soul, and it is then cleansed, and afterwards it becomes strengthened and proud. When you return, you will certainly be a different man. Take hold of everything that is valuable, and rid yourself of everything else. Conquer yourself, and in this way you will return as a man of worth, and nothing will be able to shake you. I would so much like to shield you from all troubles, but such is the lot of man that he must go through everything quite alone." Perhaps this girl, writing her letter shortly before nightfall, intuitively sensed a certain painful paradox that many soldiers experienced. They fought together, in a squad, in a company. On how they supported one another their lives often depended, and yet, on the other hand, an equally dangerous enemy of theirs was the grim and oppressive feeling of loneliness.

The letters dated in December carry the added line: Eastern Front. "Who of us soldiers from the wastelands of Russia would not want to be with his nearest and dearest at Christmas!" "You must surely think – he writes on 6 December – that I will be spending the holidays in sadness and despondency. It is God's will, and my superiors sent me to the front. Resisting this in any form would be resisting the will of God and the authorities." Bruno regards himself as a messenger "for my family in the east, and for her and for my people I want to offer up this small sacrifice and spend Christmas at the front". He honestly admits that he has landed in a good spot – it is a fairly quiet sector. The Russians have so far attacked very rarely – hardly at all. His service consists of standing 6–8 hours of sentry duty (in pairs), bringing food and firewood, and sometimes other things. Apart from that he sleeps, and if there is good light, he writes letters and reads the delayed newspapers. That is why he asks his brother not to submit an application for his release. "First of all, it would do no good; secondly, at the front it is better in winter. In the rear there is training, drills and so on. There is none of that here. It is dangerous, true – but it is calm, and I firmly believe that nothing will happen to me."

Mielke had a great deal of luck… At Stalingrad, a deadly struggle had been raging since the end of August, and by December 1942 it was entering its decisive phase. But of this – silence in Goebbels's 
<!-- s. 47 [PDF 42] -->
 propaganda. Bruno asks his brother to send him the Führer's address of 1 October, along with pages torn from other newspapers with the speeches of Goebbels, Göring and Ribbentrop. In the five-month battle, one and a half million German soldiers and their allies perished. Of the 91,000 prisoners taken in the Stalingrad cauldron, only about six thousand returned. But what news could this soldier have read in the "delayed newspapers"? Unaware of the westward turn of the wheel of history, he plans to establish numerous pen-pal contacts with potential candidates for a wife, so that after a life at the front full of unrest and renunciation he might look about for a cosy home. "It will be beautiful when I return home after the victory and, with a sense of peace, can lead a quiet life."

In December Bruno Mielke wrote a great deal. The approaching Christmas brought back memories of the family home, of his sisters, his brother, his father, and of his mother who was no longer alive (she died in 1933). "I want to write so that you especially, during the Christmas season, receive more mail from me." His first joy was the three-kilogram parcel received from home, and in it a fir wreath with a little angel's head and tinsel. "I immediately fastened it to my little cupboard, made from an empty grenade crate with two boards fitted in. Several comrades who 'happened' to find themselves nearby and were gazing with longing eyes at the holiday cake being taken out were given a bite to taste. The letter of 14 December also contains the information that soldiers who have no families should send stamps to the local party leaders, and these will pass them on to the Women's Leagues, which will prepare holiday parcels for the men at the front. The Wehrmacht, too, saw to it that every soldier received a richly stocked parcel, in which, alongside snuff, cigarettes, sweets, chocolate and darning wool, there were also envelopes for letters, a comb, and a bottle of vodka and of wine. Further on there is a paragraph

"On Saturday I washed myself, rubbed the ointment all over, and put on fresh underwear. Now no louse bites me, so the ointment works." The question is, for how long. This letter also contains a description of the prescribed daily order, or rather the day-and-night order. "We now have 6 hours of sentry duty within 24 hours – that is still bearable. Because at night we must be more on the alert and more watchful than during the day. But when day breaks, there is nothing to fear, and then our sleeping time begins." And the most important, joyful news. After barely eight months of service, as of 1 December 1942 he was promoted to Senior Private. The short letter that Bruno writes on Sunday 20 December 1942 (it reaches Łazińsk on 5 January) announces the sending of a longer description of how he spent Christmas, and contains only one account: "During the hours of the service, from 9 to 11, I stood on duty at my post, which is the more dangerous one (today we had to make a change). Mielke sits down to the account of the first day of the holidays just before midnight, right after coming off the post where, between 9 and 11 p.m., he had stood with his good comrade Schwartz. He honestly admits that he had feared these holidays and an onslaught of longing. But it was not so bad. In the forenoon the captain and the divisional chaplain came on a visit. They even walked up to his post, and the chaplain was so kind that he asked after his family and gave him a bar of chocolate, wishing him a Happy New Year. It was not so bad, either, because "the joyful strength is given me above all by my faith in the living God, who is with me […]". The brother's letter has a completely different character and weight. Everyone is living in anticipation of Anna's planned wedding. Bruno hopes that it will not stop at this one wedding. It is worth paying attention to the dense correspondence which Bruno Mielke conducts. And it is not merely his "privilege". The OKW – the High Command of the Wehrmacht – attached great importance to the smooth functioning of the field post. It was believed that frequent contact of the soldiers with home, with their loved ones, raised morale in the army, and also had a positive influence on the mood in the families, that is, on the home front. Regardless of where he was stationed on the distant eastern front, in Finland or Norway, the letters circulated efficiently and as a rule reached their addressee. From reading them one can infer that there were no particular restrictions as to the number of postcards or letters sent. What was rigorously observed, however, were weight and formats. Mail to the front reached the battalion, where it was distributed to the companies, for those units were best informed about the whereabouts of the addressee.

This and other letters show how profoundly the war destroyed, and at the same time laid bare, the complicated sense of national identity, the consciousness of one's own roots, but also of simple human decency, of many German settlers. For we often read about the "wonderfully simple and thereby beautiful childhood," the joyful days spent in Łazińsk, while at the same time Mielke writes that before the war this was for them the "difficult period of Poland." There is in these stories a strange alienation, a peculiar extraterritoriality of the land that nourished them. The system of "values" that Nazi ideologues had been instilling into the Germans for years and that came to reign in the conquered Wartheland shakes or suppresses, in these pious people, the age-old moral order built on the biblical Ten Commandments. There is not a word of protest against what the authority does, whose orders are diligently carried out. They made their neighbors into slaves, they killed patriots, they plundered their homes and land, and still they believe that "in this time heading toward destruction," on this ruin of former ideals, they will preserve their sunny land.

This is a very important and dramatic image of a man embedded, through a cruel and ruthless system, in ultimate situations, a man cornered by great history. For the family members to whom he wrote, such repeated declarations of a man who had fought for many months on the front line and claimed that everything that befell him happened "by the grace

 of God" must have carried a weight and a force of arguments not to be gainsaid. For this is noticeable – even in this relatively modest group of letters – that regardless of whether he is marching to the East to build the promised paradise, or retreating under the growing pressure of the enemy, he never gives a sign that he is undergoing a crisis of faith. Still his chief dream is to become a pastor, to celebrate masses and other rites and to proclaim the word of God. "It is not out of the question that God will have to lead us all through very difficult roads." He did not abandon his God for the new "filthy" faith. But – and this observation is worth raising – the image of the Christian: the tiny, humble "servant of God," mocked by Nazi propaganda, does not quite agree with what we perceive in the stance of this soldier. He fights on the front line, kills enemies and lice, is tough and does not want to be worse or weaker than his comrades. He differs, perhaps, from some of them in that he sees, or only senses, the coming days of defeat. In this sense one may venture the remark that he did not walk those bad roads in darkness. Another matter is whether the light that shone for him was bright to the very end.

Reading Bruno Mielke's letters, one has the impression that they were written in two languages. Personal experiences, concern for the family, confessional declarations – this is vocabulary drawn from the Bible, religious writings and sentimental ballads about love. Here even the descriptions of the side he is fighting against do not "reek" of hatred. These are Russians, opponents, in the sharpest version the enemy. And the pre-war times are an idyllic picture of "blessed hours" and the happy inhabitants of Łazińsk. Quite different phrases flow when he declares fidelity and obedience to the Führer, readiness for every sacrifice for the great Germany that is setting the world to rights. This is a set of ready-made clichés from the language of the Third Reich (LTI – Lingua Tertii Imperii).

How does he understand his part in this war? As an act of divine will. Is this war, then, also the work of God?

### Łazińsk – The Village's History Spans More Than 280 Years

Note in: Der Heimatbote, 8/81–3. Card index Wartheland (Kraj Warty), Poland  
Translated by Jacek Kin

Łazińsk is a village formerly in the Konin county, in which the German population lived for the most part. Heading from the county town Konin along the Warta toward the southwest, after some time one reached the commune of Drommin (Trąbczyn), to which, besides the village of Michalinów, belonged the settlement of Łazińsk, inhabited almost exclusively by Germans. Until the year 1945, some 170 German families still lived there. It was the year 1718 when 14 German families arrived from Silesia with their village headman at their fore, and from the owner of the landed estate of Trąbczyn they received a large forest area in order to develop it. From heavy pioneering work there arose the village of Łazińsk "from a green root" (*aus grüner Wurzel*). The families living here were called: Kuhn, Merk, Schönfisch, Haupt. These surnames were still encountered in this region until the year 1945, and can be found many times on the ancestors' chart of the Weber/Rhytthammer family.

In the year 1968, the German settlement could have celebrated the 250th anniversary of its existence, had history taken a different course.

 To the left of the cantoral school, on a gently rising hillock, there lay a richly wooded cemetery, on which our forefathers founded the first grave in the year 1727. The most ancient place of rest, whose weathered and rotted gravestones of wood and iron existed still until the year 1945. The colonists built their own school quite early, and at the same time a house of prayer, and before long Łazińsk, numbering 150 inhabitants, became the largest cantorate in the later commune of Zagórów (Hinterberg).
