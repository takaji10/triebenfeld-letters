**Prusimski** (Ostroróg-Prusimski), Kazimierz Józef Antoni, of the Nałęcz arms (1744 – c. 1806), deputy to the Sejms, activist of the emigration. Born on 4 March at Kamionna (at Bieganowo according to Dworzaczek), he was the son of Stefan, owner of Kamionna, Kolno and Trąbczyn, and of Wiktoria née Malczewska, by her first marriage the wife of Tomasz Wierusz-Kowalski.

Carefully brought up, P. inherited from his paternal uncle Paweł a considerable fortune with Kolno and Prusinów (the latter he sold to W. Żeromski in 1766); he liked best, however, to live at Śmiłowice in Kuyavia. He put his yearly income at 150,000 Polish złoty. In 1764 he signed the act of the royal election (for the voivodeship of Poznań) as Ostroróg-Prusimski. In 1769, already

\[583\]

as starosta of Nieszczewice, he was at Wschowa at the assembly [zjeździć] of the officials of the Greater Poland voivodeship (15 May) and signed a manifesto in support of the Bar Confederation and of its marshal in Greater Poland, Ignacy Malczewski. He sat as deputy for the land of Wschowa at the Sejm of 1773-1775; he signed the confederation of the Sejm and was named to the delegation for drawing up the treaties with the partitioning powers. The Sejm moreover granted him the starostwo of Nieszczewice (Nieszewice or Miszczewice) in Kuyavia in emphyteutic tenure (a long hereditary lease), and appointed several commissions to settle disputes over estates (among others over Trąbczyn, Chybów, Pawłów, Dzwonów, Berembusz-Sosnowiec?). P. sat again at the Sejm of 1778, this time for the voivodeship of Brześć Kujawski, and was named a judge of the Sejm court. In the same year he was given the Order of St Stanislaus. In 1780 he sat at the Sejm for the voivodeship of Poznań. After the second partition almost all his property lay in the Prussian partition. P. took part in the conspiracy of 1793/4, working with the castellan Dionizy Mniewski, his kinsman by marriage. He was one of the organisers of the rising in Kuyavia in 1794. It was at his house at Śmiłowice that the assembled nobility proclaimed the Kuyavian confederation, at the same time driving the Prussian garrison out of Włocławek and sinking a transport of ammunition being sent by the Vistula to the siege of Warsaw. P. was made major general of Kuyavia. He fought a war of raids against Colonel Szekely for Włocławek and Inowrocław; at Rynaszewo the insurgents were beaten (14 September).

After the fall of the insurrection P. was declared a rebel by the Prussian government, and his property was confiscated. P. answered the loss of his great fortune with the saying: "the Fatherland matters most". At the end of 1794 he managed to escape to Venice with his wife Marianna Dąmbska, and with considerable means. They lived in the Petrillo inn, where the life of the Polish emigrants centred from time to time. The French envoy in Venice, Lallement, valued P. and in his dispatches to the Committee of Public Safety (Komitet Ocalenia Narodowego) described him as "a patriot who, like D. Mniewski, contributed to the rising in Greater Poland". He and Mniewski stood at the head of a group of moderate republicans, who held that Poland should take from the achievements of the French Revolution only what answered to the national spirit. In January 1795 he took part in the constituting of the Emigration Committee in Venice. After the Peace of Basel of 1795 P. remained active in Venice, and his activity was pointed out by K. G. Hoym on 9 May 1796. In this period he drew close to Tadeusz Wyssogiert, with whom he supported the Deputation formed in Paris (22 August 1795) under D. Mniewski. Nevertheless he did not hesitate to approve Michał Ogiński (23 October), whom the Polish "Jacobins" did not want to let take up the post of agent in Constantinople.

P. received the formation of Dąbrowski's Polish Legions (backed by the moderates led by Piotr Potocki and Stanisław Sołtyk) with the reserve proper to the supporters of the Deputation. The occupation of Venice by the Austrians drove him out to Mantua in the middle of March 1797. There he met General Jan Henryk Dąbrowski, who sent him on to the Legions' depot in Milan (P. arrived there on 21 March 1797). He soon decided, however, to leave for Paris, where he was among Mniewski's trusted men. On the Deputation's orders he went with Ksawery Jezierski to Dresden (1 July) and then to England, where he tried to find Kościuszko, released from Russian captivity, and to win him for the Deputation's programme. The plan came too late, for Kościuszko had left for Philadelphia on 14 June. P. spent 7 months in England; he began a correspondence with the Naczelnik (Kościuszko, the Commander-in-Chief of 1794) and tried in long letters (18 September, 3 October) to win him for the republican camp; in this way he countered a like move by the moderates, led by J. H. Dąbrowski and Józef Wybicki. At the beginning of 1798 P. set out by way of Rotterdam for Dresden, where he settled, living modestly but keeping in touch with the "Dresden Club", which favoured the Deputation. When Kościuszko came to Paris in June, P. greeted him with a letter (13 August) in which he stressed that "the People and Freedom are the two pillars on which the Poles must rest". The Naczelnik answered him politely, regretting that he "does not know him personally" but only from his letters. After the Deputation was dissolved in March 1799 P. made contact with the Society of Polish Republicans in Warsaw and was an informant of Andrzej Domański (still on 2 July 1800). At the same time, however, he made contact with the moderates (supporters of the Constitution of 3 May 1791) and took care to stay on good terms with the legionaries (he passed on the legionaries' letters to the homeland).

P. obtained permission to return to the Prussian partition in 1803, probably through the efforts of his daughter Michalina, widow of Józef Dąmbski. Despite his efforts he did not recover the confiscated property. He did not live to see the French enter the country. After the defeat of Prussia, when General Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, the holder of the estate confiscated from P., was a prisoner of the French, efforts were renewed to have the lost estates given back to P.'s family.

By his marriage to Marianna Dąmbska of Lubraniec, daughter of Józef, castellan of Kowal, and of Barbara née Komorowska, he had an only daughter, Michalina, married to Józef Dąmbski, son of the voivode of Brześć Kujawski (d. 1798), and from 1809 to Stanisław Miączyński, adjutant of Prince Józef Poniatowski.
