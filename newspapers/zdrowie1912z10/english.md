# On the Nutrition of the People

[720]

We know absolutely nothing about how rural people nourish themselves, or we have very inaccurate information about it. We know, for example, that our people derive their nutrition mainly from the plant world, and here potatoes constitute their daily dish, and that the people have become accustomed to satisfy their feelings of hunger mainly with potatoes. This is where our knowledge about the nutrition of the people ends. There have been attempts to calculate the quantity of food consumed by the people, but these attempts, according to me, taken from one or even several villages of a given area, too broadly generalized the obtained figures to the entire country, for it is a fact that our people nourish themselves differently, depending on the region they inhabit, and besides, in the same region the difference in the people's nutrition is enormous, and dependent on their degree of wealth: a stable hand nourishes himself differently, differently a smallholder or landless peasant, and differently a wealthy peasant. So I think that if we want to have more accurate information about the nutrition of the people, we should consider separately territories that remain in more or less similar economic conditions, and besides, we need to divide the people into

[721]

categories according to their wealth, and the collected data will only then tell us how the people in a given area nourish themselves.

For my research on the nutrition of the people, I took the Słupca district, which stretches in a narrow strip on the border of the Grand Duchy of Poznań. Since the population of the entire borderland is in more or less similar material conditions, it seems that I will not make an error if I say that the data I obtained can be extended to the entire stretch of our country lying on the Prussian border. The question of rural people's nutrition has interested me for a long time; for over a dozen years I have been collecting data and have always had the opportunity to see how differently our people nourish themselves depending on their degree of wealth.

Due to the difference in the people's nutrition, I must divide the rural population into several groups and discuss each of them separately.

So, wealthier peasants owning more than 15 morgans [unit of land measurement]. Here breakfast consists of coffee with milk, bread and butter; dinner on non-fasting days of broth and meat, meat mainly pork, though sometimes beef, mutton or veal, sometimes chicken; on fasting days potato soup, or porridge with milk, or millet soup with butter, less often pea soup and bread; afternoon snack only in summer: bread with cottage cheese; supper: milk with potatoes, or milk soup with potatoes in summer, and in winter beet soup with potatoes; older ones still drink tea with bread and butter, but mainly only in winter. I should add that I consider only the normal way of life of the peasant, I omit family celebrations such as baptisms, weddings, funeral wakes, fairs, church festivals, etc., because here, due to the low culture of our peasant, there are excesses in eating, and even more in drinking alcohol in various forms. Let us now see to what extent such nutrition is sufficient for our peasant who works hard physically. For my calculations I take the nutrition that the peasant receives on an ordinary day, so I omit such fasting days when he nourishes himself worse. I assume that the better nutrition of festive days compensates for the worse fasting days, so one can take as a norm the nutrition of an ordinary day, neither festive nor fasting.

The organism of a hard-working person, such as our peasant, needs about 50 heat units per day per kilogram of weight.

It is different among smallholders: here nutrition is significantly worse, not to mention the fact that other hygienic conditions are also worse among these than among wealthier farmers.

From the above calculation it appears that the nutrition of the smallholder is insufficient, so no wonder that among smallholders we see mainly thin, exhausted, prematurely aged individuals. A manor farmhand nourishes himself in more or less the same way; here regarding this matter the conditions are the same as among smallholders.

However, worse than the two mentioned categories of people is how a laborer working for daily wages nourishes himself. In the border strip, these people in summer go to work mainly to Prussia, and there throughout their intensive work they nourish themselves on rice, skimmed milk and potatoes. Meat, due to the high price of this food item in Prussia, they eat once a week.

So the nutrition of such a laborer in summer amounts to no more than 2000 heat units, instead of 3500\. These losses, if nota bene he earned well in summer, he compensates for himself somewhat in winter, when he works very little or does nothing at all, and nourishes himself significantly better; but if due to various circumstances his Prussian earnings were not good, he continues to starve in winter as he starved in summer. From the above it appears that except for the few rich farmers in our country, the rest nourish themselves insufficiently.

But it is not enough that our peasant nourishes himself insufficiently; there are still other circumstances that significantly impair his nutrition. Let us look at who and how prepares his food.

A rural girl who has not the slightest idea about nutrition and cooking, when she marries, prepares food for herself and her husband, so her method of food preparation is

[724]

extremely primitive, and the food cooked by her hand is tasteless and poorly digestible. The number of dishes she serves to her household members is limited to 5-6 and these she constantly cooks, and we all know how the monotony of foods affects assimilation by the organism.

To this is added the dirt and slovenliness that we mainly see in the countryside during food preparation; kitchen utensils often poorly washed cause the foods prepared in them to burn, as a result of which they are tasteless and unhealthy. Plates and spoons are often dirty, and household members, sitting down to receive food, almost never wash their hands, with dirty hands they take bread and meat and in this way often introduce disease-causing bacteria into the organism. It is not enough that the majority of people nourish themselves insufficiently; the defectiveness in food preparation and monotony impairs assimilation, and little attention to the cleanliness of utensils and hands causes disease-causing microorganisms to enter the organism, which, being non-resistant due to insufficient nutrition and hard physical work, easily succumbs to them, which is the cause of various diseases among our people.

Now let us ask what is the reason that our people pay so little attention to their nutrition. Understanding the causes will allow us to find ways to change for improvement in this direction.

Our peasant has been accustomed for centuries to look at his body as something undeserving of attention. He takes food because without it the organism cannot exist, but it is all the same to him what and how he eats, as long as he satisfies the feeling of hunger. No one has yet taught him that for the soul to be healthy, one must care that the body also does not fall into disease, so he focuses all his attention on the soul, caring little about his earthly shell. The lack of the most elementary knowledge about nutrition is the reason why he nourishes himself poorly, and the lack of education is the reason for insufficient nutrition.

So with the development of education, the nutrition of our people must also improve. Since the development of education among the people does not depend entirely on us, we should strive to ensure that our people acquire certain knowledge about nutrition, through which they would excellently improve their nutrition. Much can be done in this direction.

[725]

If rural farmers today have facilitated acquisition of knowledge about agriculture, which they greatly benefit from, and through which their wealth increases, rural housewives should also be taught to cook, to provide the organism with a sufficient amount of food, and in such a form that it can be assimilated by the digestive tract.

So to introduce changes for the better in the nutrition of our people, we need first to try to raise their wealth, which will affect the amount of food consumed by the peasant, but alongside this we need to establish as many domestic economy schools for rural girls as possible, where in addition to other necessary knowledge about hygiene, they would acquire knowledge about cooking and nutrition, and moreover, in specially written popular brochures, the people should be constantly instructed on how and what to eat.

If the people know that nutrition is not such a trivial matter as they have thought so far, that health or disease depends on good or bad nutrition, if the peasant's wife instead of today's few dishes introduces a certain variety into this area of life, which is not difficult with good intentions, if moreover the dishes are prepared tastily and served in a form that arouses appetite, our peasant will gain enormously in health: his nutrition will improve, and on the other hand, at church festivals and fairs he will avoid today's dirty little-town shambles, where he currently treats himself to sausage and blood pudding, which are truly disgusting to look at, especially since having something to eat at home, he will sometimes avoid the mindless drinking of vodka during the consumption of blood pudding and sausage in the small town.
