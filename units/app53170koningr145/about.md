In 1774 the Polish parliament appointed a commission to settle, at last, the boundary between Antoni Prusimski's estate of Trąbczyn and the Chełmski brothers' estate of Łukomia. The commission sat on the disputed ground from September 1775, walked the line each side claimed, ruled for Prusimski, raised new boundary mounds, and punished the Chełmskis for years of violence on the disputed ground. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

Trąbczyn lay in the district of Konin in the province of Kalisz, in the west of the Polish Commonwealth. In the 1770s it belonged to Antoni Prusimski, Starost of Niszczewice. The neighbouring estate of Łukomia belonged to the brothers Heliodor Chełmski, a canon of Chełmno, and Stanisław Chełmski. A third neighbour was the Cistercian abbey of Ląd, which owned the village of Drzewce. <!-- context -->

Estates of this kind were bounded by earthen mounds raised at intervals and by marks cut into trees. Where three estates met stood a corner mound, from which each stretch of boundary was reckoned. A boundary was proved by leading a court along it on the ground, by older court records, and by the sworn testimony of old inhabitants. A judgment was entered in the records of a municipal court, which gave it public standing, and copies were then drawn from those records. <!-- context -->

The municipal court took shape at the end of the fourteenth century. For the nobility it first heard criminal cases under the four "castle articles": arson, an attack on a nobleman's house, robbery on the public road, and rape. In time it came to hear other criminal cases, and suits over land and sums of money, and it took over appeals from the town courts. Beside the court grew the municipal court office, where the court books were kept. The office was open every day for the entry of uncontested matters, and what was entered in its books was accepted as proof: what stood in them counted as proved. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**Where it was registered.** The decree was registered at the court of Brześć Kujawski on 23 September 1776, and from there copied into the book of the court of Konin [[1]].

**The commission.** The Polish parliament appointed a commission to end the boundary dispute between Prusimski's estates of Trąbczyn, Trąbczynek Mały or Nowa Wieś, Łazy and Osiny and Stanisław Chełmski's village of Łukom, a dispute that had run for decades with violence and the seizure of land. The act of parliament, of 1 June 1774, set out the oath the commissioners were to swear. The commission met on 13 September 1775 in the forest on the disputed ground, under Ludwik Dąmbski, voivode of Brześć Kujawski [[1]].

**A divided court.** In the first days the commissioners quarrelled over which of them should hold the pen, that is, write the court's record. The president gave it to one boundary surveyor of Kalisz; the sub-chamberlain of Poznań, the official who judged boundary disputes in that province, with the commissioners who sat on Chełmski's side, gave it to another. A settlement between the parties was proposed and four commissioners were delegated to negotiate it. It failed, and the court went on sitting in two parts; the decree is the work of one of them [[1]].

**Prusimski's boundary.** Prusimski led the court along the line he claimed. It ran from the corner mounds near the road from Zagórów to Kalisz, past an old wolf pit, the woods called Ostrowce, Miąsykąt, Dziadulina and Lusnie, a former pond, by then marsh, and eight mounds with forty-one marked trees, towards the ford called Czarny Bród. At each point he produced documents and earlier judgments, and objected to the line Chełmski claimed and to a court inspection of 1592 on which Chełmski relied [[1]].

**The rulings.** The court examined Chełmski's line as well and heard sworn witnesses. It ruled on the corner point, found Prusimski's line the better, and required him to confirm it by oath on the last mound with six witnesses. It had new mounds raised along the boundary between Trąbczyn and Łukomia, each of which the decree lists with its distance from the one before, and on part of the boundary between Trąbczyn and Biskupice [[1]].

**Penalties and adjournment.** The court found from the sworn testimony that, after Prusimski had put his own people into the inn on the highway, Stanisław Chełmski rode on it in person with several dozen armed men and had shots fired through its window; a nobleman named Krzeczkowski had his heel shot off, and the inn was set on fire. For this and for his encroachments on Trąbczyn ground Chełmski was sentenced to two weeks in the tower of the castle at Konin and to penalties payable to Prusimski, among them three hundred grzywnas, the unit in which fines were reckoned, for the unlawful use of firearms. He was also to pay 8,000 Polish złoty for timber felled and ground flooded, and the penalties due for having walked out of an earlier court on the ground in 1760. The rest of the boundary with Biskupice, and the question of costs, were adjourned to 21 May 1776. The court handed Prusimski the four sets of sworn testimony and a map of the boundary signed by the president. Six commissioners signed the decree, and with them Wojciech Czarnecki, the boundary surveyor of Kalisz who raised the mounds [[1]].
