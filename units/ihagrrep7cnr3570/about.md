In August 1796 Friedrich Wilhelm II, King of Prussia, gave estates confiscated after the Polish uprising of 1794 to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, a Prussian general, and to six other officers. The papers of the Prussian administration for South Prussia – Prussia's share of partitioned Poland – show how the King's gift of Trąbczyn and its neighbours was made. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

South Prussia was the province formed from Prussia's share of the first and second partitions of Poland. After the uprising of 1794 the crown confiscated the estates of landowners who had taken part in it. In 1796 a number of these estates, with others that had fallen vacant, were granted to officers. The grants were proposed by Count Hoym, the minister responsible for Silesia and South Prussia. <!-- context -->

### Contents

On 14 July 1796 Hoym proposed the grants to the King, who approved them by a cabinet order of 23 July. On 30 July Hoym asked that each deed of gift be drawn "cum appertinentia", with everything belonging to the estate, and on 20 August he returned the deeds, signed, for the King [[1]].

For Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen Hoym proposed the estates confiscated from Anton von Prusimski: Kamionna, Kolno, Trąbczyn, Szetlewek, Nowawies, Łazy, Old and New Laski, Osiny, the Trąbczyn Hauland and Brzyce [[1]]. Six other officers received estates: von Sanitz, Blücher, von Dolffs, von Pontanus, von Holtzendorff and von Larisch [[1]].

In 1826 the papers were looked out again when von Sanitz claimed compensation for the estates he had lost. The deeds for the six other officers are shown as scans only. <!-- context -->
