One small exercise book of field notes in German, written in pencil by Albert Breyer (1889-1939), a teacher at the German school in Sompolno and a historian of the German settlements in central Poland. It is kept in the State Archive in Poznań (Archiwum Państwowe w Poznaniu) among his papers, in a bundle of his notebooks that the archive calls "Notes on Grodziec, Józefów, Zameń and Łazińsk". This holding is the one notebook on Łazińsk and Emilienheim, of 35 pages with its covers. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

Łazińsk and Emilienheim were Protestant German settlements on the land of the former Trąbczyn estate, in the commune of Trąbczyn. Two Olęder colonies had been founded on the estate, first called Trąbczyńskie Stare Olędry and Trąbczyńskie Nowe Olędry and later Łazińskie Stare Olędry and Łazińskie Nowe Olędry; their settlers' dispute with the owner of the estate over pasture and wood in his forest is the subject of the summons of 1860, [[unit:app539680783]]. Breyer went from village to village collecting what the settlers remembered and photographing their houses, schools and graves, for his studies of the German settlements. <!-- context -->

### Contents

The cover is dated July 1936 and names Łazinsk and Emilienheim [[1]]. Under the heading "Kolonja Łazińsk" stands the stamp of the village mayor of the commune of Trąbczyn, and the notes that follow give the colony 2,040 Morgen and 250 farmers. About 1880, with a question mark, the colony received servitudes from the Trąbczyn estate; they were added to the old land at the notary's and are heavily taxed [[1]].

Breyer twice asks when the remaining manor land of Trąbczyn was parcelled out. Jesse bought it, and it is now called Włodzimierow [[1]]. He notes how the land of Michalinów is divided into house plots, strips and meadows, the land of the village mayor and of the school, and the classes of the land of Emilienheim, 337 Morgen in all [[1]].

Much of the notebook is about schools and their teachers. In Łazińsk Mielke has taught since 1925; the other teachers named are Klingbeil, 9 years, Ed. Zelmer of Łazińsk, 34 years, Radke, 10 years, and Rasch, 50 years, who was still teaching at 80. On the first day of lessons there were 186 children in the school, and for ten years one teacher had 170 pupils. The military authorities complained to the Ministry of Education that the recruits of the commune of Trąbczyn could not speak Polish [[1]]. Emilienheim was joined to Drzewce because 11 persons were lacking to make 200, and the village mayor there does not know the German people [[1]].

Breyer records where the settlers went: many to Volhynia, around Novograd-Volynsk and Żytomierz, people from Łazinsk to Russia and back, others to America, and an aunt of Krüger's to Brazil [[1]]. He notes whom they marry, which hymn books they used, field names, sayings, the slow, drawn-out speech of the "Brücher" of the marsh villages, houses of clay and timber, Pomeranian barns, and the wooden grave boards in Łazińsk and Emilienheim, which he sketched [[1]].

The last pages are a log of his photographs: subject, aperture, filter, exposure time and hour. One page is headed July 1935 and names Gostynin, Leonberg and Grabie, places outside this district [[1]].

### Form and language

Pencil notes in German, in Latin script, with field names and a few notes in German Kurrent; they are short and much abbreviated, and quote the settlers' own words. Breyer separates his notes with a short line and a small circle, and drew the shapes of houses, barns and grave boards. The pencil is faint, and very faint on the lilac inside covers, so many words are read with doubt. <!-- context -->
