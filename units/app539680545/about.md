Albert Breyer (1889-1939), a teacher at the German school in Sompolno, studied the German settlements of central Poland and what became of them. In the 1930s he collected evidence on one question: what happened to the German surnames of the country around Zagórów when the families who bore them became Polish. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The German settlements of the Trąbczyn country – the Olęder colonies of Łazińsk and Emilienheim, the Hauländer villages – kept their language and their parishes into the twentieth century, and their surnames with them. After Poland's restoration the state offered a legal way to change a name, and many German families took instead the shorter way their clergy and commune heads showed them: a German name made to look Polish. Breyer, who collected among these settlements in the 1930s, gathered the evidence on these sheets. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The names.** In the commune of Zagórów, from as early as 1843 and 1855, German surnames had been given Polish forms: Bobel became Rybicki, Grüning Zielinski, Breitkreutz Breitkrejc, Schmeckel Szmigielski, Stange Sztanga, Niemann Nowak [[1, 2]]. Breyer lists some fifty such names, from Arminski to Wilmański [[3, 5]]. Others were not Polonised but translated: Zech became Czech, Rolle Rola, Sack Zak, Blum Kwiatkowska, Erdmann Ziemiecki, Hoppe Chmielewski, Bohrer Swiderski [[5]].

**An old practice.** From a Breslau print of 1741, George Schlag's "Polnisch-deutsche Briefe", Breyer copied eight Polish names with their German equivalents, Wojciech Wilkotek as Albrecht Bärwolff, Jakub Wierzbrecki as Jacob Weidner: the exchange of names between the languages was as old as the German schools of Silesia [[4]].

**Breyer's essay.** Whoever walks through Warsaw, Lublin or a market town, Breyer writes, meets German names on the shop signs whose bearers may not be addressed as Germans. Changing a name by law, announced in the state newspaper "Monitor Polski", was slow and costly; commune heads and evangelical pastors, among them Pastor Winkler in Wieluń and Pastor v. Boerner in Zduńska-Wola, took the simpler way of putting the whole name into Polish, so that a Jahn became Koguziński and a Blum Kwiatkowski. "Thus German family names are mutilated", he concludes, "fitted to the language-feeling of the majority people" [[7]]. Pastor Winkler is presumably the "P. Winkler" whose list of names opens the collection [[1]].

**The schools.** A separate essay records that the German secondary schools of the Łódź region, at Łódź, Pabianice, Zgierz and Sompolno, had agreed on one twelve-year course of study [[6]].
