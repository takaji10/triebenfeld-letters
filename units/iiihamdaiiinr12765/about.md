Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen lost the Trąbczyn estates in 1807, when the region passed to the Duchy of Warsaw, and from 1814 he and, after his death in 1818, his heirs asked to be compensated. They petitioned the Prussian government, which took up the claim with Russia half-heartedly until Russia, which ruled the region from 1815, refused the claim in 1816 and again in 1820. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

In 1796 the King of Prussia granted Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen a group of estates in South Prussia, the province formed from Prussia's share of the partitions of Poland. The estates had been confiscated from the Starost Prusimski after the uprising of 1794 [[2, 10]]. They comprised Trąbczyn with several villages in the district of Konin, and Kamionna and Kolno further west [[2]]. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen sold Kamionna and Kolno to Leixner, a bank director in 1804 or 1805 [[2, 10]].

Prussia ceded the province by the Peace of Tilsit in 1807, and the Duchy of Warsaw was established in it. Fourteen weeks after the peace a Polish administrative commission took possession of all the estates without a court judgment and transferred them to Prusimski's daughter [[2]]. She was Dąbska by her first husband and Miączyńska by her second [[2, 15]]. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen brought an action, and the tribunal at Kalisz declared that it had no authority to set aside the commission's order [[2]]. After the Congress of Vienna the district of Konin belonged to the Kingdom of Poland under the Russian Emperor, to whom the claim was thereafter addressed [[6]].

### Contents

#### Petitions of 1814 and 1815

Peter Friedrich von Triebenfeld, the general agent of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, petitioned the State Chancellor, Hardenberg, in Paris in May 1814 and in Vienna in October 1814 and March 1815 [[1, 2]]. The petition of March 1815 states the case in full and asks for the return of the Trąbczyn estates with nine years' revenue, 36,500 Rthl [[2]]. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen wrote in April 1815 to Baron vom Stein and in May 1815 to Hardenberg. He gives his loss as 325,400 écus in the first letter and as more than 261,000 Rthl in the second, and in the second asks to be given the crown estate of Sokolnik in compensation [[4, 6]]. The replies defer the matter until the settlement of Polish affairs and refer to a negotiation on the compensation of all holders of granted estates in South Prussia [[1, 3, 5, 6]].

#### Report of Zerboni di Sposetti, 1815

Hardenberg requested a report from Zerboni di Sposetti, the senior Prussian official at Poznań [[6]]. The report of 2 November 1815 rejects the calculations submitted for Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, states that he had allowed Trąbczyn to deteriorate and had encumbered it with debt, and concludes that Prussia could do no more than ask the Russian government to decide the claim [[10]]. By April 1816 Triebenfeld had died [[10]].

#### Intercession at St Petersburg, 1816

In April 1816 the ministry instructed the Prussian envoy at St Petersburg, Major General von Schöler, to bring the request to the Emperor's notice in general terms and without pressing it, compensation being a matter of grace [[11, 13]]. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen was informed of the instruction [[12]]. Schöler reported on 23 November 1816 that the Russian ministry had refused, and the ministry sent Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen a copy of the Russian note [[20, 21]].

#### Petition of the widow von Brehmer, 1816

The widow of Colonel von Brehmer petitioned as heir of her brother, Major von Seehausen, who in 1803 had lent Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen 5,000 Rthl secured on Szetlewek, a village of the Trąbczyn estates; neither capital nor interest had been paid [[15]]. An official of the ministry held that any compensation Russia granted should go to the creditors registered on the estates [[15]]. The ministry directed her to sue Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen before his own court and informed Schöler of the creditors' position [[16, 17]]. She then submitted a judgment of 1806 in her favour; the ministry replied that it could take no further step [[18, 19]].

#### Claim of the heirs, 1820

After the death of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen his heirs had their claim recommended to the Russian government by Duke Eugen of Württemberg, a Russian general and the Emperor's cousin, who pointed to two Prussian generals who had been compensated [[22]]. The Russian envoy at Berlin, Alopeus, replied on 31 January 1820 that the claim had been refused in 1816 and that the Emperor could not act on the recommendation [[22]]. The ministry took no further step and referred the matter to the Minister of the Interior, von Schuckmann [[23]].

#### Count Bethusy, 1819 to 1820

In the same note Russia refused a request of Count Ernst Bethusy, a landowner in Silesia and in the Kingdom of Poland, for free movement between his properties under article 18 of the Treaty of Vienna; the Polish government held that the article applied only to properties divided by the new border [[22]]. Both Prussian ministries thought the request inadmissible [[23, 24]].
