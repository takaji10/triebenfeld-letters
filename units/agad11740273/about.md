In July 1807 the Governing Commission – the provisional Polish government set up under Napoleon – was sitting at Dresden, and Michalina Dąbska, the daughter of Antoni Prusimski, asked it there to give back her father's confiscated estates. Her petitions, and the Commission's resolution that granted them, are how the Trąbczyn estates came back to the Prusimski family. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

Prussia confiscated the estates of the Starost Antoni Prusimski in 1796 and gave Trąbczyn and Kamionna with Kolno to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen (1746-1818), a Prussian general. In the war of 1806 and 1807 Napoleon drove Prussia out of the Polish provinces it had taken in the partitions. On 14 January 1807 he set up the Governing Commission, a provisional government of Poles under Stanisław Małachowski. It administered those provinces until 5 October 1807, when the government of the new Duchy of Warsaw took office. In July 1807, after the Peace of Tilsit, the commission was at Dresden, where Napoleon gave the Duchy its constitution. <!-- context -->

Józef Wybicki, a member of the commission, had also lost estates to Prussia. Napoleon returned them to him by a decree given at Finckenstein on 5 June 1807, and the commission ordered the decree carried out on 12 July: [[unit:agad1174016]]. Michalina Dąbska took that decree as her precedent. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The petitions of 20 and 21 July.** On 20 July 1807 Dąbska wrote to the commission in Polish. She had twice asked for the return of the estates confiscated from her father for the benefit of the Prussian generals she names as Prince Hohenlohe, Bischoffswerder and Zanitz: Trąbczyn in the department of Kalisz, and Kamionna, Kolno, Pawłowo, Pawłówko and a storehouse at a place written "Wrocławek" in the department of Poznań. She had had no answer. She asked the commission to intercede for her, so that she might obtain the judgment Wybicki had obtained from the Emperor [[2]]. On 21 July she petitioned Napoleon himself, in French and in the same terms [[3]]. By Napoleon's order the commission's Director of War, Prince Józef Poniatowski, laid it before an extraordinary session at Dresden the same day [[3]].

**The resolution of 21 July.** Napoleon had referred Dąbska's case to the commission. That day at Dresden the commission, with its president Małachowski, took the justice Napoleon had done Józef Wybicki by his decree of Finckenstein as its example and extended it to her and to two others, General Niemojewski and Wichrowski: their hereditary estates, confiscated by the Prussian government, were to return to them [[1]]. The resolution named no estate [[1]]. Its fair copy is in the commission's register: [[unit:agad1174016]].

**The petition of 23 July.** Two days later Dąbska wrote to the commission again. The Emperor's decree for Wybicki had been taken as the principle and pattern for the commission's decree of 21 July. She asked that the commission have its resolution carried out as the decree for Wybicki had been: its Directorate of Internal Affairs, the department that ran the country's administration, was to order the administrative chambers of the departments to put her in possession of the estates [[4]].

Trąbczyn was handed to Dąbska's agent in October 1807: [[unit:oe1bu9454]]. <!-- context -->
