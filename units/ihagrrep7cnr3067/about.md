After the Polish rising of 1794 was put down, Prussia judged the insurgents of the part of Poland it had taken the year before: fines for most, the fortress for some, confiscation for the leaders who had fled. In February 1797 the Grand Chancellor, Heinrich Julius von Goldbeck, laid the outcome before the King in a memorandum (promemoria) and proposed a further pardon, and the King refused it in his own hand. What was not in question on either side was the confiscation of the fugitives' estates, which for the most part had already been disposed of. Among the fugitives Goldbeck names Antoni Prusimski, written "Prudzinski", whose Trąbczyn had been granted to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen in 1796. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

South Prussia was the province Prussia formed from its share of the second partition of Poland in 1793; Count Hoym was its minister in Berlin. When the rising led by Kościuszko spread there in 1794, the Prussian authorities named Antoni Prusimski, Starost of Niszczewice and owner of Trąbczyn and Kamionna, among its leaders, and he left the country. Heinrich Julius von Goldbeck, Grand Chancellor of Prussia and head of its justice, writes here as the minister responsible for the courts that tried the insurgents. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**Goldbeck's promemoria, 16 February 1797.** By the laws, Goldbeck writes, many of the authors and participants of the South Prussian rebellion would have deserved death and the rest confiscation and the fortress for life. Letters of pardon, the amnesty patents of 24 September and 10 December 1794 and the King's own instruction of 3 April 1795 had softened this: only six or eight chief ringleaders were to be punished with the rigour of the laws, and not with death; the rest were to pay fines set by a finance commission at Poznań; only those who would not submit or could not pay were to go to the fortress for a time. The justice, he notes, had no share in these ordinances. The chief ringleaders, among them Niemoiewski, Wybicki, Xaver Dombrowski, Prusimski (written "Prudzinski") and the canon Jezierski (written "Jetzierski"), had fled, and only the property they left behind could be confiscated; other promoters of the rebellion had settled with the commission by paying fines. Of 387 insurgents, 307 had only paid fines, 49 had been sentenced to the fortress for up to three years, 26 for long terms, 2 for life and 3 to death, one of whom was pardoned under the gallows and one of whom escaped. If the King wished to extend his mercy, Goldbeck advises that the confiscation of the fugitives stand, since most of the confiscated estates had already been disposed of, and that the fines still be collected, since the province's expenses counted on them; the short sentences might be remitted, the long ones halved, except for three men condemned chiefly as highway robbers, and the life sentence of Stockowski, who seemed to have acted from simplicity and vanity rather than malice, cut to ten years, with a pension or dowry out of the confiscated estates for his daughter. If the King agreed, the amnesty was to be made public and Hoym and Goldbeck himself were to receive their orders [[1]].

**The King's decision, 16 February 1797.** Beneath Goldbeck's signature King Friedrich Wilhelm II wrote that he found none of the condemned worthy of a reduction of the punishment; those condemned to fines were rather to be made to pay them, and those unable to pay to be put under arrest in proportion. [[1]]

**The condemned.** Goldbeck enclosed a list of the rebels sentenced to death, to the fortress for life or to long terms in the fortress, with their offences; the long terms had begun between February 1795 and November 1796. Franz von Wittwicki and Andreas Retznowski were sentenced to death for leading the canons' servants against the military at the attack on Włocławek and murdering an officer and an excise official. For life: Joseph von Stockowski, general of the insurgents of Wieluń; Joseph Nowicki, sentenced to the gallows for the seizure of an artillery transport at Włocławek and pardoned; and Joseph Sobolewski, who joined a band of robbers after the rising. Twenty-six men served long terms of four to fifteen years, among them two priests, the secretary of the insurgents' commission of order, the generals of the insurgents of Łęczyca and Kalisz, and the president of the confederation of Wieluń and Ostrzewo [[1]].
