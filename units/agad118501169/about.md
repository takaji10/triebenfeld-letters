A report of the Commission of Instruction of the Council of State of the Kingdom of Poland, written in French and dated at the commission's session in Warsaw on 28 October 1834. It is kept in the Central Archives of Historical Records (Archiwum Główne Akt Dawnych) in Warsaw among the files of the Council of State of the Kingdom of Poland (fond II Rada Stanu Królestwa Polskiego), as unit 1/185/0/1/169. The manuscript holds 42 written pages, numbered 157 to 198 in pencil, with a blank leaf after them; the report is published here as one document. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The Kingdom of Poland, set up by Congress Poland in 1815 out of the Duchy of Warsaw, had its own Council of State, whose organisation gave it the decision of conflicts of jurisdiction: cases where a court and an administrative authority each claimed, or denied the other, the competence to judge. The Commission of Instruction was the Council's reporting body; it prepared the reasoned report on which the Council decided. <!-- context -->

The conflict decided here grew out of the restitution of the Trąbczyn estate. Prussia confiscated Trąbczyn in 1796 and gave it to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, who borrowed 30,000 écus against it in 1800 from a Sieur Grotowski. In 1807 the Governing Commission of the Duchy of Warsaw restored the estate to Michalina Prusimska, the daughter of the last Polish owner, married first Dąbska and then Miączyńska, and the Court of Appeal of the Department of Posen had her ownership inscribed with the express reservation of the rights of third parties. Weigel, to whom Grotowski's claim had been assigned, had the inscription transferred to himself in 1808 and sued for the accumulated interest before the Tribunal of Kalisz in 1810; the Tribunal struck the claim from the registers, holding that the restitution decree had extinguished the rights that rested on the confiscated estate. The Court of Appeal and the Supreme Tribunal confirmed that outcome in 1820 and 1826, and the Supreme decision of 4 September 1821 ordered that the judicial course in the case not be obstructed. In 1829 the Council of Administration authorised the Commission of Finances and the Treasury to raise a conflict of jurisdiction against the courts' incompetence, and the Commission laid its report before the Council of State in July 1830. [[1]] <!-- context -->

The correspondence of the Prince's agent holds the other side of the same story: Weigel, a banker at Wrocław, was one of the great creditors of the Hohenlohe estates, and his compensation claim of 1830 and Prussia's protests run through the letters of the 1820s. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The judicial course.** The report first retraces the affair in the courts, in the order given above [[1]]. It keeps the Tribunals' own grounds: the four points of the Kalisz judgment of the 1820s, with the argument that the restitution decree, as a special privilege, had restored the estate with its encumbrances extinguished, and the five points on which the Supreme Court founded its sentence of 1826. <!-- context -->

**The administrative course.** It then retraces the same affair in the administrative channel: the Dame's petition of 1811 to the King of Saxony, then Duke of Warsaw; the Liquidation Commission's report of 1818 to Prince Czapski, Minister of the Kingdom, proposing to set her claims before the King of Prussia diplomatically first; the Prussian reply of 1819; Berlin's intercession for Weigel, asking that the question be withdrawn from the Tribunals; the Commission of Justice's reports of 1820 and 1821; and the events that led to the authorisation of 1829 and the report of 1830 [[1]]. <!-- context -->

**The argument.** The bulk of the report answers the Finance Commission. That commission had argued that the Tribunals gave an ironical interpretation of the restitution decree and so infringed the Treaty of the State, and that they exceeded their powers by taking the decree as the basis of a civil judgment. The Commission of Instruction answers that the Finance Commission proves at most that the courts judged wrongly, never that they ought not to have judged at all, which alone could ground a conflict of jurisdiction; that the courts' competence followed from the nature of the object in litigation, a mortgage claim; and that the Sovereign's interpretation of his own decrees does not displace the civil courts' application of them. It then takes the three grounds on which the Finance Commission justified the conflict (a° to c°) against the four conditions the organisation of the Council of State sets for raising one, and finds that none of the four was met, that no conflict of jurisdiction existed or exists, and that what the Finance Commission brings is in substance an appeal in cassation, a power the Council of State does not hold. <!-- context -->

**The conclusion.** The Commission of Instruction advises that the Council of State set the demand aside as based on no legal principle, and hands in a draft decree drawn to its conclusions [[1]]. <!-- context -->

### Form and language

The report is in French, the working language of the Kingdom's central bodies, in the administrative prose of the period: long sentences, grounds numbered 1° to 4° and 1° to 5°, arguments lettered a° to c° and answered ad a., ad b., ad c. The heading on the first page is heavily corrected in the manuscript. A passage on p. 160, a first draft of the grounds, is struck out with a line through it; this edition does not record crossed-out text. A passage on p. 159, on the Hohenlohe action of 1808, which the archive's transcript skips, is restored from the scan. <!-- context -->
