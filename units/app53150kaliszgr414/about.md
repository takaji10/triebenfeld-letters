The Lusnia wood, a pine wood with a small Olęder settlement on the disputed ground between Trąbczyn and Łukom, was handed to Stanisław Ścibor Chełmski of Łukomia in November 1771 on the strength of judgments he had won against Antoni Prusimski of Trąbczyn. Twelve Olęder settlers, named one by one, passed with the wood to Chełmski; the handover shows how a party who had won at law in eighteenth-century Poland was put into possession by an officer of the court. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

Lusnia was a wood on the disputed ground between Trąbczyn and Łukom. Antoni Prusimski, heir of Trąbczyn and Starost of Niszczewice, treated it as his and had settled Olęder farmers in it. Stanisław Ścibor Chełmski of Łukomia claimed it as his from of old. Through the 1760s Chełmski obtained judgments against Prusimski, several of them by default, before a court sitting on the ground and before the Crown Tribunal. A party that had won in this way was put into possession by an officer who went to the place, read out the judgments and handed the property over before witnesses. That act is a delivery of possession (traditio). <!-- context -->

Four years later the tables were turned. The commission that fixed the boundary in 1775 found Lusnia to be Trąbczyn's, set this delivery of possession aside as unlawful, and ordered Chełmski to repay the rents he had taken from the twelve settlers and the profits of the inn: [[unit:app53170koningr145]]. <!-- context -->

The municipal court took shape at the end of the fourteenth century. For the nobility it first heard criminal cases under the four "castle articles": arson, an attack on a nobleman's house, robbery on the public road, and rape. In time its competence changed and came to include the other criminal cases, and suits over land and over sums of money, and it took over appeals from the town courts. Beside the court grew the municipal court office, where the court books were kept. The office was open every day for the entry of uncontested business, and what was entered in its books was accepted as proof. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The judgments.** On the Monday after All Saints 1771 the court's officer, Wawrzyniec Wstowski, sworn general commissioner of the provinces of Poznań and Kalisz, came to the wood at Chełmski's request. Before the noblemen Cajetan Grabski and Antoni and Konstanty Fulwarski, father and son, Józef Jaroszewski, the steward of Trąbczyn, and the court messenger Joannes Bykierski of Węgierce, he read out the judgments Chełmski had won against Antoni Prusimski: one given on the disputed ground in 1761; a decree of the Crown Tribunal, the kingdom's highest court, given at Bydgoszcz in 1766, ordering first of all that Łukom and Trąbczyn be divided by a boundary; an order of the Tribunal of 1767 and a second decree of 1768; and a judgment of 1770 against Prusimski for defying the decree [[1]].

**The delivery.** He handed over to Chełmski the pine forest and wood called Lusnia, as belonging to Łukomia from of old, with the Olęder settlement founded in it not long before, which the settlers now call Olędry. First a house built for the sale of drink, with a new stable and no keeper; then the settlers, twelve of them, by name: Michał Kinos, Henryk Bedka, Jan and Krystyan Tulka, Bogusław Zelmer (written Celmer), Samuel Frinderberg, Bogusław Effthman, Krzysztofowa Kulawa (a widow or wife), Maciej Klinczpor, Brudzewski, Antoni Pisarz and Wawrzyniec Welner. No one contested the delivery. An inventory was drawn up, Chełmski was left in peaceful possession, and the settlers were ordered to obey him. A week later the act was registered at the court office in Kalisz [[1]].
