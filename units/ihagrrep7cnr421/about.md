In June 1794, weeks before he joined the rising, the Starost Antoni Prusimski borrowed 12,000 ducats from his neighbour Stanisław Chełmski of Łukom and gave him a written undertaking that, if the money was not repaid by St John's Day 1795, Chełmski might enter Trąbczyn and its villages and hold them until he was paid. Prusimski fled, his property was confiscated, and Trąbczyn was given to the Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen. This file of the Prussian ministry is Chełmski's attempt, in the autumn of 1799, to recover the debt from the Fiscus, and the King's refusal. It holds the only copy in the edition of Prusimski's undertaking, in his own Polish. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

After the rising of 1794 the Prussian authorities in South Prussia sued its fled participants for the confiscation of their property, and in 1796 gave the confiscated estates to Prussian generals. A cabinet order and a declaratory rescript of the ministry of justice, both of September 1796, regulated what the creditors of an insurgent could claim from the Fiscus; Chełmski and his lawyer argue over what they meant. The papers are a ministry file: petitions received, the ministry's decision on them, the drafts of the answers with their registry notes in the margin, and the copies the petitioners enclosed. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**Chełmski's petition, October 1799.** Chełmski tells the King that he lent Prusimski 12,000 ducats in gold in 1794 against a written undertaking, that Prusimski fled before the day of payment and was condemned to confiscation, and that he himself took possession of Trąbczyn and Nowawies but had to give them up to the Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen after a judgment of revision. Referred to the Fiscus, he sued it; the Government at Kalisz first told him to prove that his claim was secured on the estates, then dismissed the suit as a merely personal claim. He argues that the cabinet order of 14 September 1796 makes the Fiscus answer for an insurgent's personal debts out of the confiscated property, and asks to be heard [[1]]. He encloses the Government's two decrees, of 10 and 27 September 1799 [[2]]. In the margin of his petition the ministry wrote its decision, and the King's answer of 21 October 1799 says it in full: the Government's resolution stands, since every creditor of an insurgent must understand that the costs of putting down the rising and the damage done far exceed the whole estate of such an insurgent [[1, 3]].

**Mondio's petition, December 1799.** Chełmski's lawyer, the criminal councillor Mondio of Kalisz, pressed the complaint again on 7 December 1799: the Government had confused the suit Chełmski first brought against the Prince, as owner of the Trąbczyn estates, with the suit against the Fiscus, and the ministry's own rescript of 1796 required a hearing where a party did not accept a dismissal [[4]]. A copy of the same petition, dated 1 December, stands beside it [[5]]. The office's slip of 23 December ordered that Mondio be sent a copy of the answer of 21 October [[4]], and the draft that did so is the last paper of the file [[7]].

**The suit and the bond.** Mondio enclosed a copy of the suit Chełmski had brought against Prusimski before the Government at Poznań, dated 19 March 1799 [[6]]. It tells the loan of 16 June 1794, the witnesses Jakub Bieńkowski of Mierzewo and Antoni Przeradzki, Chełmski's entry on the estates when the money was not repaid, the Fiscus's suit to evict him of 7 December 1795, and the settlement that left him to sequester the estates and take his interest at 7 per cent from their income [[6]]. With it is the copy of Prusimski's undertaking itself, signed at Łukom on 16 June 1794: if he did not repay on the eve of St John the Baptist 1795, Chełmski might enter Trąbczyn, Nowawies, Osiny, Łazy and all the Hollanders of the estate and hold them, without account, until the sum was paid [[6]]. The suit's own references, to the Fiscus's suit to evict him of 7 December 1795 and to a hearing "on 27 February of this year", read as if it was first written in the year after that suit [[6]].
