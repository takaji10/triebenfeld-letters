When the Duchy of Warsaw gave the Trąbczyn estates back to Michalina Miączyńska, born Prusimska, in 1807, the debts that Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had raised on them stayed on the land. The Prussian Ministry of Foreign Affairs followed her claim to have the estates freed of the Prince's debts, and the Polish courts' judgments of 1819 to 1827 that struck the debts out against his Prussian creditors. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The Starost Antoni Prusimski owned Trąbczyn and Szetlewek in the district of Konin, Kamionna and Kolno near Międzyrzecz, and Pawłowo with two further villages near Wągrowiec [[1]]. Prussia confiscated the estates in 1796 on the charge that he had taken part in the uprising of 1794, and gave them to Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen and to General von Sanitz [[1, 4]]. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen borrowed on Trąbczyn and sold Kamionna and Kolno to Leixner, a cashier of the royal bank [[1]]. In 1807 Prussia lost the province, and on 21 July 1807 the governing commission of the new Duchy of Warsaw put Prusimski's daughter back in possession of all the estates [[1, 5]]. The mortgages that Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had raised stayed in the mortgage register [[4]].

From 1815 the district of Konin belonged to the Kingdom of Poland, which the Russian Emperor ruled, while the estates near Międzyrzecz and Wągrowiec lay in the Prussian Grand Duchy of Posen. Miączyńska was therefore a subject of both states, and her claim reached Berlin through the Russian envoy. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**Miączyńska's claim against Prussia.** Miączyńska asked the commissioners who were settling the accounts of the former Duchy of Warsaw that Prussia should pay off the debts the men to whom it had given her estates had placed on them: 156,000 Reichsthaler on Trąbczyn and Szetlewek and 160,000 on Kolno and Kamionna, which Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had sold for 142,000. A report made at Warsaw on 29 April 1818 argued her case: she was an orphan under age when the estates were taken, that other owners recovered their estates free of debt, and that Prussia compensated the generals von Sanitz and von Zastrow with other estates and could do the same for Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen by taking over his debts [[1]]. The Russian envoy at Berlin, Alopeus, sent the report to the Prussian foreign minister, Count Bernstorff, on 20 January 1819 and supported it [[1]]. The ministry's draft reply of 11 May 1819 refuses: Prusimski's estates were confiscated by a court's judgment, the request has been refused before, and the decision of the Prussian courts stands [[2]].

**The ministry's view.** An official of the Prussian ministry who read the Warsaw report noted his objections beside it. He denied that Prussia is answerable to anyone for acts of government in the former South Prussia, called it untrue that Miączyńska had been left without a guardian and that the confiscation was against all justice, and asked why she had kept silent after 1807, when she was put back in possession. Not even Napoleon, he held, had granted a claim for what was done in the years between; Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, for as long as he held the estate, had held it lawfully and as full owner; and the owners had got back what was there [[1]]. The refusal of 11 May 1819 takes the same line [[2]].

**A creditor's suit, 1819.** In November 1819 Tarczewski, an advocate at Warsaw, told an unnamed client, one of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen's creditors, that his suit against Miączyńska was about to go before the court of appeal, and asked for the mortgage certificates on which the claim rested [[3]].

**Prussia's protest for the merchant Weigel, 1827.** On 10 February 1827 the Prussian envoy at St Petersburg, General von Schöler, wrote to the Russian foreign minister, Count Nesselrode. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had borrowed 30,000 Reichsthaler from Grotowski on Trąbczyn, and the mortgage had passed to Weigel, a merchant at Wrocław. When Weigel sued Miączyńska for unpaid interest, the civil tribunal at Kalisz dismissed his suit on 6 March 1819 and ordered the mortgage struck out; the court of appeal set that judgment aside, and the high tribunal at Warsaw restored it on 17 July 1826. The courts held that the return of the estates in 1807 had cancelled the debts raised on them in the meantime. Schöler answers that the Peace of Tilsit and the conventions of 1815 and 1819 between Prussia and Russia uphold mortgage claims, and asks that the judgment be annulled and its execution suspended; Prussia's notes of 1819 and 1820 on the matter, he complains, had never been answered [[4]].

**The judgment against the other creditors, 1827.** On 30 July 1827 the civil tribunal at Kalisz gave judgment in Miączyńska's suit against two further creditors of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen: the Lichnowski brothers and the widow of Franciszek Grotowski. The court holds that Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen possessed the estates only for as long as the confiscation stood, that his debts fell with his title, and that it must decide as the courts decided against Weigel. It orders both mortgages struck out of the register of Trąbczyn and Szetlewek [[5]]. No Russian answer to Schöler's note is known.
