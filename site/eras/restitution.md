In 1796 Prussia had confiscated the estates of Antoni Prusimski, Starost of Niszczewice, for
his part in the Polish rising of 1794, and given Trąbczyn to a Prussian general, Prince
Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, who borrowed heavily against it. When Napoleon
drove Prussia out, the estates came back to Prusimski's daughter without a court's judgment.
In July 1807 the Governing Commission, the provisional Polish government of the new Duchy
of Warsaw, extended to Michalina Dąbska, born Prusimska, the decree by which Napoleon had
returned Józef Wybicki's confiscated estates to him: the estates were to return to their
true owners
([the resolution]({{ '/documents/agad1174016/2/' | relative_url }})). The resolution named no estate and said nothing of the
Prince's debts. In October a judge took Trąbczyn from the Prince's officials; he never got
it back.

The debts stayed. The mortgages the Prince had raised on the estates, 156,000 Reichsthaler
by Michalina's count, still stood in the registers, and the men who had lent the money
wanted to be paid. Her answer, and her family's for thirty years, was that the confiscation
and the grant had lasted only as long as the Prussian rule that made them, so the debts of
the Prince fell with his title when the estates were returned.

She turned first to Prussia itself. In January 1819 the Russian envoy in Berlin, speaking
for her as a subject of the Kingdom of Poland, which the Tsar ruled, asked that Prussia,
which had confiscated the estates and given them away, pay the mortgages off; the Prussian
foreign ministry refused in May
([the claim]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/1/' | relative_url }})). Her courts proved
kinder. One of the creditors, Karol Weigel, a merchant of Wrocław, had sued her for the
interest on a mortgage of 30,000 Reichsthaler, and the Tribunal of Kalisz had struck the
mortgage from the register instead. The Court of Appeal at Poznań set that judgment aside in
1820, but in 1821 the Tsar ordered that the courts be left to decide, and in July 1826 the
Supreme Tribunal at Warsaw struck the mortgage out for good, on grounds that outlived the
case: the return of the estates was to restore the owner to the fullness of her rights, not
burden her with debts she had never contracted
([the report]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})).

Prussia protested. Its envoy at St Petersburg asked in 1827 that the judgment be annulled;
no answer is known
([the note]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/4/' | relative_url }})). At Warsaw the Polish
government told the Prussian consul that its Administrative Council had agreed unanimously
that Weigel must be paid, and in 1829 allowed its finance commission to argue before the
Council of State that the courts had had no right to decide the case
([the report]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})). Weigel's claim had grown
to 63,100 Thaler with interest; the finance minister, Prince Lubecki, called the sum
excessive and had the estates valued
([the letters]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12367/6/' | relative_url }})). The rising of
November 1830 against Russian rule interrupted everything; in March 1832 the valuation of
Trąbczyn was ordered again, and the president of the Bank of Poland was charged with settling
the claim
([the order]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12367/8/' | relative_url }})). Only in October 1834
did the Council's reporting committee answer: the courts had been entitled to judge, what
the finance commission wanted was a further appeal, and the Council of State was no court of
appeal; the demand should be rejected
([the report]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})). Whether Weigel was ever
paid is not known.
