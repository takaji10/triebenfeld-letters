The estates came back to their rightful owner without a judgment. In July 1807 the
Governing Commission of the new Duchy of Warsaw extended to Michalina Dąbska, born
Prusimska, the decree by which Napoleon had returned Józef Wybicki's estates to him: the
estates were to return to their true owners — a resolution that named no estate and said
nothing of the debts Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had secured on them. In October a judge took
Trąbczyn from the Prince's officials; he never got it back.

What came with the land was the paper. The mortgages the Prince had raised on the estates
— 156,000 Reichsthaler by Michalina's count — still stood in the registers, and the men
who held them wanted to be paid. Her answer, and her family's for thirty years, was the
argument that became the era's doctrine: the confiscation and the grant had lasted only
as long as the order that produced them, so the debts of the intervening owner fell with
his title when the estates were returned.

She opened the campaign against Prussia itself. In January 1819, through the Russian
envoy in Berlin, she demanded that Prussia — which had confiscated the estates and given
them away — pay the mortgages off; the ministry refused in May
([the file on her claim]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/1/' | relative_url }})). The
Polish courts then gave the argument its shape. The Court of Appeal at Poznań held in
1820 that a claim arising from a mortgage obligation belongs before the courts, and
annulled the Tribunal of Kalisz's judgment against her
([the report that records it]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})); in
1821 Tsar Alexander ordered that the judicial course not be obstructed; and in July 1826
the Supreme Tribunal at Warsaw confirmed the striking of the mortgage of the merchant
Weigel of Wrocław in grounds that outlived the case: the restitution was to reinstate the
owner in the fullness of his rights, not burden him with debts he had never contracted
([the sentence]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})).

Prussia did not let go. Its envoy at St Petersburg asked in 1827 that the judgment be
annulled; the file holds no answer
([the note]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/5/' | relative_url }})). At Warsaw the
Polish government, embarrassed by its own courts, authorised a conflict of jurisdiction
in 1829 — while telling the Prussian consul that the Council had agreed unanimously that
payment must be made
([the report]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})). Weigel's claim had
grown to 63,100 Thaler with interest; the finance minister, Prince Lubecki, called the
sum excessive and had the estates valued
([the letters]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12367/6/' | relative_url }})). The November
Uprising of 1830 interrupted everything; in March 1832 the valuation of Trąbczyn was
ordered again, and the president of the Bank of Poland was charged with settling the
claim
([the file's second volume]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12367/9/' | relative_url }})).
The Council of State's Instruction Commission answered the conflict of jurisdiction only
in October 1834: none existed, the recourse was in substance an appeal in cassation,
which the Council by its organisation is not, and the demand should be set aside as based
on no legal principle
([the report]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})). The scans end there,
and do not show whether Weigel was ever paid.

The records of these years are at the Central Archives of Historical Records in Warsaw
and in the Prussian foreign ministry's files now at the Geheimes Staatsarchiv in Berlin;
the edition has both sides of the argument, in the files the two states kept.
