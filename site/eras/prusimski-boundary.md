A boundary in this country was a physical thing before it was a line on paper: mounds of
earth thrown up along an agreed line, notches cut into trees, and witnesses old enough to
remember where both had stood. Trąbczyn and Łukom were two estates whose boundary had
never been fixed with care. The argument about it was under way by June 1589,
when Albertus Trąmpczyński charges Thomas Łukomski before the land court at Konin with
building a pond at Łukom that flooded woodland and meadow of Trąbczyn, Nowa Wieś and
Osiny ([the order for an inspection]({{ '/documents/app536017/1/' | relative_url }})).

By 1644 the quarrel had changed shape. Jerzy Chełmski, now owner of Łukom, charges four
Trąmpczyński brothers of Trąbczyn, sons of the late Stanisław, with sending their subjects into his wood to cut some
eighty boundary marks into the trees — taking a large part of his ground, as he says —
and with felling about a hundred building trees; they do not appear, and the court
condemns them by default
([the judgments]({{ '/documents/app536036/1/' | relative_url }})). From then on the
dispute is about where the boundary runs, and everything else follows from that.

By the 1720s Trąbczyn belongs to the Prusimski family. The methods of the quarrel stay
physical: in 1728 two brothers, Franciszek and Józef Chełmski of Łukom, charge Krzysztof Prusimski and his son Antoni
with burning shingles and a hut on their ground, seizing four oxen in an armed raid and
carting off hay, and the court sends a sworn commissioner to examine old witnesses on
oath about who owns the disputed ground
([the decree]({{ '/documents/app536040/1/' | relative_url }})). In 1767 the new heir,
Antoni Prusimski, learns from his court messenger that an embankment raised by the people
of Łukom has backed the water up and flooded the [Olęder]({{ '/glossary/#haulaender' | relative_url }})
settlers in the Trąbczyn woods, with their fields and meadows
([the reports]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr114/1/' | relative_url }})).

The wood called Lusnia — pine, with an Olęder settlement lately founded in it, a tavern
and twelve settlers — changed hands by force of decree in 1771: Stanisław Chełmski, who
had won before the Crown Tribunal, the kingdom's highest court, is put in possession of it by a court officer, and the settlers are
ordered to obey him ([the delivery]({{ '/documents/app53150kaliszgr414/1/' | relative_url }})).
Six weeks later his own messenger reports the dam of a pond on the disputed ground dug through and the fish let out,
loopholes cut in the tavern's walls and its windows shot to pieces — the work, he says,
of more than thirty Trąbczyn subjects in Prusimski's presence
([the reports]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr115/5/' | relative_url }})).

Stanisław Chełmski's claims on Trąbczyn were not only about land. In September 1768 a merchant of Witkowo, Jan Tracholz,
made over to him, before the municipal court of Pyzdry, what he claimed from the late Paweł Prusimski, Antoni's uncle and
heir of Trąbczyn before him: 1,053 imperials, 13 groszy and (it seems) 6 Pfennig for pine timber Paweł had seized in the
Trąbczyn forests, and 1,513 złoty for sawn goods and other outlays
([the transfer]({{ '/documents/app5321pyzdrygr75/1/' | relative_url }})). On those claims Chełmski sued Antoni
Prusimski, in the decree of October 1777 and the citations that followed; the suit was still before the courts in 1786
([the decree]({{ '/documents/app536046/2/' | relative_url }})).

The Polish parliament tried to end the matter. The commission it appointed met in the
forest between the two estates in September 1775 and split: the commissioners under
Ludwik Dąmbski, voivode (governor) of Brześć Kujawski, found Antoni Prusimski's boundary line the
better, had mounds raised along it and sentenced Stanisław Chełmski to the tower for an armed raid on a tavern of Prusimski's; a second set of
commissioners, whom Chełmski had brought, gave a decree of their own for his line
([the decree]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr145/1/' | relative_url }})). Each side
protested against the other's decree, and nothing was settled — while a second quarrel
opened on the money side, Chełmski having bought a merchant's claim against Antoni's late
uncle Paweł and sued on it
([the suit]({{ '/documents/app536046/2/' | relative_url }})).

A law of 1776 sent disputes left with two contradictory decrees to the Crown Tribunal, and
its decision of 23 January 1782 is the dispute's turning point: the corner where Trąbczyn,
Łukom and the abbey of Ląd's village of Drzewce met is fixed as Prusimski's commissioners had it, a straight line is drawn
between the two points on the map, and the Olęder settlers Chełmski had taken are ordered
back to Prusimski, all but one, Martin Hunt
([the Tribunal's decree]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr153/1/' | relative_url }})).
Carrying the line out took the rest of the year. Two boundary chamberlains, the officers who marked boundaries on the ground, one engaged
by each side, disagreed; Chełmski in person pushed away a man holding the surveyor's chain
and kicked it off the line, and the chamberlain on Prusimski's side stopped and asked the
Polish army's War Department for soldiers to protect the work
([the protests]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr118/17/' | relative_url }})). The Tribunal
quashed both chamberlains' acts, and the boundary chamberlain of Gniezno then did the
work: forty-one mounds thrown up along the line, thirty-one stakes driven into the marsh,
the older mounds of both sides levelled, and Martin Hunt delivered
([the decree]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr153/2/' | relative_url }})). Two centuries of
dispute were decided.

The decree of 1782 settled the boundary in law, but the quarrels between the two estates did not end with it. On the day the boundary chamberlain of Gniezno opened his court, Chełmski's people felled
221 pines on Trąbczyn's side of the new line
([the protest]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr119/1/' | relative_url }})); suits and
counter-suits followed through 1783. In June 1788 Prusimski's forest keeper stops a peasant of Łomowo, one of
Chełmski's villages, carting a felled building tree out of the pine wood, and cart, oxen,
cow and mare are shown at the court office at Konin as proof of the theft and given back
([the entry]({{ '/documents/app53170koningr121/1/' | relative_url }})). Six years later
Prusimski joined the Polish rising of 1794, and Prussia, which had taken the region in
1793, confiscated his estates.

### The succession of the Prusimski brothers

A second quarrel of the same years ran inside the family. The brothers Antoni and Paweł
Prusimski died without children, and their whole substance, paternal and maternal, had
to be divided as they had shared it between them in 1740. The heirs of their three sisters
sued for their shares; the abbey of Ląd was summoned over the family's jewels and silver
deposited in its treasury; and a claim of the Radoliński family to 43,000 złoty on
Trąbczyn, which the other side said rested on a forged letter, the court left undecided. On
29 March 1784 the castellany court of Poznań sat at Trąbczyn and gave its decree: their
mother's fortune was shared equally among all six children, and their father's estates went to the brothers' nephew,
Antoni in Kolno Prusimski, [starost]({{ '/glossary/#starost' | relative_url }}) of Niszczewice
([the decree]({{ '/documents/app5320pozgr1181/1/' | relative_url }})).

### The parish

When the archbishop's visitors inspected the parishes of the Konin deanery in 1790 and
1791, they found at Trąbczyn the church of St Martin, consecrated in 1546, built in part by the
Trąmpczyński family and enlarged by Katarzyna Prusimska, Paweł's widow; a priest presented by the
heir; and 760 souls in the villages of the parish, the dissidents (Protestants) among them living
almost all in the Olęder settlements
([the visitation]({{ '/documents/adwl85/1/' | relative_url }})).
