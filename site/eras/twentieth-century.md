By the twentieth century the manor of Trąbczyn had passed out of the family that had
fought for it since 1807. Its remaining land was bought by a man named Jesse and called
Włodzimierow, and about 1911 it too was parcelled out, almost all of it to German
settlers ([Breyer's second notebook]({{ '/documents/app539680669/2/' | relative_url }})).
The [Olęder](/glossary/#haulaender) settlements of the estate — Łazińsk and Emilienheim
among them — were now villages of the commune of Trąbczyn in the Polish state.

What survives of them in this archive is the work of a visitor. Albert Breyer, a
historian of the German settlements in Poland from Königsberg, went to Łazińsk and
Emilienheim in July 1936 and wrote down what the settlers told him: about their land and
their schools, who had gone to Volhynia, Russia, America or Brazil, how they spoke, and
how they marked their graves
([his notebooks]({{ '/sources/app539680669/' | relative_url }})). In a notebook of 1935
and 1936 he counted the farmers of Łazińsk — 87 in 1840, 146 by 1935
([his table]({{ '/documents/app539680716/7/' | relative_url }})) — and took notes on
Orlin and Józefów: their speech, their houses and customs, their poverty, and their
dealings with the Poles
([the notebook]({{ '/sources/app539680716/' | relative_url }})).

His copies of the settlers' older papers — the liquidation tables of Łazińsk, Emilienheim
and Orliny — stand with the era of what they copy, not with his own; that is the rule the
archive keeps for copies. His notebooks are the last account of the settlements before
their world ended. In 1940 the German occupation renamed the commune of Trąbczyn
*Drommin*, an Amtsbezirk run by an Amtskommissar at Grabina; the name was confirmed in
1943 and lasted, like the occupation, to 1945.
