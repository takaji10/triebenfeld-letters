*The Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen years are one of the seven eras in the history of Trąbczyn;
[the story]({{ '/the-story/' | relative_url }}) tells all seven in order.*

In 1796 the King of Prussia gave the confiscated estates of Trąbczyn to one of his generals,
Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen. The Prince was deep in debt, and for
eleven years he and his agent, Peter Friedrich von Triebenfeld, tried to turn the land into
money: they sold parts of it, let the rest to settlers, and borrowed on all of it. In 1807,
after Napoleon had driven Prussia out, the estates went back to the daughter of their
Polish owner, and the Prince spent the rest of his life trying to recover them or to be
compensated. Triebenfeld told the Prince of all this in his letters, from 1798 to 1816;
the contracts show what was agreed, and the files of the Prussian ministries how the case
looked from Berlin.

## Who is who

**Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen** (1746 to 1818) was a Prussian general
and governor of Wrocław, with estates and ironworks in Silesia. In 1796 and 1797 the
Prussian crown granted him estates in South Prussia, the province formed from Prussia's
share of the partitions of Poland. They lay around Konin, east of Poznań, with outlying
parts further west. He never lived on them, and he was in debt throughout.

**Johann Wilhelm Glenck**, his building and economy councillor at Wrocław, saw to the
estates first: he had the Prince registered as their owner and made some of the earliest
sales.

**Peter Friedrich von Triebenfeld**, a Prussian Councillor of War and Forests (*Kriegs- und
Forstrath*) with estates of his own in Silesia, acted for Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen from 1798
and held his general power of attorney from 1805. He signed for several of the Prince's
debts himself, and so went on working for him, and being pursued by his creditors, long
after the estates were lost. Where this account says what Triebenfeld reported, it is from
his letters to the Prince.

**Antoni Prusimski**, Starost of Niszczewice, owned Trąbczyn and Kamionna before the grant.
His daughter **Michalina Prusimska**, later Dąbska and then Miączyńska by her two marriages,
recovered the estates in 1807.

## I. Confiscation and grant, 1794 to 1797

In 1794 the Poles rose against Russia and Prussia, which had divided much of their country
between them the year before. After the rising was put down, the Prussian crown confiscated
the estates of the landowners who had taken part. The Prussian authorities named Antoni
Prusimski as one of its leaders; he fled, and a court at Toruń declared his estates forfeit
to the Treasury. In December 1796 he was at Venice, where he went to the Prussian resident,
the King's diplomatic agent, ill and broken by the death of his wife, and asked for a
certificate that he could not travel
([the resident's report]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr1414/2/' | relative_url }})). The
Prussian authorities had summoned him to justify his leaving the country; the summonses had
reached him late, and he wanted more time to answer. He died, as his daughter later put it,
before he had a reply
([her claim of 1818]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/1/' | relative_url }})). Pachoński,
a historian, counts "Antoni Prusiński", starost of Niszczewice, among the Polish emigrés at
Venice at the end of 1794 ([Pachoński 1973]({{ '/literature/pachonski1973/#p-240' | relative_url }}),
note 10). Berlin passed the resident's news to the ministers for the new provinces in
January 1797 ([the letter]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr1414/1/' | relative_url }})). In
fact Prusimski was still living in 1803, at Dresden, when the King allowed him and the canon
Xaver Jezierski to return to South Prussia; his confiscated estates were not given back, and
the ministers who reported on the case set out where they had gone, Trąbczyn to
Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen
([their report]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/5/' | relative_url }}), [the King's order]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/6/' | relative_url }})).

In the summer of 1796 Count Hoym, the minister responsible for Silesia and South Prussia,
proposed that some of these estates, with others that had fallen vacant, be granted to
officers. The King approved on 23 July, and Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen's deed of gift was dated
9 August 1796 ([the grant]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3570/1/' | relative_url }})). Six
other officers were granted estates at the same time, among them Blücher.

Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen received what had been Prusimski's: the town of Kamionna and the
market town of Kolno in the district of Poznań, the villages of Trąbczyn, Szetlewek,
Nowawies, Łazy, Old and New Laski and Osiny with the Trąbczyn Hauland near Konin, and Brzyce
in Kuyavia, the region around Włocławek. Hoym put their yearly income at 4,000 Thaler
([his letter of 30 July 1796]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/39/' | relative_url }}),
[the deed of gift]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/1/' | relative_url }})).

A second grant, of 19 June 1797, added sixteen villages that the state had taken from the
church, among them Zagórów, Pszczew (in German, Betsche), Kopojno and Drzewce, because
Prusimski's estates had turned out to be heavily in debt
([the grant]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/5/' | relative_url }})). For both, the Prince was to
pay the crown a fixed sum each year and to keep the clergy who had held the church villages.

Putting him in possession was slow. In October 1797 the King ordered the war and domains
chambers at Poznań and Petrikau, which administered the crown's property in the province,
to finish the business: the handover had been a mere formality, and those who held parts
of the estates on pledge or lease had stayed where they were
([the King's order]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/40/' | relative_url }})). Registering the
Prince as owner took years. The government at Kalisz wanted a certified copy of the judgment
confiscating Prusimski's estates, and Poznań could not supply it, the judgment having been
given at Toruń ([December 1798]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/42/' | relative_url }})).

## II. The exchange scheme, 1798 to 1800, and the first challenge

The Prince's first plan was to be rid of the estates. They were scattered, burdened with
debts and hard to manage from Silesia, and he wanted to exchange them with the crown for two
compact Prussian royal estates, Krotoszyn and Polajewo, and so clear his debts.

Triebenfeld spent the autumn of 1798 in Berlin calling on everyone who could help:
[Cabinet Councillor Beyme]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/77/' | relative_url }}), the finance
minister [Struensee]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/79/' | relative_url }}), Count Hoym, and
Colonel von Köckritz, the King's adjutant-general, who became the Prince's most useful
friend at court. The report on the exchange had to come from the minister for South
Prussia, von Voss, and it was slow in coming.

For a few weeks the exchange looked settled. Köckritz sent word that the business
[could be counted as good as concluded]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/96/' | relative_url }}). It
was not. Voss reported that the state would lose 17,400 Reichsthaler a year, on a valuation
by a war councillor named Nöldichen who had, Triebenfeld protested,
[never inspected the properties]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/87/' | relative_url }}). In
December 1798 [the King refused]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/85/' | relative_url }}), and the
Prince was still [petitioning against the refusal two years later]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/101/' | relative_url }}).

> The honest Cabinet Councillor Beyme let me read the papers in the Cabinet myself.
> <cite>[Letter 96]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/96/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

From then on the Prince kept estates he could not afford, and turned from trying to
exchange them to raising money on them.

His title was challenged too. Michalina Prusimska, Antoni Prusimski's daughter and still a
minor, sued him through her guardian before the Government at Poznań, the province's high
court, for the Brzyce estates, which she claimed as her mother's property
([the Prince's letter]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3709/3/' | relative_url }})). On her
complaint the King called in the files in June 1800 and then ordered the case hurried and a
settlement tried ([1]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3709/1/' | relative_url }}),
[2]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3709/2/' | relative_url }})). In November the Prince asked
the Grand Chancellor, the head of the Prussian judiciary, for other judges, since the
councillors at Poznań also sat on the board that supervised guardianships, which conducted
the orphan's suit. The order giving the case to the Government at Warsaw came too late:
Poznań had given judgment on 9 December 1800
([7]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3709/7/' | relative_url }})); how it decided is not known.
In January 1802 Michalina petitioned the King from Dresden to hasten the appeal
([10]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3709/10/' | relative_url }})).

## III. Dividing the estates, 1798 to 1806

The Prince's answer to his debts was to sell what would sell and to let the rest in
hereditary lease. Under such a lease the settler paid a sum to enter, then a fixed
rent every year, and held the land as heritable property.

The outlying estates went first. Glenck sold the farm of Szetlewek to the miller Samuel
Lucke for 12,333 Rthl, of which 8,333 went to Ignatz von Radzinski, who had lent money on the farm
and held it as security until he was repaid
([the sale]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/24/' | relative_url }})). Kamionna and Kolno were sold
in 1804 to George Conrad Leixner, an official of the bank at Frankfurt an der Oder, for
142,000 Rthl ([the sale]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/5/' | relative_url }});
[Triebenfeld's letter]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/136/' | relative_url }})). In July 1804 the whole
lordship of Pszczew was made over to its townspeople and six villages for 180,000 Rthl and a
yearly ground rent of 1,000 Rthl, most of the price being met by taking over its mortgage
debts ([the contract]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/18/' | relative_url }})). The authorities at
Poznań refused to allow it in May 1805
([the refusal]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/23/' | relative_url }})); the King approved it only
in 1806.

Trąbczyn and Zagórów were divided among settlers, and Triebenfeld recruited widely.
Moravian Brethren from Bohemia, whom he called Hussites,
[came to look at the land]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/120/' | relative_url }}) at 300
Reichsthaler a *Hufe*, then
[went home to fetch their co-religionists]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/126/' | relative_url }}).
Mennonites from West Prussia
[offered to buy Zagórów and Trąbczyn entire]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/146/' | relative_url }})
and divide them among themselves. Alongside them were the *Hauländer*, settlers on a tenure
of Dutch origin much older than Prussian rule,
["die Hauländer oder Collonisten"]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/216/' | relative_url }}).

In April 1805 Triebenfeld signed a
[preliminary agreement (Punctation)]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/15/' | relative_url }}) letting 270 *Hufen* at
Trąbczyn, Łazy, Osiny, Neudorff and Przybysław at 300 Reichsthaler the *Hufe*, with fifteen
more every year in a rent that could never be bought out. New villages were to be laid out
in two straight lines, with thirteen free *Morgen* in each for the schoolmaster, the village
head and the churchyard. Seventeen men signed it, ten of them with a cross, Germans and Poles side by side, and one
holding, three *Hufen*, was taken by a woman, Elisabetta di Simony, in her own name. The
crown's consent came only afterwards. On 28 January 1806 the chamber at Kalisz, acting on the King's orders of October and December 1805, allowed
both lordships to be divided, provided that no peasant holding lapsed and that the subjects
received land in exchange for their rights of pasture and wood in the forests
([the consent]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/8/' | relative_url }}),
[a later copy]({{ '/documents/app539680801/2/' | relative_url }})).

The hereditary leases were all on much the same terms. The settler paid entry
money and owed a fixed rent for ever, and the lord took a tenth of the price whenever the
holding was sold. He paid the taxes, bought his drink from the lord's tavern and ground his
corn at the lord's mill. Two years in arrears and he forfeited the holding. He also gave up,
in advance and in writing, any claim to relief for fire, flood, war or bad harvests.

Where the land was still forest the terms were lighter. A hundred *Hufen* of the forest of
Drzewce, in the lordship of Zagórów, went to eighteen settlers with no entry money and four
years free of rent while they cleared it, then at 30 Reichsthaler the *Hufe*, half of which
they could buy out ([the lease]({{ '/documents/app539680801/3/' | relative_url }})).

One small lease shows the system at work. On 31 March 1806, before the estate's own court
at Mariantów, Triebenfeld
had let one *Hufe* of the Trąbczyn forest to Christoph Erbet of the Trąbczyn Hauland for 100
Reichsthaler entry money and 30 a year, the first three years free
([the record]({{ '/documents/app5371057/1/' | relative_url }})). Five years later Erbet still
owed the costs, and Hawich, an official of the estate, went to the peace court at Konin to
make him pay.

Not every lease paid. The estate of Oleśnica had been let to three townsmen, Giese, Bagans
and Gietzinger, for 20,000 Reichsthaler entry money. In September 1805 Triebenfeld
petitioned the King: the lessees had paid 10,000, raised from their own sub-lessees, and
since then neither rent nor taxes, and had sold off parts of the estate. The government at
Kalisz had twice refused to have the court take over the estate's management, and he asked
the King to order it ([the petition]({{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3705/1/' | relative_url }}));
no reply is known.

By 1806 a revenue survey certified by the chamber at Kalisz put the two lordships of
Trąbczyn and Zagórów at
[40,260 Reichsthaler a year]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/179a/' | relative_url }}), and the
hereditary rents from the newly divided land at nearly three times the dues from the old
manorial tenants; sixteen new villages and five hundred families were promised to the King.

The debts grew faster than the rents. Countess Schlabrendorff, one of the Prince's creditors,
[would not wait past Christmas]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/118b/' | relative_url }}) for
the 50,000 Reichsthaler he owed her. In January 1805, through Köckritz, the Invalids' Fund, the Prussian
military pension fund, agreed to advance
[150,000 Reichsthaler]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/158/' | relative_url }}), 50,000 of it at once
against a mortgage and Triebenfeld's own signature. The Prince had given Triebenfeld a bond
for 250,000 Reichsthaler, registered on Zagórów, which Triebenfeld could pledge to lenders
piece by piece ([the register]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14525/21/' | relative_url }})). At Berlin
on 28 January 1805 he pledged the first 50,000 of it to the Invalids' Fund
([his bond]({{ '/documents/iharep162nr295/1/' | relative_url }})), behind the debts already
registered on the town
([the mortgage certificate]({{ '/documents/iharep162nr295/3/' | relative_url }})).

> Any breach of word is foreign to my heart, and shall remain so to my last breath.
> <cite>[Letter 158]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/158/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

In April 1804 Triebenfeld sent the Prince a draft letter for approval, and asked that
someone else make the fair copies:

> I am as though crushed, and, since my good wife is being laid to rest today, I have
> withdrawn with the children to my brother-in-law's.
> ([Letter 124]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/124/' | relative_url }}))

The rest of the letter is business.

## IV. The entail of 1805

In his will of 7 October 1805 Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen placed his principal properties under
entail: they were to pass undivided to one heir and could not be sold or divided. The King
confirmed it on 19 December 1805
([the confirmation]({{ '/documents/oe1u199/1/' | relative_url }})). The entail covered his houses at
Wrocław and the park at Szczytniki, the estate of Bytków, the lordship of Koszęcin, and the
South Prussian lordships of Zagórów, Trąbczyn, Pszczew, Wieniec and Bątkowo. If the male
line ended, the South Prussian estates were to go to his third daughter, Auguste. He bound
them to his heirs in the same year in which they were being let out in parcels.

## V. War, and the loss of the estates, 1806 to 1807

In October 1806 Prussia was defeated at Jena and Auerstedt. Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen commanded
at Jena and surrendered at Prenzlau at the end of the month, and held no further command.

Triebenfeld never names the battle. At the end of October 1806 he wrote from Wrocław
[in relief]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/26/' | relative_url }}): he had learned through a
secretary that the Prince was alive, after rumours, presumably of Jena and Prenzlau, had made
things sound worse.

> My joy was now greater than my distress had been before, and I only thank God that you are alive.
> <cite>[Letter 26]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/26/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

By the Peace of Tilsit in July 1807 Prussia gave up South Prussia, and the Duchy of Warsaw
was formed from it. The province was governed in the meantime by the Governing Commission, a
provisional Polish government that Napoleon had set up in January. Prusimski's daughter,
Michalina Dąbska, followed it to Dresden, where Napoleon was then dictating the Duchy's constitution. On 20 and 21 July she petitioned
[the commission]({{ '/documents/agad11740273/2/' | relative_url }}) and
[the Emperor]({{ '/documents/agad11740273/3/' | relative_url }}) for her father's estates, pointing to
a decree of Napoleon that had given Józef Wybicki, a member of the commission, his confiscated
estates back. On 21 July 1807 the commission
[extended that decree to her]({{ '/documents/agad1174016/2/' | relative_url }}): the estates were to return
to their true owners. On 5 October 1807 Hawich, an official of the Prince's estates, reported from Pyzdry that
the Trąbczyn estates had been
[taken over by an appellate judge from Kalisz]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/28/' | relative_url }})
and handed to the heirs of the former owner, and two weeks later Triebenfeld wrote that
Kamionna had been [taken in the same way]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/27/' | relative_url }}). As Triebenfeld later told it, fourteen weeks after the peace a Polish
administrative commission took possession of all the estates without a judgment of any court
and gave them to Prusimski's daughter
([his petition of 1815]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/2/' | relative_url }});
[a letter]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/265b/' | relative_url }})).

Michalina Prusimska went by several surnames: Prusimska, then Dąbska, and after her second
marriage Miączyńska, which Triebenfeld also wrote Moscinska and Moszynska. In 1810 the King of Saxony, as Duke of Warsaw,
[refused a petition of hers]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/198/' | relative_url }}) concerning
Trąbczyn. In August 1811 von Sanitz, another of the officers granted estates in 1796, wrote
that he had heard from the Saxon court that Polish commissioners, on her petition, had
[ordered the estates returned to her]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/217/' | relative_url }}). Triebenfeld's
objection was always that she had taken possession
["without any decrees or judgments whatever"]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/215/' | relative_url }}).
Her own tenure of the estates, and the thirty years' dispute over the debts left on them, are
the next era: [The Miączyńska restitution]({{ '/eras/restitution/' | relative_url }}).

Even so, on 28 January 1808, three months after the estates were taken, the Prince's estate
court was still issuing settlers their papers:
[at Alexandrien]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/6/' | relative_url }}) under the seal of the
"Hohenlohesches Patrimonial Gericht amt Zagorow", and
[at Pyzdry]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/7/' | relative_url }}) in Polish, as *Sąd Patrimonialny
Maiętnosći Zagurowskiey*, Hawich confirmed a man named Gottlieb Sarre in a *Hufe* and a half
of cleared forest.

## VI. Lawsuits and arrears, 1808 to 1812

By 1808 the creditors had reached Triebenfeld himself. As Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen's general
agent he was
[arrested in Berlin by two French gendarmes]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/30/' | relative_url }})
for two years' arrears of interest on the Invalids' Fund loan, which he had signed for
([his account of it]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/42/' | relative_url }})). In 1809 he
[left Berlin]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/45/' | relative_url }}) to avoid arrest under another
judgment, [was robbed on the road]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/57/' | relative_url }}) of a trunk
worth 800 Reichsthaler, and had bailiffs in his house.

> Now I am a true beggar-dog, and must still thank God that I was not myself murdered.
> <cite>[Letter 57]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/57/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

On 7 March 1809, at Konotop, he gave Hawich thirteen powers of attorney to act for the
Prince in the Duchy of Warsaw: to sue to have Michalina Dąbska removed from Trąbczyn; to have
the court's management of the estates left to the Prince, imposed for unpaid interest,
lifted, the King of
Saxony having granted debtors three years' grace; to remove Honrichs, an estate official who had had himself
appointed its administrator and had sold land without authority; to sue two of the Prince's creditors,
Oppenheimer and Wolff, over 72,000 Reichsthaler; to collect the arrears; and to appear for
Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen at the Diet in Warsaw, where every landowner now had to appear in
person or lose his estates
([the powers of attorney]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/48/' | relative_url }}),
[a second copy]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/302/' | relative_url }})).
The Prince also sued for the estates themselves; the tribunal at Kalisz, the Duchy's court
for the department, declared that it had no authority to set aside the commission's order
([Triebenfeld's petition of 1815]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/2/' | relative_url }})).

By January 1811 Triebenfeld was, on his own count,
[conducting thirty-one lawsuits at once]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/212/' | relative_url }}) and
recovering almost nothing of what was owed to Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen: the colonists paid none
of their yearly rent, and the tenants of Oleśnica, Kopojno and Drzewce none of their lease
money, while taxes and interest on the estates' debts still fell due. He wrote from a peasant's
house at Świątniki, where he was recovering from a serious illness.

The colonists on the Zagórów estates, who held their farms in hereditary lease, could not
pay their yearly rent (the "interest" of their contracts). Grain prices had fallen, and
the estate officials advised letting them off part of the arrears. In 1811 Triebenfeld proposed
that a third of the year's rent be
[remitted as an act of princely grace]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/215/' | relative_url }}),
because otherwise, he wrote, "the people out of obstinacy give nothing at all". In the same
year, weighing whether Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen should keep the estates at all, he proposed the
opposite: to have most of the colonists
[removed by court judgments]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/216/' | relative_url }})
and the land turned back into demesne farms.

The settlers in arrears had signed away any claim to relief in their contracts. Six of them
had taken the farm of Althütte: Martin Giering from Racotten, Wilhelm Daniel Schulz from
Krolikower Holländ, Gottfried and Michael Dumel from Razoten, Gottfried Damt, and Christoph
Schoenfisch from Trąbczyner Holländ, none of whom could write their names
([their contract]({{ '/documents/oe1bu14526/17/' | relative_url }})).

## VII. The claim, 1814 to 1816

After Napoleon's defeat the future of the Duchy of Warsaw was open, and Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen
pressed for the return of the estates or compensation for them. Triebenfeld petitioned the
State Chancellor, Hardenberg, in Paris in May 1814
([petition]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/1/' | relative_url }})), and then went to the
Congress of Vienna.

He [reached Vienna in October 1814]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/242/' | relative_url }}), after
three days on the road, lodged at the Golden Duck in the Schulerstraße, and found prices far
beyond his means. Asking the Prince for another 300 Reichsthaler in gold, he
[listed his expenses]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/243/' | relative_url }}) line by line: the
room, the coffee, the one glass of wine he took for the sake of appearances, the servant and
the carriage.

> I have nothing on my back, and often nothing in my belly.
> <cite>[Letter 266]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/266/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

He petitioned Hardenberg again in October 1814 and in March 1815, the second time asking for
the return of the Trąbczyn estates with nine years' revenue, 36,500 Reichsthaler
([the petition]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/2/' | relative_url }})). Wilhelm von Humboldt
[took up the case]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/250/' | relative_url }}) and asked for copies of
everything, and Hardenberg was reported as calling Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen
[the worst-treated of men]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/262/' | relative_url }}).
Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen wrote himself, in April 1815 to Baron vom Stein and in May to
Hardenberg. He gave his loss as 325,400 Reichsthaler in the first letter and as more than
261,000 in the second, and in the second asked for the crown estate of Sokolnik in
compensation ([to Stein]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/4/' | relative_url }});
[to Hardenberg]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/6/' | relative_url }})). The replies put
the matter off until Polish affairs were settled.

Where the new frontier ran decided whose the estates would be. The river Prosna was to be the
border; if it was drawn as Russia wanted, Trąbczyn and Triebenfeld's own Blizanów would fall
[under Russian sovereignty]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/266/' | relative_url }}), beyond the
reach of any Prussian court. In February 1815 he reported the line
[settled against him]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/271/' | relative_url }}). The district of
Konin became part of the Kingdom of Poland under the Russian Emperor. In March 1815 Napoleon
returned from Elba, and Triebenfeld
[wrote of Grenoble and Lyon]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/4/' | relative_url }}) and of reports
that Napoleon stood
[almost at the Rhine with 200,000 men]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/286/' | relative_url }}).

In Berlin, Hardenberg asked Zerboni di Sposetti, the senior Prussian official at Poznań, for
a report. Zerboni went against the claim on 2 November 1815: the Prince's figures were wrong, he
had let Trąbczyn decline and loaded it with debt, and Prussia could do no more than ask the
Russian government to decide
([his report]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/10/' | relative_url }})). In April 1816 the
ministry instructed the Prussian envoy at St Petersburg, Major General von Schöler, to raise
the matter with the Emperor in general terms and without pressing it, compensation being a
matter of grace ([the instruction]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/11/' | relative_url }})). On
23 November 1816 Schöler reported that the Russian ministry had refused
([his report]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/20/' | relative_url }})).

The creditors were waiting too. In April 1816 the widow of Colonel von Brehmer petitioned
for the 5,000 Reichsthaler her brother had lent Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen in 1803 on Szetlewek,
of which neither capital nor interest had been paid
([her petition]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/15/' | relative_url }})); an official of
the ministry thought that any compensation from Russia should go to the creditors registered
on the estates.

By 1815 the Prince's letters to Triebenfeld had turned cold, and he
[answered three of them point by point]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/273/' | relative_url }}),
defending his conduct and asking for money for his children in Silesia.

> Your word was sacred to me, and upon that ground I raised the edifice.
> <cite>[Letter 273]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/273/' | relative_url }})</cite>
{: .pull}

In March 1815 Triebenfeld's daughter Charlotte wrote to the Prince from Wrocław for the
third time, unanswered; she did not know where her father was and could reach him only
through the Prince:

> My misery has now overstepped all bounds, the more so since I must now also give up the
> hope of making my situation known to my father.
> ([Letter 7]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/7/' | relative_url }}))

In December 1815, back at Blizanów and recovering from an illness, Triebenfeld wrote that a
daughter had died four days after giving birth, and that Barbe, a friend in Vienna who had
lent him money there, had also died. He wrote, he said, so that the Prince might
know [that he was still alive and where he was]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/297/' | relative_url }}).
He wrote for the last time on 31 January 1816, and by April he was dead.

The Prince's creditors registered
[142 claims]({{ '/documents/oe1bu9454/303/' | relative_url }}) against him, from the saddler Gleisberger
at Potsdam and the gardener Nickel to bankers and noble heirs, in all 1,414,377
Reichsthaler in silver and 17,874 in gold. The largest private creditor was a *Frau Charlotte
von Triebenfeld*, owed 112,000 Reichsthaler, the sum of an advance Triebenfeld had often
mentioned to the Prince; whether it is the same money, and whether she is his daughter, is
not known.

## VIII. After Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen's death, 1818 to 1860

Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen died in 1818 at Sławięcice in Upper Silesia. His heirs renewed the
claim, and Duke Eugen of Württemberg, a Russian general and the Emperor's cousin, recommended
it to the Russian government, pointing to two Prussian generals who had been compensated. On
31 January 1820 the Russian envoy at Berlin, Alopeus, answered that the claim had been refused
in 1816 and that the Emperor could not act on the recommendation
([his note]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/22/' | relative_url }})). Prussia took no further
step and passed the matter to its Minister of the Interior
([May 1820]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12765/23/' | relative_url }})).

The debts the Prince had raised on Trąbczyn outlasted him. Countess Miączyńska asked Prussia
in 1819 to pay them off, 156,000 Reichsthaler by her count, and was refused
([her claim]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/1/' | relative_url }}),
[the refusal]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/2/' | relative_url }})). The Polish courts then
held that the return of the estates in 1807 had cancelled the debts raised on them in the
years between: the mortgage of Weigel, a merchant at Wrocław, was struck out in 1819 and
finally in 1826, and those of the Lichnowski brothers and of Grotowski's widow in 1827
([the judgment of 1827]({{ '/documents/iiihamdaiiinr12366/5/' | relative_url }})). Prussia
protested on Weigel's behalf for years, in St Petersburg and at Warsaw, and the Kingdom's
Council of State was still weighing the question in 1834
([the report of 1834]({{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }})); whether Weigel was
ever paid is not known. That story belongs to the next era,
[the Miączyńska restitution]({{ '/eras/restitution/' | relative_url }}).

The same argument was turned against the settlers a generation later. In 1859 the colonists
of Trąbczyn sued its owner, Count Włodzimierz Miączyński, over their rights in his forest. In
October 1860 he answered with a counterclaim against 97 of them, asking the Civil Tribunal at
Kalisz to hold that the contract Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had made with fourteen colonists on 18
May 1805, which had sold them forest and marsh and given them free pasture and wood in his
forest, did not bind him: what the Prince had undertaken had fallen with his title in 1807
([the summons]({{ '/documents/app539680783/1/' | relative_url }})). How the court decided is
not known.

## The people you will keep meeting

On Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen's side, Glenck manages the estates at first and later appears, in
Triebenfeld's account, as an obstacle. Hahn, the Prince's agent in Wrocław, writes when the Prince does not. Amelang is the agent in Berlin. Honrichs and Hawich are two
different men, easily confused, both officials of the estates, and each accused the other of
fraud.

In Berlin, Hoym proposed the grant and Voss reported against the exchange. Köckritz, the
King's adjutant-general, arranged the Invalids' Fund loan. Beyme was cabinet councillor.
In the years of the claim come Hardenberg, Humboldt, Stägemann, Zerboni di Sposetti at
Poznań and Schöler at St Petersburg.

Among the buyers and creditors, Leixner bought Kamionna and Kolno. Cosmar is the lawyer whose
settlement of 1811 with the creditors Triebenfeld thought fraudulent, and whose removal
he pursued through the Vienna years. Weigel and Stössel are creditors large enough to be
negotiated with. Oppenheimer, van der Lahr and Schlabrendorff are creditors who went to law.
On the other side of the dispute stand Antoni Prusimski and his daughter.

Each of them has an entry on the [People]({{ '/people/' | relative_url }}) page.
