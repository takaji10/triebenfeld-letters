<p class="lit-links-head">In the documents</p>
<p>Żychliński (1891) says that Michalina, born Prusimska, the widow of Count Józef Dąmbski, married Count Stanisław Miączyński in August 1809 and brought him the Trąbczyn estates. In the letters to and from the Prince of Hohenlohe of 1814 and 1815 she is "the Moscinska born Prusimska", who holds the estates given back to her in 1807 and stands high in favour with the Russian governor general Lanskoy; a letter of 2 February 1815 says that "Moscinski, husband of the born Prusimska" and Lanskoy are bosom friends. Moscinski is the letters' form of Miączyński. The letters give no date for the marriage.</p>

The Prussian resident at Venice, Count Cattaneo, reported on 14 December 1796 that Prusimski had come to him in ruined health and dulled almost to stupor by the death of his wife. This unclouded domestic happiness was broken by the death of his beloved wife, and twelve years later by that of his only daughter, after barely two years of married life. Almost at the same time as this bright star went out in his lonely widower's life, Count Mieczysław's sight went out too. He went blind; but even this terrible blow could not break his life. On the contrary, from then on he lived a doubled inner life. The serenity of a clear conscience and an upright character spread over his face; his mind kept the freshness of youthful flight, which flowed from under his fingers in a broad stream of melody when in hours of longing and loneliness he sat down at the piano, or sparkled in quickness of view, deeper learning and knowledge of the world and of men, and in a wit that wounded no one, when he found himself among relations, friends and acquaintances. One often had to admire this aged but hale old man, who bore the most grievous infirmity on earth with such calm of spirit and such ease! Deeply attached, as we have said, to his native soil and its tradition, he knew Polish literature excellently and delighted in its treasures. He liked to listen to the songs of the country people, and from this folk yarn, from these simple flowers of feeling, he spun and then set his "Kujawiaki", now sad and tender like the quiet murmur of a spring rain, now full of that dashing verve by which the melodies of our people are marked. And so Miączyński's "Kujawiaki" will always remain a lasting monument to him in the field [printed "dzisdzinie", for dziedzinie] of Polish music, and will be a precious source of folk song for our composers to come. His was a noble and strong nature; he was an oak of past centuries amid the stunted generation of today, and he too fell, unshaken to the last moment by the storm of misfortunes, like a mighty oak that only a sudden thunderbolt from heaven or a cruel, treacherous axe can bring to the ground.
