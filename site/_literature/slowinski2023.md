A book of 96 PDF pages (printed pp. 7-101 and unnumbered leaves) by Mirosław Słowiński, Konin 2023, with the epigraph "Manuscripts do not burn" – Woland's word from Bulgakov's Master and Margarita. It edits a wall cache of letters found in the former cantor's house at Łazińsk and discusses them from the position of a regional historian; Scripture epigraphs from the Warsaw Bible of 1975 head each group of the letters (Psalm 25:4, 146:2, 18:36, 17:5, 71:1 among them). Twenty-five photographs of German soldiers under Petsamo/Pieczenga, from public-domain veteran archives of the Alpine countries, stand between the pages; their authors could not be traced.

### The introduction: the Germans of the Zagórów land

The letters came to light in 1995, when Bogdan Karaszewski, who had been given one of the four teachers' flats made in the former cantor's house in 1945, took down an inside door with its frame and found, in the space between two walls everyone had taken for one, a tightly wrapped bundle. It held letters with Wehrmacht field-post stamps, a baptism book (the Taufe of the kantorat of Łazińsk, covering 1912 to 1918, kept by the cantor of the time, E. Cellmer), altar cloths, carpentry tools – and, sliding down from above, thirty-one books, the first a biography of Joseph Goebbels. Karaszewski kept the hoard boxed for nearly twenty-five years and handed it over whole in October 2020 to the Dom Kultury at Zagórów (its director Łukasz Parus), which had announced a search for materials on the region's Protestants.

The introduction sets the story in place and time. In 1718 fourteen German families come from Silesia into eastern Wielkopolska near Zagórów; the lord of the manor of Trąbczyn leases them forest land and fallows in perpetuity, and the village of Łazińsk rises. (The book's form of the family's name is "Prusińscy"; the usual form is Prusimscy.) In 1746 the owners of the Trąbczyn estates, Katarzyna and Paweł Prusińscy, mark out further settlements, held under Olęder law: Evangelicals, free people, with their own self-government; in the last of the villages located by the heirs of Trąbczyn there were also Polish Catholic peasants. From 1746 to 1789 the heirs of neighbouring Łukomia likewise mark out new locations. "Olęder" was the name of the law, not of a nationality: it was brought to the Commonwealth by Mennonites fleeing the Netherlands, and the Warsaw Confederation of January 1573 is named as its legal guarantee. Following Zbigniew Chodyła, the book counts 637 Olęder settlements founded in Wielkopolska from 1721 to 1793/1795, of them 228 in the Kalisz voivodeship, and says the Dutch and their descendants were about one per cent of the settlers under that law. Peasants from Prussia settle in the ring of villages around Zagórów, Łazińsk the oldest and largest German-speaking settlement. After the Congress of Vienna the land lies in the Russian Kingdom of Poland; Poles, Jews and Germans live for a century and more side by side without mixed marriages or conversions, the children of the Germans and the Jews taught only their Bible and Talmud. From 1918 the well-to-do German and Jewish families and the pastors from the Reich leave for Germany; the German settlers, bound by language and religion, had literally hewn their place out of the forests below Zagórów, and when they travelled to Prussia they thought they were going on a visit. Then the Reich's agitation, the preaching of Mitteleuropa, the reports of the Konin police that only the Hakata Germans and the Bund Jews were hostile to the Polish state – and the letters' own witness that in 1939 many of the minority stood to fight the invaders in the Wehrmacht's uniform. Of the German teacher Gustav Tonn of Płatkownica's letter to Bruno Mielke of 15 July 1936 – "do not flaunt your Germandom... if Poland is to be our homeland and is, we must also be patriotically minded towards Poland" – the book asks how such counsel stood against the aggressive nationalist and Nazi propaganda then being carried among the German minority. One theme is absent from the whole war correspondence: from September to 15 November 1941 the Zagórów ghetto was liquidated and its Jews carried off to the woods near Kazimierz Biskupim to be murdered, "as if the thousand Jews, neighbours of two hundred years, had never been here at all."

The cantor's house itself: the first brick building stood at Łazińsk in 1839, and "a hundred years later" the community, whose works the cantor Gustav Adolf Mielke had directed since 1925, put up the new building with classrooms, an office and the dwelling of the schoolmaster and cantor in one. (The 1942 newspaper feature and the annex's German-era note date the new school to 1933; the introduction's "a hundred years later" is the book's own reading). Mielke was an active propagandist of the German nationalists and of Nazi propaganda, was made to leave Łazińsk in 1936 and settle somewhere in Galicia, and returned with the Wehrmacht in 1939. Through the war the school, the kantorat shorn of its rights and the Mielke family lived in the building. On 18 January 1945 the Russians came; in April every German farmer was expropriated, told by some that it was a temporary measure; the house was converted into flats for teachers. From 1993 German-registered cars began to appear at Łazińsk and the nearby Kopczyk, their passengers documenting the old farmsteads, sometimes to the point of the police being called.

The hiding place itself: the end of 1944, the eastern wind and the coming front; probably one of the elder sisters, Leokadia, was at home. The cache was prepared with time, technical skill and ingenuity – most likely filled from above, from the attic, and the gap plugged. The hoard held letters from the front of the younger son Julius Samuel Mielke – Bruno – and letters and cards from the father, the brother Gustav and the sisters Leokadia (Kadzia), Anni and Olga to Bruno. It also held many loose letters, postcards and documents of 1936-1939 (among them the permit of the starosta of Konin of 26 September 1939 for Gustav Adolf Mielke to move freely about the Konin powiat and to use a bicycle). How letters from home sent to the front came to be back at Łazińsk is not known – perhaps Bruno left them there on leave; the baptism book of 1912-1918 survived.

The library walled up with the letters speaks for the household: Wilfrid Bade's biography of Goebbels (Lübeck 1938), Hans Wendt on the Potsdam National Assembly of 21-23 March 1933, Treitschke's Deutsche Geschichte im 19. Jahrhundert with an introduction by Alfred Rosenberg, Walther Gehl's Der deutsche Aufbruch, Bruno Behm's Deutsche Haltung vor Fremden (the last a Tornisterschrift of the OKW). There are also Bidez's Julian the Apostate, Ranke's Geschichte Wallenstein, a life of Frederick William IV, Raabe's Chronik der Sperlingsgasse. Further there are the Bible of Vienna 1886 – the wedding present of 14/26 June 1892 to Samuel Julius Mielke and Natalie Tonn, with its handwritten family chronicle – two collections of sermons, guides to marriage and sexual life, songbooks, and Schlag nach über England. Of the authors: Gehl killed in June 1942 on the Eastern Front; Behm of the extreme Austrian right; Treitschke's essay of 1879 and its adoption as the Stürmer's motto ("Die Juden sind unser Unglück"); Rosenberg's Myth of the Twentieth Century beside Mein Kampf, a million copies sold to 1944. Bade, the biographer, government councillor in the ministry of propaganda, died in December 1945 in Soviet captivity at Kovno; but the book's interest is the hero of the biography: Goebbels himself, his doctorate at Heidelberg (1922), his fanatical service, his oratory – a figure that must have made a deep impression on the well-read household of the cantor's house. Why the cache was made at all – fear for the letters of son and brother, fear for the books that constituted the family's national-socialist identity, hope of return – the book leaves open, asking whether the preserving of Rosenberg and Goebbels shows that the terrible defeat had taught the expellees nothing.

### The letters, 1940-1944

Bruno Edwin Mielke was born on 8 September 1911 at Białołęka, the ninth of ten children of Samuel Julius Mielke and Natalie née Tonn (married 14/26 June 1892 at Pułtusk). The children's names and birth years are recorded in the family chronicle of the wedding Bible: Emil Leonhard 1893, Leokadia 1895, Florentina Amalie 1897, Gustav Adolf 1899, Olga Johanna 1901, Apolonia Dorota 1905, Hugo Georg 1906, Oswald Julius 1909, Bruno Edwin 1911, Jerene Anna Natalie 1914. He passed out of the State Teachers' Seminary with German as the language of instruction at Łódź on 28 June 1932 – "the State German Teachers' Seminary at Łódź", as he wrote in 1940, a loss of Poland from the pre-war biographies the book marks. For five years he helped Alexander Gross, the pastor of the Evangelical-Augsburg parish at Zagórów, and worked as teacher and cantor at nearby Anielewo. He taught at Konary in the gmina of Grodziec and signed a document of 1940 as teacher and junior school commissioner at Maślaki in the gmina of Wilczyn. Both villages, like Łazińsk, settlements of German Protestant colonists contracted by Polish lords since the end of the eighteenth century. His dream, running through the letters, was to study at the Evangelical-Theological Faculty of the University of Vienna ("I have a teacher's training, also in music, but I know no foreign language except Polish... I am no genius, but, as the late director wrote on my certificate, I am diligent"); the letter to the dean of 10 October 1941 asks to be admitted when the war releases him, and says that if he falls, his brother will inform the dean.

The hoard's letters to the sisters run from 1940: the Lehmann family of Dresden thanking sister Flora for a parcel two days after 22 June 1941 and thanking "our dear Führer that he attacked in good time". Bruno cramming Latin "for Vienna" on 8 July 1941, the antichrist to blame that he cannot be a teacher, his comrade Rentz fallen. The register of 28 April 1942 at the commune office with Gustav, Mundt, Grunwald, Schmechel and Stolp. Gustav's long letter of 1 May 1942 misses his brother at the school desk, is lonely ("Apart from you I have no one; our girls themselves need support, and father... sleeps and eats and reads all day; but he is well"), announces his resolve to marry, and sends food with the brotherly warning not to share it all away. On 30 May 1942 Bruno writes from Innsbruck that the training in the Alps is ending and the next day they move to Lapland – the 2nd Gebirgs Division, formed on 1 April 1938 from the Austrian 6th division, is to be the form of his war.

The letters of the northern front, from June 1942: the march ("By God's grace we reached Finland safely... two days by train and we found ourselves near a small town close by the Arctic Circle... a march that is to last many weeks"). The arrival report (about 300 km at up to 35 km a day, marching at night in the land without night, "the sun bends to the horizon, stays there, and after midnight rises majestically again"; the last 15 km by lorry with the other lame ones). The borrowed postal code and the smallness of the parcels (up to 100 g), the request for little biscuits baked of flour and dried. On the margin he writes that it is good to know Russian, for interpreters with an officer's rank have a much lighter life. The book sets this in the campaign: Finland's agreement with the Reich of October 1940, its alliance of May 1941, its declaration of war of 25 June, Operation Silberfuchs under General Dietl, the object Murmansk and the railway. The failed attack stopped by 17 July 1941, the nickel mines, the Führer's order of 22 September to break off, the Russian pilot's destruction of the bridge at Parkkino on 28 September – where Mielke's company was stationed a few kilometres from Petsamo, the front stabilized on this stretch until September 1944. (The note 20 records the present cemetery at Pieczenga: "Unseren 12 000 Toten Kameraden", built 2000-2001 by the veterans of the Alpine countries, with EISMEERFRONT 1941-1944 on a separate tablet.)

The family's July letters carry the northern front home: Gustav from Zagórów (22 July 1942), sister Anna and her fiancé Hans at the Łazińsk house, many Łazińsk men on leave from garrisons in Germany and France, the request for a photograph in uniform. Two letters of the father survive: that of 20 April 1942, written on the Führer's birthday and dedicated to him ("May the Lord God keep him in good health for a long time, until he has overcome all evil and we can sing with him the great Hallelujah of peace"), troubled that many of the locals avoid the church. And that of 28 July 1942 offering himself as a Russian interpreter, telling of the late wet harvest, and ending "Jesus makes miracles. Heil Hitler!". The letters of Olga and her husband Erwin thank Bruno for money sent and for shielding the homeland with his body; their little son is christened Adolf, the baptism modest and at home, the pastor's speech fine, the godparents Gustav and Leokadia. Anna's letters (22 September 1942 and later) are the morale the book dwells on: pride in the decisive fight, "when you return you will certainly be another man; conquer yourself", and her own plainness against his mysticism.

Bruno's long letter of 2 August 1942 to Gustav answers none of these (it probably crossed them in the post, or came back with Bruno's baggage): the move to the front on 22 August. The food (750 g of bread a day, on Sundays a third of a bar of chocolate and a fifth of a litre of schnapps). The old front soldiers' tales of the previous winter on the northern front, the preparations already being made in summer. It also holds the sentence that gives the book its title: "My hope and prayer is to survive this one winter, and if God grants the grace of victory again, my hope is not empty. In any case it is beautiful that one of us takes part in this, and for me it is an obvious duty, even if I, with my 31 years, do not entirely understand it! Would you believe that I already feel it?" The book weighs the sentence at length: pride, or the question rising from the subconscious what he is taking part in; the greater enemy is nature; the private who has not yet met the war machine that grinds human flesh. The same letter carries his pastoral vocation ("if I return safe and well, I shall prepare myself for the most beautiful of professions... to carry it out with the greatest blessing; my heart is full of longing for a life dedicated to God - and as a pastor I can do it best of all"). It carries as well his conditions for a wife (she must belong to the Saviour and be pious). It also gives his advice to Gustav about Truthilde ("watch her and talk thoroughly with her, how far she is sanctified by Christ"). Gustav, 43, the cantor, teacher and schoolmaster of Łazińsk since 1925, still seeking a wife. Bruno's counsel unvarying: she must be a deeply believing soul, and it would be good if she were not plain.

The pastor Gross writes from Zagórów on 13 October 1942 of the intercessory prayer held at Łazińsk on 27 September and of the Sunday prayers for the men at the distant front. The December letters bear "Eastern Front": Bruno chooses to be at the front for Christmas as a small offering for his family and people ("the days of the feast I shall not spend in sadness; that is God's will and my superiors sent me to the front"). He owns his stretch is a quiet one, describes the watch of six to eight hours, the food and firewood carried, the sleeping, the late newspapers. He asks Gustav not to petition for his release: at the front in winter it is better than the training and drill of the rear. The book sets this against Stalingrad, whose decisive phase the December of 1942 was, and against the silence of the propaganda; Bruno asks for the Führer's speech of 1 October and clippings of the speeches of Goebbels, Göring and Ribbentrop, and plans his correspondence with prospective wives. The Christmas parcel from home (a three-kilogram parcel with a fir wreath with an angel's head and lametta, fixed to a locker made of an empty grenade box, colleagues given a bite to taste). The Wehrmacht's own Christmas parcels (snuff, cigarettes, sweets, chocolate, darning wool, envelopes, a comb, a bottle of vodka and of wine). The lice ointment that works ("I washed on Saturday, smeared myself all over and put on fresh linen; now no louse bites"), the day reversed by the polar night (dinner at six in the evening, supper at three in the night, breakfast at one in the afternoon). The promotion to Oberschütze of 1 December 1942 after eight months of service, read out at his post in the presence of the enemy by Captain Lebech and Lieutenant Hensler. Before this, the captain asked Mielke what class of the nationality list he belonged to: the Volksliste. The Polish school year's letters come: Anna Steinbart of Konary, his successor at the school ("I shall probably not be your successor much longer"), the director of the Łódź seminary Erich Kunitzer with his son Friedrich fighting in the Kalmyk steppe and "I too would rather die at the front than fall under the rule of the Jews". And Gustav's December letter, summoned to the education office at Konin, sworn in as a tenured official, "a teacher of the Führer", with its cold closing news: "Again and again come word that some of Łazińsk or the neighbourhood have fallen on the Eastern Front. The fighting is not easy. Not a few of them die."

The New Year's letters of January 1943: Bruno's short letter of 20 December received on 5 January (the shell that struck his main post while he prayed, "by mistake", in the side bunker). The birthday parcel; Bruno's anxiety of 10 January for his sister's soul – her fiancé Hans "rejects the teaching of Scripture and wants to protect you from such things", and Bruno means to give them a Bible as a wedding present and will not come to the wedding. Anna's steady answer ("If I left Hans for your sakes I should gain nothing... he loves me too much to force me into anything; am I to wait for a pastor, who may more than once have been insincere? I do not want to be forty or more like my sisters... he is at least loyal and trustworthy, and in no case an unbeliever"), and her warning against his religious mysticism ("you think you are no dreamer, but I think you are not far from it; forgive me if I give you pain"). Bruno's letter of 20 January on his New Year's Eve ("at ten I could vanish behind one of the snowed-up hills and receive the year 1943 praying, from God's hand"). It also holds his heavy brotherly counsel to Gustav, who carries some great burden between himself and God: go to the pastor, "he will summon the wonderful divine power (which Christ hands on with the office) and name the sin by name". The book reads in this the zeal of the future pastor for the religious formation of his siblings. It asks whether the kantorat's bans (no baptisms, funerals or services by lay preachers and cantors, the cantors separated from the Church as teachers – the ordinance of the Reichsstatthalter, Gustav reports, the neighbouring parishes strained since the summer) lie behind the brother's crises. It quotes Romans 8:35-39 and reads in the letters, under all the declarations of loyalty to the Führer, "a power above them to which this soldier gave himself wholly: God."

The front routine of 27 January 1943 (the snowstorms unknown in the Wartheland, the hoped-for relief into the rear, "maybe then my leave will begin, and when I return from leave, after your wedding, it will be warm and light; then the great offensives will begin and the war - God's grace willing - will end. And how is your wedding? Have you decided to go to Erika?"). Gustav's letter of 28 January 1943: Bruno's savings book at the Sparkasse (4,871 marks to the end of 1942), the oath of a tenured official at Konin, the photographs developed at Poznań – and the cold news of the fallen. On 30 January 1943 the Berlin radio plays Bruckner's Seventh before the first official communiqué of Stalingrad; no possibility that the front did not hear it, and to speak of the catastrophe threatened the charge of defeatism. Yet the letter that follows is of marriage: Bruno will marry only when released from service; the girl of Trzemeszno, Elfride Fiedler, housewifely, lively, talkative, of little education ("a good thing; I do not want a wife with a higher education to instruct me unceasingly") – and the hook: she, her parents and siblings belong to the Sabbatarians. If she decides to marry him she must go over to Lutheranism before the engagement, "I have not the least desire to go to church alone"; if she does not, he breaks off at once. The answer of the 23-year-old of Trzemeszno is plain: "I am a Seventh-day Adventist... we celebrate the seventh day" – and the acquaintance ends. Then Ursula of Lipice, leading a group of the Bund Deutscher Mädel, whose two returned letters she encloses again: love had blinded her. She has returned to her old way, "I believe in the almighty God who will not allow Germany's fall; every faith directed against my beloved homeland I reject... our Führer never meant to separate the Church from the state - on the contrary! In 1933 services were made a duty for all the party's organizations. But the Church rejected it", the Church cannot have German youth. The hierarchy acts together with the enemy states against the Reich, and when victory is won the matter with the Church will be settled – "you have sincere faith, but you have reason too, which lets you discern the future; formerly you sat in your village cut off from the events of the world."

Stalingrad and its shock (the generals Strecker and Groscurth ending their report not with Heil Hitler but "Long live Germany"); the reserve weeks of February 1943 ("today is the first Sunday - to my joy and everyone's without service... I went into the snowed-up mountains and found again an altar for prayer... I cannot tell everyone this; most would take me for a dreamer"). The propaganda's answer at the Sportpalast on 18 February 1943 (Goebbels's total-war speech before 14,000 selected guests, "Nun, Volk steh auf, und Sturm brich los!", the book edition published the same day). Bruno's letter: "that the decision with Russia and so the end of the war will come this year we all feel; personal matters must now be set aside; when all is over there will be the beautiful return home and, as the crowning of joy, my wedding". On 4 March 1943 the urgent order calls up the Volksdeutsche of the Warta Country to the 2nd battalion. Bruno is assigned to a heavy machine-gun as its operator, with the private Atze to help him. The hoard keeps the only account of a battle in the whole correspondence ("From 12 at night (from Friday to Saturday) our heavy weapons were made ready... it is unbelievable how everything roars, seethes, boils and bubbles, and when the shells whistle over to the other side carrying death and destruction. Again - as always - there were many fallen and wounded on both sides (among the Russians many more)... the Russian prisoners testified that in those very days the Russians planned an attack on our positions; only we forestalled them. Yet one feels so wonderfully God's care and love").

On the day of remembrance, 21 March 1943, no leave yet, the Russian's silence since 15 March read as exhaustion ("as Hermann Göring announces, the enemy is fighting with his last reserves; and we, by the totalization of the war, can still put one more army in the field; therefore we are all firmly convinced that the Lord will send us victory this summer and grant peace to the poor people on both sides"). The letter of April 1943 to Gustav asks how "total war" has affected their life, whether the brother means to volunteer – though so needed at Łazińsk, and with the wedding in prospect (the Wehrmacht will soon decide without asking Gustav's opinion). It reports the Führer's speech of two weeks before: "In this special spring day, when we have happily survived the winter and countless dangers, we should rejoice and be thankful that God through our Führer preserves us from the threatening evil, and how it strengthens us soldiers, that we do not bear our sacrifices, do not suffer and - die - in vain." Six days later the tone is different: no leave for Easter, "I long already for quiet, for the blessed hours... Łazińsk, Zagórów, also Anielewo, Białobłoty, Maślaki, Konary, Grodziec - all these are blessed places!"; the decisive attacks must soon swell in the east; his counsel that they listen and proclaim the holy word in the time of Pascha, which he now lacks so sorely; so they will arm themselves best for the times of drought that may come."

Then a silence in the hoard until the letter of 27 July 1943. The book fills it with Kursk (5 July to the end of August 1943, Citadel, the greatest tank battle in history, after which the German command never regained the strategic initiative in the east): "With us it is comparatively quiet... confirmation that the Russians are using all their forces in the south to win back the central areas. Here it is often gloomy and misty. Then it is dangerous, for the Russians like to attack then; but we are on our guard and our machines do not fail." His thoughts run to Łazińsk, where the people can quietly gather the harvest. He thanks sister Anna for the parcel and tells her he is no longer writing to Elfride - "not recklessness or indifference, but rather responsibility". On Sunday 8 August 1943, in the blessed hour of the services, he writes in a religious exaltation ("In spirit I see in all countries the temples"). He sees in them those made his siblings through the blood and salvation of Christ. He sees himself in the pulpit in the most splendid of all official garments, and instead of greetings asks them to read Psalm 23 - which stands printed whole in the book.

The letter of 16 September 1943, dated "in the field" - road-building, nine to ten hours with pick and spade, "I am grateful that the food is good (better than last year) and that I can work for Germany and not as a prisoner of war in Russia, as has befallen many" - is chiefly his text for Gustav's 44th birthday. The 36-year-old Elza, known only by report, pure in faith, character and virtue. "you cannot set too high judgements and demands – inner worth remains more important than outward beauty"; even at her age she may still bear a few children. The year's dark news behind it: Sicily landed on 10 July, the Fascist Grand Council deposing Mussolini on 25 July, Italy's capitulation of 3 September, the Duce rescued by German commandos - which fills Bruno with admiration ("It pleases me especially the loyalty of our Führer. This matter in Italy will at least become clear and further steps will certainly soon be taken"); Mussolini's republic of Salò. And Gustav's answer from Łazińsk reads the Poles' changed bearing ("One can rage that the Poles are becoming impudent; all the same I would ask you to confine yourself to quiet observing and reporting; provocative intrusion breeds hatred, and hatred is capable of much. Let the gendarmerie see to the malefactors, it is called to it. The Poles rejoice in vain for political freedom. The Germans are in form!") - and sends as a birthday present a drinking glass made of a cartridge case fired at the Eastern Front in Russia.

The later letter of Gustav Adolf of 12 December 1944 (field post 33 977, by then an artilleryman of the naval infantry at Hamburg) gives his sister instructions for the Polish girl assigned to the farm as a slave: "First you must take her sharply in hand, so that she knows her place. You must lock everything up and allot everything to her; one does not know whether she does not steal and is in league with the farmhand" - with a swineherding manual and the warning not to wait with the pig-killing lest the girl and the farmhand steal the pig from the sty. (The note 29 adds that the letters of Bruno and Gustav contain the seeking out of richer Polish farmsteads to take over, and instructions on "keeping the Polish slaves briefly".)

The forged document: a hoax of the underground's information and propaganda service of the Home Army (AK), planted among the Germans - the "Reichsstelle für Familienzuwachs" of Berlin, 15 May 1943, file I.A.4711, "Concerning the fight against ruin (of the nation)". The recipient is appointed a reproducer "to serve 9 women and 18 girls" for the birth statistics, his wife without the right of divorce, promotion to "officer reproducer" with the order for copulation first class with red ribbon for a second district, extra food rations - signed "Hüpfauf". (Probably the neighbourhood knew that Gustav Adolf had long been looking for a wife.) The book notes the RuSHA's selection among the slave labourers sent to Germany.

The autumn and winter of 1943-44 from Finland: the field post number 31 059 of the 136th Gebirgsjäger Regiment; the birthday money and the jar of little bouquets of berries found between snow and ice. On 8 November 1943 the tenth anniversary of the mother's death (she died in 1933). The Catholic field mass ("there too one can hear a worthwhile word"). The wood, the Gospel of the Sunday ("many are called, but few are chosen", Matthew 22:14). The letters to Gustav (the deferment, "as a teacher Gustav can do more for the community on the spot than as a recruit"; the lessons twice a week, the teaching of German to the weak, the library of 500 volumes he runs). The Feldpost-Bücher and the Tornisterschriften (Orłowski's Literatura w III Rzeszy, Der Posener Türmer printed by the NS-Gauverlag Wartheland for the mayor of Poznań for Christmas 1943/44; "the individual is nothing!... obedience and fidelity unto death"). The course of the medics he volunteers for ("the medical service is not easy and no less dangerous in battle, for it must be in front and dress the wounded – but is all the more beautiful... I have a very strong faith"); the stolen safety pins of the medical bag he asks home to replace so as not to have to report the theft; the field post's machinery (the OKW's care for morale, the weight and formats strictly kept, the post reaching the battalion and divided to the companies).

The Christmas of 1943 from "my snug medical room": no leave at Christmas (the queue of the older men; maybe January, "which may mean a long cold journey, but others also travel in winter alive there and back, so I shall probably endure it too"). The two current correspondents (a pretty and intelligent neighbour from Konary who is not a believer, and a Volksdeutsche from Wallensen who writes "a little unintelligently"; "but one thing is certain for me: I shall not marry before I find a believing girl"). The comparison with the comrades in the south ("fifty per cent better and more beautiful"); the commander's word that "the front from the Arctic Ocean to Italy can at any moment become open" - and the gift home of Der Weg des Glaubens, sent from Hamburg. The commander knew what he was saying: in January 1944 the Red Army lifted the siege of Leningrad (27 January 1944), Army Group North withdrew to Lake Peipus, Mielke's unit among it; in the south the liberation of Ukraine, Volhynia and the Crimea; Teheran at the turn of 1943.

The letters of 1944: 2 March ("after a heavy week at the post we have, by God's kindness, quiet days so far; every day is like a feast, like a present. Only hold out until Easter, and we shall have the winter behind us. Perhaps the next one we shall have peace already"). 7 March interrupted by the alarm ("we must move; hard days are coming again"; the letter reached Łazińsk 23 March 1944, by field post 31 059E - the 136th regiment of the 2nd Gebirgs Division, 10th company). On 12 March, reaching Łazińsk in five days by the field post ("How once more God's kindness has crowned me, that he has granted me (us) a free and quiet Sunday... and join the invisible prayerful community in heaven and on earth"). The cross of Golgotha letting sinners lay down their burdens - "the soldier Mielke grants himself full absolution". The five-fingered gloves he asks for (he has two-fingered ones, so his hands do not freeze); the writing twice a week when the situation is tense. The move 70 km to the rear ("The barracks are spacious and airy. For a whole week now we have been papering the walls and working at making them beautiful... Outside there is a thaw and in some places last year's moss is showing"). On 20 April 1944, the Führer's birthday, the promotion to corporal (Obergefreiter) - "after two years of service that is nothing great, but dear to the heart all the same". His first service as a corporal held in the melting snow, a tuft of moss for the altar ("That hour in solitude lets one see one's weaknesses"). The shame of sitting in the north listening to the pines while his comrades fight in the south ("Compared with them we are here on holiday" - "may it not get worse"). The company commander Oberleutnant Hengl, son of the lieutenant-general Ritter von Hengl commanding the 2nd Gebirgs Division (note 21). Note 21 names Georg Ritter von Hengl, the First World War flyer with ten English planes, the knight of the Military Order of Max Joseph, commander of the 2nd Gebirgs Division 1943 and of the XIX Mountain Corps in the period of the letters, taken prisoner in Norway in May 1945 - from Wikipedia.

And the girls: "lost" to Anna Steinbart, his hopes now on Elizabeth Kaschuba ("not plain, educated, an excellent housewife" - where did he have this from?), Gustav urged on with Erika, the double chevron of the corporal. The letter of 4 May 1944, by air (field post 48 341, the 67th Medical Company of the 2nd Gebirgs Division), tells that Bruno Mielke had been on leave at Łazińsk - he could therefore have left part of the letters that reached him at the front. It is the letter of the brother's soul: Gustav had received the Lord's Supper at Easter (note 22 explains the Lutheran Eucharist), overcome himself to strong fidelity to his God after long hindrance. "It makes me happy also because I can enter well into your situation and understand you and your needs." There was a time when the brother's departure from the Church aroused his great, oppressive care; on leave they had spoken of it with the father, who spoke anxiously of his elder son "with unspeakable sadness". "Papa despite his many errors and weaknesses is a dear soul! I am so grateful to my Lord that I still have him"; the younger son's patronizing note towards the father. Now the care turns to sister Anni, long unseen in church: "Is that not a sign of her doubtful inner state? Is it not the first sad fruit of 'Germania' with the spirit and 'religion' taken over from Hans?" The book takes the question into its longest stretch of commentary: nazism as a political religion, the völkisch primacy of race and nation. The criticism of Christianity as "not a religion of the sword" (Natalia Stawarz's Wikipedia piece, notes 23 and 26). The Deutsche Christen, the Bible scholars proving Christ an Aryan. The book adds Gustav's own letter of 1 May 1942 as its fruit: "Should wise men then at all have lived and held God back and said: What are you doing, choose a Saviour from the German people and not from among the Jews. But it pleased God so" - written when in Zagórów and the neighbourhood there was no longer a single Jew; and Gustav's ciphered question "g. J." - gegen Juden, against the Jews (note 24: the interpreter Jacek Kin's reading, which the author holds justified by the hoard and the cantor's other utterances); Orłowski on the brown poets ("The scale of irrationalism, brutality, servility and cultishness..."); Hitler as the Lord's anointed, the faithful dead as martyrs. Bruno rejects the new religion with the full force of a believing Christian ("We gladly want to do everything for the Führer and the fatherland, but to remain a traitor and Judas to ourselves and our conscience and the Lord of all lords – that is something no one can demand, and no reasonable man (certainly also the Führer) will demand it. If they do not want us as we are, let them 'release' us. Better to follow that simple, ordinary life than to remain with a broken conscience and without moral support in this bloody, serious period!"). He congratulates Gustav for remaining faithful to his faith despite the honouring with important offices - "we already fought in the difficult period of Poland, and woe to us if we depart from our so clearly marked way. The decision therefore lies on the other side: whether they will let believing people work, or whether they want to be rid of them" (they chose the second: from another letter Gustav was soon called up to the navy). "Before the war, in the difficult period of Poland, we remained in the faith, whereas in the young Warta Country everyone gulped down the 'new fame'. They abandoned it and departed far." The book marks the two languages of the letters: the biblical diction of the personal and confessional passages, the ready clichés of the Third Reich's language (LTI) of the declarations of loyalty. And the passage where Bruno prepares his family "for the worst" - the taking of the Warta Country by the Red Army ("How will they, poorest of you, then be treated by the liberated Poles!"). The prayer to forgive those whom their own conduct had injured. The shudder at remembering that he had once urged his brother to move to Konin ("It is God's grace that in this time sliding towards destruction we have this quiet island"; "may you live in Łazińsk many sunny and happy days"). The book's weighing: the alienation, the eksterytorialność of the land that fed them. The neighbours made slaves, the patriots killed, the houses and land plundered, and still the faith that the sunny land would be kept. The believing soldier who fights in the first line and kills, hard, no weaker than his comrades - "another matter is whether the light that shone for him was to the end bright."

The last preserved letter is dated 14 May 1944 in Norway (the same field post 48 341, the Luftfeldpost stamps on the envelope) - written after the transfer northwards. Three months later Finland ceased fighting (4 September 1944) and signed the armistice of 19 September, its troops taking part in the operations against the Germans withdrawing from Lapland to Norway. From 15 September the Finns fought on the Allies' side; in October the Petsamo-Kirkenes operation drove the German 20th Mountain Army out. Bruno writes: "How it will be for me here, and how far my wishes will be fulfilled here, remains in God's hands. His will brought me here; to His guidance I want to go on entrusting myself." Transferred suddenly to a medical company, he writes on the Sunday afternoon. The last sentences of the 33-year-old that have reached us show a man who sees the whole tragedy of the war, and a muffled trace of something stirring in his conscience: the two small fair Norwegian girls coming shyly to the soldiers' kitchen with a vessel. The roll of sweets given to the younger, who like her sister wore a little case with a Christ's cross on her neck ("That child I would not want to see again! (I prayed quietly about it)"). A wide search in Polish and German sources on the net for his further fate brought nothing; the letter of 14 May 1944 is the last material trace he left. "You walk in darkness, and like a coat of night is your unknowing" - the rabbi's words to Father Józef Suryn in Iwaszkiewicz's Mother Joan of the Angels (note 27). In the war's last year the 2nd Gebirgs Division went from Norway to Denmark, fought the Western Allies at Trier and Philippsburg-Heilbronn, and ended the war partly in Württemberg, partly capitulating in the Tyrol (note 28, from Wikipedia).

The way from Konin south-westwards to the gmina of Drommin (Trąbczyn), to which, besides Michalinów, the settlement of Łazińsk belonged, inhabited by about 170 German families to 1945. The year 1718, when fourteen German families came from Silesia with their village headman at their head and received from the owner of the Trąbczyn estate a great stretch of forest to make fruitful. The village grown "from a green root" (aus grüner Wurzel); the families Kuhn, Merk, Schönfisch, Haupt, met with in the neighbourhood to 1945 and on the ancestors' tablet of the Weber/Rhytthammer family. ### Where the documents say otherwise

The book is a witness of the twentieth century, not a document of the estates' history. Its introduction and its annex repeat the founding of Łazińsk in 1718 by fourteen families from Silesia - the Ostdeutscher Beobachter's figure of 1942, which stands against the records; the book is a second witness to the date, not a settlement of it. Its "Prusińscy" of 1746 stands against Chodyła's Prusimscy, and its contemporary note's "Andrzej i Paweł Prusińscy" against the introduction's own "Katarzyna i Paweł". Its account of the cantor Gustav Adolf Mielke: cantor and schoolmaster of Łazińsk since 1925, the new school built in 1933 by his means per the annex's German-era note (the introduction's "a hundred years later" against it). Made to leave Łazińsk in 1936 (the 1942 article says the Poles let him work four years from 1933), teaching again from 1940, called up later to the navy, the artilleryman of the naval infantry at Hamburg in December 1944. It is the fullest account of the man the 1942 feature and Breyer's notebook of 1936 (APP 53/968/0/-/669, "Mielke seit 1925") name. These Mielkes are one man.
