### 9 The Nobility of Poland–Lithuania, 1569–1795

Robert I. Frost

University of Aberdeen

#### I

> We live in the present, we know not the future: possessions are nothing but accidental, subject to all kinds of vagaries; but Nobility is eternal.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-1">1</a></sup>

Even for a sophisticated Frenchman like Jean le Laboureur, visiting Poland in 1645, the noble society of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was impressive enough to stimulate philosophical awe. In a Europe dominated so completely by noble élites, the Commonwealth was the noble state par excellence. Foreigners marvelled at what the Irishman Bernard Connor termed the ‘extraordinary Grandeur of the Nobility’, which enjoyed unparalleled powers and privileges:

> the Polish Nation is divided into two sorts of People, the Gentry or Free-born Subjects, who are hardly a tenth Part of the Kingdom, and the Vassals, who are no better than Slaves to the Gentry, for they have no Benefit of the Laws, can buy no Estates, nor Enjoy any Property, no more than our Negroes in the West-Indies can.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-2">2</a></sup>

Though this was an exaggeration, the extent of noble power certainly seemed impressive. Already by the early sixteenth century, the Polish szlachta (nobility) was a legally defined Estate, with extensive privileges at law.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-3">3</a></sup> In 1374, it was exempted from ordinary taxation on hereditary noble land without its explicit consent. By the principle of neminem captivabimus nisi iure victum, accepted by the monarchy in 1433, no nobleman could be imprisoned unless formally convicted of a crime. The 1496 privileges of Piotrków banned non-nobles from acquiring landed estates, gave the nobility wide economic privileges and restricted peasant mobility; the legal basis of

Gradually, the szlachta established control of the political system. The need for noble consent to taxation required the gathering of the nobility in the local sejmiki (dietines). From 1454, the king could not summon the nobility to perform its military duty in the general levy (pospolite ruszenie) without the agreement of the sejmiki, which were henceforth to scrutinize all new legislation. To facilitate the conduct of business, a bicameral Sejm (Diet) was established in the 1490s, consisting of the Senate – comprising Catholic bishops, government ministers and the palatines and castellans who were the chief provincial officials – and the Chamber of Envoys, composed of delegates (usually two) from each sejmik. By the statute of Nihil Novi (1505) all new legislation had to be passed by the Sejm. Following the parliamentary union of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania at Lublin in 1569, the Sejm became exclusively noble. On the extinction of the Jagiellonian dynasty in the male line in 1572, the right of all nobles to participate in royal elections was institutionalized; henceforward the election of the monarch viritim was regarded as the fundamental guarantee of noble liberty. Finally, the nobility took control of its own judicial affairs with the establishment of separate tribunals in Poland (1578) and Lithuania (1581) as supreme appellate courts for cases involving nobles, in which judges were elected by the nobility, not appointed by the king. These three developments extended noble control over the executive, legislative and judicial functions of the state. According to Laboureur, ‘There is no more true Nobility in the world than that of this Kingdom; there is none also which enjoys such advantageous privileges.’<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-4">4</a></sup>

By 1600 Poland-Lithuania seemed to be – and was seen to be – a state run by nobles for the nobles. Indeed, it was the extent of power and privilege won by the Polish szlachta which helped persuade Lithuanian nobles to accept political union against the opposition of a small group of magnates. From 1569, powerful forces of cultural integration operated among the noble élites of Poland and Lithuania, coalescing round a shared vision of a state based on the perfect coincidence of citizenship with noble status. This was justified by a heady mixture of classical political theory, whose principles were adapted to match a noble-dominated political system,<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-5">5</a></sup> with an elaborate justification of this monopoly through the creation of a mythical common past and identity for the new Commonwealth's ruling class. This identity-myth claimed that the szlachta, whether of Polish, Lithuanian, Ruthenian or German descent, formed one political nation, descended from the ancient Sarmatian tribe which had long resisted the Roman Empire. Although the szlachta enjoyed, in theory at least, a greater degree of social and political power than nobles elsewhere in Europe, nobility was defined in essentially similar terms. Szymon Starowolski (1588–1653), from a poor Volhynian noble background, who rose to be a canon of Cracow Cathedral, wrote that

Marcin Kromer (1512–89), Bishop of Warmia, stressed the importance of virtue, of living in a noble fashion, of land ownership, and of descent:

> The Polish nobility separated itself off for the government and defence of the rest of the people and of the country. Thus those who form this estate in former times were called knights and now – because of their duty to provide service as cavalrymen – are most frequently called equites, by those who speak and write Latin; in the mother tongue, however, they are called shliahta, from the szlachetność (nobility) of their family – in which, if I am not mistaken, the word was borrowed from the German (Geschlechter) – and ziemianie (the landed ones) or, as it is commonly explained, terrigene. This name comes from the lands and fields which they till and where they have lived so long, which they inherit, buy or are granted by the prince.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-8">8</a></sup>

The rationalization of the nobility's privileged position, and the idealizations of noble life offered by poets and political theorists were, therefore, highly conventional: nobility was essentially transmitted by descent, having originally been earned for the services rendered by a noble's forebears. Since nobility was in origin a reward for virtue and service, especially military service, nobles could be created, although nobles by birth enjoyed greater status than those who had been born commoners.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-9">9</a></sup> A nobleman was expected to maintain a noble lifestyle and perform the functions proper to his station: he was to live as a gentleman, to be based on the land and to defend the fatherland in time of danger; indeed, the legal requirement to serve in the general levy survived into the eighteenth century.

The extent of noble domination might suggest that the Commonwealth's governing élite was a stable and static class which, having apparently reduced the monarchy to a cipher and the peasantry to what Connor and other foreign observers perceived as abject servitude, had complete control over its own position, status and membership. Yet reality was very different from theory and, despite the clearly defined legal framework, the conventional idealization of the nobility, and the spread of Sarmatism in the seventeenth century, the Commonwealth's ruling élite was anything but a homogeneous class, and proved to be both volatile and highly unstable. Much of the apparent control was illusory, and the period from 1569 to 1795 saw constant changes within this élite, ending with the collapse from within of the noble nation and a fundamental redefinition of nobility itself.

#### II

The Sarmatian myth claimed a common origin for the whole szlachta, but the assertion of a common identity could not conceal that the Commonwealth was a State whose component parts, each with its own traditions and social structure, had only recently come together. By the thirteenth century a noble Estate with clear legal privileges, composed of the landed, warrior class, had emerged within the medieval Kingdom of Poland. The influence of the Latin civilization of western Europe was strong: many Polish terms for the ordo equestris (knightly Estate) came from German via Czech, including szlachta itself, and herb (coat-of-arms) from the German Erbe (inheritance). Yet while the Polish nobility shared many characteristics with the nobilities of Hungary and Bohemia, it acquired particular features of its own as a result of Poland's political development.

Originally, there had been various groups among the military entourages of the earliest Polish princes, and a distinction between those referred to as nobiles, who enjoyed greater privileges, and the simple rycerze or milites (knights). Already by the twelfth century the initial practice of temporary, revocable grants of land by princes to both nobiles and milites was giving way to the establishment of hereditary ownership. Despite the widely differing economic and social positions within the broad categories of nobiles and milites, several factors prevented the solidifying of a feudal hierarchy. From the eleventh century, the practice of conferring knighthood on a wide range of members of the landed-warrior class spread rapidly. The custom of partible inheritance acted against the entrenchment of the economic power of the upper nobility, not least because it all but caused the permanent break-up of the state when Bolesław the Wrymouth divided his kingdom on his death in 1138. This trend reached its peak in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth century, as Władysław Łokietek struggled to reunite the Polish state, which he ruled as king from 1320. The death of Władysław's son, Casimir the Great (1333–70), without a male heir – although it did not lead to the break-up of the kingdom – ensured that the nobility was able to maintain and extend the privileges it had won while central authority had been weak.

By the mid-fourteenth century, any trend towards a feudal hierarchy had been decisively reversed. Immunities and privileges were granted to the szlachta as a whole: nobilitas was now taken to apply to all who possessed land iure militari and who could, according to statutes passed under Casimir the Great, demonstrate noble descent. Even those who did not own land, but were noble by descent – the szlachta nieosiadła or impossessionati – were regarded as nobles, even if they did not enjoy all the privileges of the Estate. This trend towards equality of treatment was consolidated in the great wave of privileges granted by Casimir's successors after 1370 as the price of recognition as king. The emergence of noble clans, comprising groups of noble families of differing status under a common coat-of-arms as a focus of identity, encouraged the idea that the nobility was one estate with common privileges: adoption into a clan remained a way into the nobility until the sixteenth century.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-10">10</a></sup>

Despite this trend, the nobility remained highly diverse, reflecting differences between the kingdom's component provinces. The influence of magnates was greater in Little Poland than in Great Poland, where the middle and petty nobility was both numerous and powerful. Royal Prussia came under the Polish Crown after the Thirteen Years War against the Teutonic Knights (1454–66). It only began sending official envoys to the Sejm in 1569, and it maintained its own political structure into the eighteenth century. The continuing importance of the great trading cities of Danzig, Elbing and Thorn meant that the nobility was never in as dominant a position as elsewhere: representatives of these three cities sat in the Senate of the Prussian Landtag (the only bicameral sejmik in the Commonwealth), and the lesser cities sent envoys to the lower house until 1662. The duchy of Mazovia, part of the Polish state until 1138, was only reincorporated in 1529; it remained the heartland of the petty nobility. The dukes of Mazovia, engaged in constant battles with the pagan Prussian and Lithuanian tribes, had encouraged settlement of the forest wastelands which made up so much of their duchy by liberally distributing charters of nobility to colonists, whose numerous descendants defended their noble status with tenacity: so numerous were they that Bartłomiej Paprocki, who published his Coats of Arms of the Polish Nobility in 1584, gave up in despair when he came to Mazovia.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-11">11</a></sup>

#### III

If the influence of the Latin civilization of Western Europe was strong in the development of the Polish nobility, the nobility of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, while increasingly open to western ideas and concepts, emerged from a more distinctly eastern tradition, in which military service lay at the heart of the system. From the thirteenth century the Grand Dukes of Lithuania ruled vast tracts of sparsely populated territory including the Lithuanian heartland round Wilno and Troki and much of the western Ruthenian lands (present-day Belarus and Ukraine) which came under Lithuanian rule after the collapse of Kievan Rus in the face of the Mongol onslaughts of the thirteenth century. Constant warfare against the Tatars, the pagan tribes of the Baltic coastlands, the Teutonic Knights who sought to subdue them, and various eastern Slavic princes meant that the transformation from a military to a landed estate took rather longer than in Poland.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-12">12</a></sup>

The Grand Duchy before the 1386 Union with Poland was an uneasy amalgam of pagan Lithuanians and Orthodox eastern Slavs. The influence of east Slavic culture was strong: since Orthodox clergymen were probably the only literate members of society, Old Court Ruthenian – an ancestor of modern Belorussian and Ukrainian – became the official language. Western influences, however, were always important, through contacts with Poland and the Teutonic Knights. By the fourteenth century, the sources describe the élites of Lithuanian society in various ways: in Latin as nobiles, nobiliores, satrapae, potentiores, barones, meliores; an individual might be known as shliaikhtich, from the Polish szlachcic, or in eastern terms as kniaz or boiar.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-13">13</a></sup> Quite what these terms meant in practice is not always easy to determine. East-Slavic influences remained strong: the importance of princely blood was recognized, and descendants of the houses of Gedymin, the Lithuanian ruling dynasty, and Rurik, which had ruled Kievan Rus, were entitled to use the title kniaz (prince, duke). The Grand Duke maintained a small council of high nobles; otherwise, there were various ranks of servitors, who might be bonded men, free peasants or enjoy the greater privileges of the zemianin – a member of the military service class.

It was in the context of the growing political relationship with Poland from the late fourteenth century that a noble Estate in the western sense began to develop. Lithuania, facing fierce challenges from the Teutonic Knights and from Muscovy, increasingly looked to Poland for military support. After the Lithuanian Grand Duke Jogaila (1377–1434) converted to Catholicism, taking the name Władysław Jagiełło, to become King of Poland in 1386, he wooed the pagan Lithuanian boyars into adopting the Catholic faith in 1387 by granting them certain privileges, the most important of which, for the creation of a true landed nobility, was the removal of service obligations on private estates and the introduction of rights of inheritance. The uncertainty of Lithuania's political status after 1386 and growing Polish influence, however, gave some – but not all – lesser groups among the landed-service élites the opportunity to win an ever greater degree of privilege and control over their affairs.

For most of the fifteenth century, Lithuania was governed by various members of the Jagiellonian dynasty, while the head of the family assumed the Crown in Cracow. As ideas concerning noble rights and privileges entered from Poland, and the de facto rulers of Lithuania starting with Vytautas (Witold), Jagiełło's cousin, Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1392 to 1430, sought to maintain control by buying off political support, the position of the Lithuanian landed élites began to change. At the Union of Horodło in 1413, hereditary rights over certain estates were reaffirmed for Catholic boyars, and 47 leading Lithuanian families were ‘adopted' by Polish noble clans, assuming their coats-of-arms. Over the next 150 years, these privileges were gradually extended to wider strata of the military-landed élite. From the very outset the conversion to Catholicism of the leading Lithuanian boyars and the failure to include Orthodox Ruthenians among those granted privileges after 1387 threatened to blow the fragile state apart. Ruthenian boyars displayed growing hostility to Vytautas, which erupted into civil war on his death, fought between Śvitrigaila Algirdaitis, a younger brother of Jagiełło, and Žyigimantas Kestutaitis, a younger brother of Vytautas. Śvitrigaila allied with the Teutonic Knights and courted Ruthenian support; although he was ultimately defeated, the victorious Žyigimantas was forced to extend equal privileges to Ruthenian boyars in 1434 as the price of peace.

Although some Ruthenians still looked east, and there was a degree of support for political union with Muscovy until the mid-seventeenth century, from the 1430s the growing attraction of the Polish model of extensive noble privileges brought the élites of Poland and Lithuania ever closer, especially against the background of developments in Muscovy, where the behaviour of Ivan the Terrible (1533–84) created a negative image of Muscovite power which lasted deep into the seventeenth century. As the autonomy of the high nobles was steadily reduced during the fifteenth century, there was a series of defections of Ruthenian magnates to Muscovy, but the growing attraction of noble privileges granted on the Polish model restricted the appeal of that route; while the position of the Muscovite dvorianstvo remained uncertain and dominated by the service ethic, their Lithuanian counterparts began to flourish.

The privileges of 1492 and 1506 agreed that the prince could decide nothing new without the agreement of his council, but the growing power of the magnates was matched by the political assertiveness of the middling Lithuanian servitors, who were still required to serve and who did not enjoy the full rights and privileges of the upper levels of Lithuanian society. As the practice of meeting in local deliberative bodies on the Polish pattern spread, the assertiveness of the middling Lithuanian nobles increased. When called out to fight at the start of the Livonian War in September 1562, large numbers of servitors refused to obey the commands of their leaders, demanding union and the extension to them of the privileges enjoyed by their Polish counterparts. Following the definitive establishment in the 1560s of sejmiki, which largely followed the Polish model, the Second Lithuanian Statute of 1566 formally established a bicameral Sejm on Polish lines; 3 years later the triumph of the middling nobility, in alliance with the Poles, was crowned with the Lublin Union.

There were, however, those left behind, since the very complexity of the Lithuanian system of service made it unclear who, exactly, should be included among the new class of citizens. Sigismund I (1506–48) attempted to reform and better regulate the system of service; in 1528, during the long series of wars against Muscovy, the Lithuanian Sejm fixed the amount of service required from service lands for the next 10 years; in 1529 a list of cavalry servitors was drawn up, including 19,842 men from 13,060 families, of which nearly one-third were provided by magnate families. In the 1550s, under Sigismund August (1548–72), the last of the Jagiellonian dynasty, the massive grand ducal estates were surveyed as the basis for an extensive land reform which, by tying the peasants to the land, created a substantial incentive for servitors to demonstrate their status by performing military service, and in the great muster of 1567, 27,776 cavalrymen turned out; on this occasion under a fifth (4890) were provided by the magnates. For generations, the appearance of an ancestor's name in the muster lists of 1528 and 1567 was seen as a clear proof of noble status; many petty servitors, however, were not able to afford the equipment required, and as the Union with Poland both solidified the definition of what constituted a noble and extended substantial privileges to those who were able to do so, a substantial group of boyars<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-14">14</a></sup> were effectively excluded from the Promised Land of noble privilege. Some sank into the peasantry; others managed to continue their precarious existence in service to great magnate houses.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-15">15</a></sup>

The Union of Lublin was, however, merely one of the most important stepping-stones in the gradual – though never complete – convergence of the Commonwealth's noble élites. Yet integration had its limits. Local identities remained strong, and sejmiki provided an important focus for local political action. The truncated Grand Duchy of Lithuania which finally accepted the Union retained its own army, administration and law, as well as a powerful sense of autonomy. For all the influence of the middling Lithuanian nobility on the events of 1569, the power of the magnates across the vast lands of the old Grand Duchy in its pre-1569 borders remained much more substantial than in Poland.

In the heartlands of the old Grand Duchy, the transition did not cause substantial social disruption; it was, however, to be a different matter in the Ukrainian lands, where the limits of convergence soon became apparent. Many leading Ruthenian families, including the Vyshnevetsky (Wiśniowiecki), Chartorysky (Czartoryski) and Sangushko (Sanguszko), enthusiastically embraced the ethos of the szlachta in the century after 1569. This left large groups in the Ukrainian lands, who by their lifestyle were little different to the middling and petty nobility of the rest of the Commonwealth, poised awkwardly between its undoubtedly attractive socio-political model and its increasingly unwelcoming cultural and religious exclusivism. The emergence of Cossack society in the second half of the sixteenth century provided an alternative, in which some petty and middling Ruthenian nobles sought the military employment which was such a traditional part of the concept of noble functions. Yet the creation of a large Cossack host, useful to the Commonwealth though it was in time of war, was felt by many to be a potential threat to noble liberties, since the Vasa monarchs Sigismund III (1587–1632), Władysław IV (1632–48) and John Casimir (1648–68) all saw the Cossacks as a potential source of military support for their ambitious political plans to strengthen the monarchy.

For over half a century, the Commonwealth and the Cossacks coexisted in an ill-defined and ambiguous relationship which finally shattered apart in the great Cossack rising of 1648. The 1659 Treaty of Hadiach sought to integrate the Cossack élites into the noble nation by creating a Grand Duchy of Ruthenia and guaranteeing a place in the noble nation to Orthodoxy, but opposition from nobles in the rest of the Commonwealth to the mass ennoblement of Cossacks and the extension of privileges to the Orthodox Church caused its failure. #### IV

Nevertheless, the szlachta remained extremely diverse. This was inevitable, since it was extremely large. Noble status was conferred upon all children of a noble, both male and female; the szlachta, already large in 1569, therefore had a distinct tendency to grow very quickly. Quite how large it grew is unclear. Connor estimated it to constitute nearly the tenth part of the population at the end of the seventeenth century; from the nineteenth century, the figure of 8–10 per cent was widely accepted. As scholars began to investigate the sources more systematically, however, rather than rely on impressionistic estimates, a more realistic figure emerged. Of particular importance are the registers for the hearth tax raised at various points after 1629, the poll tax levied on four occasions between 1662 and 1676, and the censuses taken at the end of the eighteenth century; in 1790–92 by the Poles, and by the partitioning powers between 1772 and 1827.

Even making allowance for a degree of inaccuracy in the tax registers, which sought to list all members of noble households over the age of ten, but which were administered by a nobility not renowned for its cheerful acceptance of taxation, local studies have consistently revised downwards the percentage of noble inhabitants: the figures of 4.8 per cent for the palatinates of Poznań and Kalisz and 1.66 per cent for the Cracow palatinate in the mid-seventeenth century are significantly lower than previous claims.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-16">16</a></sup> Emanuel Rostworowski's widely respected estimate for the late eighteenth century suggests a figure of 6–6.5 per cent of a population of approximately 14 million for the whole Commonwealth before the First Partition in 1772, and 7–7.5 per cent of approximately 10 million thereafter. A more accurate general picture will not emerge until more local studies are complete, but it seems that a figure of 6–7.5 per cent of the population is more likely than 10 per cent.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-17">17</a></sup> Even this reduced figure, however, means that the Commonwealth's nobility constituted a higher percentage of the total population than anywhere else in Europe, with only Spain and Hungary coming close.

As significant as the size of the nobility was its highly uneven distribution, which reflected the differing histories and traditions of the Commonwealth's component parts. In general, there were fewer nobles in Lithuania and Ukraine – less than 0.5–1 per cent in the palatinates of Kiev and Bratslav – than in Poland, although parts of western Lithuania, especially Samogitia (Żemaitija), also had a high concentration of petty nobles. Even within Poland there were substantial variations, with fewer nobles on the whole in Royal Prussia and Little Poland than in Great Poland. The tendency of the petty szlachta to group together meant that local concentrations could be high, even in Little Poland: although some parishes in the Cracow palatinate had no noble inhabitants, there were 23 where the noble population was over 5 per cent, and four where it was between 8.6 per cent and 12.8 per cent.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-18">18</a></sup> It was, however, Mazovia which saw the greatest concentration: some estimates put the noble population as high as 20–25 per cent, and villages inhabited entirely by nobles were not uncommon.

No estimate of the size of the nobility can ever be entirely accurate, since it proved impossible in practice to establish a firm boundary between noble and commoner, despite the apparently simple definition of nobility and the control the nobility exercised in theory and at law over its own membership. The number of formal ennoblements did diminish after the king was deprived of his independent initiative in 1578: the Sejm created 334 new nobles, mostly for military service, between 1578 and 1601, and granted Polish nobility (indygenat) to six foreigners. Another 336 native ennoblements and 183 rights of indygenat were granted between 1601 and 1764.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-19">19</a></sup> According to the Third Lithuanian Statute (1588) Jews converting to Catholicism automatically obtained noble status, although the numbers involved appear to have been small until the 1760s.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-20">20</a></sup>

There were less formal ways of entering the nobility. In such a vast State, with such a large population of petty nobles, it was not difficult for a commoner, particularly if he had money, to adopt the hallmarks of a noble lifestyle and to pass himself off as noble. Since the bearing of arms had long been regarded as one of the central functions of the nobility, and since there was always a substantial number of commoners in the Commonwealth's armies, it was relatively easy for commoners with long military service to slip into the nobility without formal ennoblement, especially since they often served among petty, landless nobles. But military service was not essential: Many simply adopted a surname of the noble type and moved: Mazovia's numerous population of petty nobles was constantly overspilling into other regions and it was easy to claim noble status in a far-off part of the Commonwealth where it was difficult for checks to be made. Another favourite route was through service to magnates, who felt more able to rely on the loyalty of clients whose status was in some doubt. #### V

Nobility might, therefore, be relatively easy to acquire for those who desired it and who had acquired some, at least, of the trappings of nobility. Maintaining an economic position sufficient to support a noble lifestyle without recourse to plebeian pursuits was far more difficult. The nobility might be equal before the law, but it varied widely in its economic condition, from a tiny group at the top, which controlled vast estates to the landless gołota (the naked ones) at the bottom. The fact that the nobility paid extraordinary taxes voted by the Sejm makes it possible, where records have survived, to document this economic diversity. The act introducing the poll tax in 1662 divided the nobility into various groups. Possessores or posesjonaci – noble landholders in possession of allodial or leasehold estates – were to pay 3 zloties, and 1.5 for their wives and children above the age of ten. Impossessionati – nobles without land who earned their living through military service, or service at the Court or on the estate of a rich noble – were to pay 2 zloties. A noble landholder without serfs who worked his own land up to a fixed amount (about 16 hectares), paid 2 zloties for himself and 15 groszy for his wife and children. Poorer nobles – the szlachta częstkowa, who owned or leased part of a village, and the szlachta zagrodowa (smallholders) – each paid 1 zloty, and 15 groszy for their wives and children. In the palatinate of Cracow in 1662, just over half of the nobility (2275 out of 4279) were classified as possessores; 485 (11.5 per cent) were listed as impossessionati; 227 (5.3 per cent) served other nobles as estate factors or managers; 788 (18.4 per cent) served in a more humble capacity; 405 (9.5 per cent) were classified as smallholders and 99 (2.3 per cent) represented the poor nobility who farmed their land themselves.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-30">30</a></sup> The 1673 act renewing the poll tax divided the nobility into noble landholders (szlachta osiadła),<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-31">31</a></sup> nobles in service, and nobles without serfs. In the palatinates of Poznań and Kalisz, 5133 out of 9484 nobles came into the first category (54.1 per cent), 484 were classed as factors or estate managers (5.1 per cent), 3029 (32 per cent) as nobles in service, 361 (3.8 per cent) as poor nobles – mostly szlachta częstkowa – and 477 (5 per cent) as smallholders without serfs.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-32">32</a></sup>

The large number of noble smallholders and poor nobles was encouraged by the laws and customs surrounding the inheritance of land. The effects of partible inheritance were exacerbated by the protection afforded by law to the property rights of noblewomen. Normally, a quarter of an estate was allocated to any daughters to provide them with dowries, the rest was divided up amongst the sons. Often shares were allocated by the father to sons as they came of age, subject to guarantees designed to protect widows in respect of the property they had brought to the couple as dowry.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-35">35</a></sup> Partible inheritance tended rapidly to reduce the family patrimony and income; by the late sixteenth century perhaps the majority of the Commonwealth's nobility was poor or at least impoverished. The economic condition of many was often worse than that of peasants, and was frequently commented on by foreigners: in the 1670s, Ulrich Werdum, from Frisia, expressed his surprise at seeing a Mazovian nobleman proudly wearing his sabre as he drove manure to his field himself.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-36">36</a></sup> Surplus children were sent to serve richer nobles, or rented tiny plots of land from larger landowners, or took up a military career, or migrated to the towns, where they swelled the ranks of the indigent szlachta brukowa, the ‘pavement nobility', or dropped out of the nobility altogether.

The relative impoverishment of so many and the constant threat of poverty for a great many more ensured that many of the theoretical constraints upon the economic activities permitted to nobles were, in practice, ignored. Commercial activity was another means of trying to guarantee the sort of income necessary to keep up noble appearances. The richer nobles were extensively involved in trade, commerce, manufacturing and money-lending (albeit usually through the medium of factors and managers), and lesser nobles were quite prepared to move to towns or cities in pursuit of a livelihood. The town of Bochnia in the Cracow palatinate, which had grown rich on the proceeds from its salt-mines, provides a good example both of the way in which rich burghers were able to pass into the ranks of the nobility, and of the extent to which nobles migrated to towns to engage in activities which theoretically threatened them with derogation, while still maintaining their noble status. Between 1531 and 1630, about 30 per cent of patrician families in Bochnia used the title nobilis. Involvement in such supposedly derogating activities certainly did not prevent these urban nobles from participating in the general political life of the palatinate or the Commonwealth: several had close links with the royal Court, and one family – the Wielopolskis – rose out of the Bochnia patriciate in the seventeenth century to a place among the Commonwealth's magnate élite.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-41">41</a></sup>

Between the late sixteenth and the early eighteenth century several factors increased the gap between a small number of rich nobles and the ordinary szlachta. Economic developments played a part in this process. Richer nobles profited most from the favourable market conditions of the sixteenth century, while the impact of the long series of wars fought within the Commonwealth's borders after 1648 merely intensified this trend: poorer nobles lost a significant part of their land to wealthier landowners, who were better able through their economic and political power to avoid or absorb the worst effects of the wars and the downturn in demand for Polish grain. On the whole, those owning several villages increased their holdings, those owning one or two managed to hold their own, while it was the poor nobility which did worst. One study of the Kalisz district in Great Poland shows that between 1580 and 1655 the number of villages held by those owning only part of a village dropped dramatically, from 160 to 92, which was from 51 per cent of the villages in the district to 29 per cent. Those holding more than two villages increased their holdings from 50 villages (16 per cent) to 96 (30 per cent).<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-42">42</a></sup>

Access to royal land often played a vital part in this process. Royal estates comprised some 15–20 per cent of the Commonwealth's land, but the king was in no position to administer the royal domain directly and individual estates – known as starosties (starostwa) – were leased out, in most cases for life.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-43">43</a></sup> Since few officials enjoyed a salary, the grant of a starosty was in theory a reward for public service; in practice, the proceeds far outweighed any service rendered. This was despite the triumph in the 1560s of the anti-aristocratic ‘Movement for the Execution of the Laws', which sought to prevent the alienation of the royal domain and put royal finances on a firm footing. Henceforth, in theory, the kwarta – one-quarter of net, one-fifth of gross revenue from royal lands – was assigned to the upkeep of the small army which protected the south-eastern frontier. The noble leaseholder was to keep a further fifth, with the rest returning to the royal – from 1590 the state – treasury. The monarchy's inability to carry out regular audits of its estates or to enforce the law, however, soon ensured that although payment of the kwarta was fairly regular from the early seventeenth century, in practice the leaseholder kept the rest of the proceeds. Ironically, the triumph of the Execution Movement had the opposite effect to the one intended; moreover, the limited reforms that were introduced did not apply to Lithuania.

Starosties varied widely in profitability, but they contributed significantly to the economic well-being of their holders. Some, particularly in the east, were highly lucrative. The starosty of Bila Tserkva near Kiev, the richest in the whole Commonwealth, was valued for the kwarta in 1771 at 63,506 zloties, an enormous sum; altogether there were six starosties in the Kiev palatinate alone valued at over 10,000 zloties.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-44">44</a></sup> The select few who acquired several starosties could reap substantial profits. These great complexes of land, frequently scattered across a wide area, gave their owners the important advantage of flexibility. Concentration on the European economy has tended to exaggerate the importance of the Baltic grain market and play down the significance of the internal market: only some 2.5 per cent of the grain produced in the Commonwealth was exported, even during the boom years of the sixteenth century, and sales on the internal market were worth four to five times more.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-47">47</a></sup> Far from being dependent merely upon the Baltic trade, magnates were well placed to exploit all kinds of markets, both internal and external, and to sell not just grain, but timber, livestock, and a wide variety of raw materials; indeed the Ukrainian lands, where many of the largest latifundia were based, being far from the river network which served the Baltic, concentrated more on the lucrative cattle trade.

The agrarian economy was more varied than is often appreciated. While serfdom was certainly important, it was by no means the uniform system as which it is often portrayed, however convenient that view might be for general theories about the development of the European economy. The extent of labour-service (robot) obligations varied widely; in some areas, such as Royal Prussia, estates were almost entirely worked by hired labour. Even where serfdom was common, there was extensive use of hired labour: most large estates could not function without it, particularly at harvest-time. In Great Poland 10–15 per cent of the peasantry was free, working as hired labour for noble estates on contracts; 91 per cent of 337 estates studied in Little Poland between 1512 and 1642 used hired labour.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-48">48</a></sup> Even on manorial estates based on robot, the demesne constituted only about 25 per cent of the land; the remaining 75 per cent was made up of peasant plots, which were far from subsistence farms, but were often heavily involved in the local market; 50 per cent of peasant farms in Little Poland in 1590 were themselves employers of hired labour.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-49">49</a></sup>

Stanisław Lubomirski (1583–1649) maintained a permanent court of some 270 people, excluding menial servants. Many magnates kept private military retinues, as much for display as for defence, but they could mobilize large numbers in an emergency. #### VII

Wealth brought power. From the late sixteenth century, the influence of a small number of families within the political system grew enormously. The limiting after 1569 of the monarchy's financial independence meant that the royal Court was in an almost constant state of penury and could not act as the centre of social life in the way it did elsewhere in Europe. Chronic suspicion of royal intentions prevented the emergence of a substantial military establishment, and the inability of the inadequate fiscal system to pay the Commonwealth's forces during the long cycle of defensive wars between 1648 and 1721 meant that the royal government was wholly unable to provide careers compatible with noble ideas of suitable employment which impoverished nobles might have welcomed and which the developing military and administrative systems of the Commonwealth's neighbours had begun to provide: by the mid-eighteenth century, a substantial number of nobles from Great Poland were serving in the Prussian army.

Wealthy nobles partly filled this gap. It was on their estates, at their courts and in their private military retinues that poorer nobles sought employment as factors, administrators or servants. Others leased or rented lands from magnates who could not or would not farm all their lands directly, especially in the second half of the seventeenth century, when the depredations of war stimulated the widespread development of the szlachta czynszowa: landless nobles who rented their properties. In consequence, magnates commanded significant clientèles made up of nobles who depended on them directly or indirectly for their livelihood.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-58">58</a></sup> The Commonwealth's political structure gave these clientèles great scope: the liberum veto – the extension of the principle of unanimity in debate which enabled one envoy to break the proceedings of a Sejm or sejmik, first recognized in 1652 – enabled faction leaders to block the policies of their rivals, while the fact that envoys to the Sejm were delegates from their sejmiki, furnished with instructions, not representatives with plenipotentiary powers, meant that magnates were able to use their local power bases to win significant leverage at the centre of politics. Foreign nobles continued to use their titles if they obtained Polish nobility; native nobles were all too keen to seek honours abroad: the Radziwiłłs, Lubomirskis and Ossolińskis were dukes of the Holy Roman Empire and the Myszkowskis were created marquises of the Empire by Pope Clement VIII. Titles were, however, of relatively minor significance. Differences in status within the nobility, which elsewhere were denoted by a hierarchy of titles, were in the Commonwealth provided by the hierarchy of office. At its head was the Senate, ranked according to a strict order of precedence led by the two Catholic archbishops (of Gniezno and Lwów) and fifteen Catholic bishops. The leading lay senator was the castellan of Cracow, ahead of the palatines, among whom were counted the castellans of Wilno and Troki and the starosta general of Samogitia. Next came the major castellans, who were governors of the royal castle in the leading city of each palatinate, then the minor castellans, governors of royal castles in lesser cities, of whom there were none in Lithuania. Technically ranked at the bottom of the Senate, though actually possessing the enormous prestige of power, were ten government ministers: the grand marshals, chancellors, vice-chancellors, treasurers and marshals of the Court, of whom there was one each for Poland and Lithuania.

Certain non-senatorial offices were also highly prized, particularly the posts of grand or field hetman (commanders of the Polish and Lithuanian armies), which conferred a great deal of power, and of court treasurer, which was lucrative, as well as a number of honorific posts, such as chamberlain, steward, or carver, whose duties, if they existed, were largely ceremonial; these dignities were, nevertheless, much coveted: they were frequently the preserve of young members of prominent families. #### VIII

It might seem, therefore, that a small group of wealthy families had subverted the egalitarianism which still formally survived, and stealthily overturned the victories won by the mass of the nobility in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Historians in the Polish Peoples Republic after 1945, required to write within a broadly Marxist framework, devoted much attention to documenting and explaining this phenomenon. From the 1950s, the standard interpretation of the Commonwealth's development was that the sixteenth-century ‘szlachta democracy' was overturned by a ‘magnate oligarchy' during the seventeenth century. As the power of these magnates grew, they seized control of the helm of state in their own ‘reactionary' interests, while pretending to keep up the façade of the ‘progressive' szlachta democracy which they consistently undermined.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-62">62</a></sup>

This theory was thought to provide a model of Polish development which might explain its divergence from the general European pattern. There are, however, serious objections to it. First, the classic period of the ‘magnate oligarchy', which was meant to have started at some point after 1648, corresponded exactly with the period in which the Commonwealth's political

Second, it proved remarkably difficult to agree on the attributes of a magnate. It is impossible to define any élite group on the basis purely of landholding, since the value of property varied greatly from one part of the Commonwealth to another: Dworzaczek calculated that a nobleman from Little Poland would have to own twenty villages to enjoy the same income as his counterpart from Great Poland who merely owned ten.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-63">63</a></sup> Senate membership was important, but the Senate was small – only 150-strong at its peak in the seventeenth century, of which 52 were minor castellans of lesser prestige – and the practice of life-tenure of office meant that turnover was slow. The problem with attempts to delineate a magnate élite is that they assume a stability at the top level of noble society which did not exist. Although social life and politics were dominated in particular periods by a tiny élite of families and individuals, this élite was subject to constant change, with only a handful of families maintaining their position unchallenged.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-65">65</a></sup> There is a natural tendency within any aristocracy for families to rise, decline or die out, but certain peculiar features of the Commonwealth's political and social structure ensured that the instability within its élite was greater than elsewhere in Europe and does much to explain why noble society in the Commonwealth failed to settle down into the stability and relative calm experienced in other noble-dominated societies between the mid-seventeenth century and the French Revolution.

No family could take its position for granted. By its very nature, the system caused frequent and bitter frustration and was liable to intense periods of volatility. Since appointment was for life, a monarch had to accept the senators and even ministers bequeathed to him by his predecessor. Instability also had economic roots. A great deal of study has been devoted to the dazzling fortunes of great nobles, and the ways in which one was accumulated; far less to the problems of preventing its dissipation. Yet partible inheritance was not just a problem for Mazovian smallholders; it also affected the greatest families. In 1586 the sons of Mikołaj Radziwiłł the Black established three separate entails from his vast estates, an example that was followed by the Zamoyskis (1589), Myszkowskis (1601), Ostrogskis (1609) and Sułkowskis (1783). Other families, however, showed no interest in following suit. There was some opposition on the grounds that entails violated the principle of noble equality, but in each of the above cases, Sejm approval was forthcoming in return for the acceptance of certain obligations on the part of the holder. The main reason why only seven were ever established seems to have been lack of interest rather than szlachta opposition. The holder of an entail was not as free to dispose of his property as other landowners: if he had acquired no great allodial holdings outside the entail it was difficult to provide for his younger children, and he could not sell land to pay his creditors, a dilemma that spectacularly confronted Janusz Sanguszko, holder of the ill-fated Ostrogski entail, in 1753.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-75">75</a></sup>

Even the wealthiest faced enormous problems. #### IX

The result was anarchic stalemate. By 1721, with the Commonwealth's international position in ruins, its political system apparently paralysed and its economy wrecked, the lofty ideals of the mid-sixteenth century seemed to have been destroyed. Yet as the gap between ideal and reality grew, it became increasingly difficult to sustain the pretence. The political strains of the period after 1717, when Russia virtually established a protectorate over the Commonwealth, against the background of enlightened ideas which no longer saw arguments from tradition or authority as necessarily conclusive, finally helped undermine szlachta solidarity. The result was unexpected: as noble society began to fragment under its last king, Stanisław August Poniatowski (1764–95), this apparently moribund system regenerated itself. The reforms of the Four Year Sejm (1788–92) culminating in the Constitution of 3 May 1791 may have come too late to preserve the Commonwealth – indeed they helped provoke its destruction – but they embodied a dramatic break with the Sarmatian past.

The growing appreciation of the chasm between ideal and reality stimulated not only an intense debate about the political reform which was so clearly necessary, but also a fundamental re-examination of the very assumptions on which the whole socio-political structure had rested since the mid-sixteenth century. The reign of Poniatowski – himself from the reform-minded Czartoryski 'Family' – was dominated by the problem of reform. Partly under the influence of enlightened ideas, the Commonwealth's constitution was subjected to growing criticism and unfavourable comparisons with other European states. In Court circles, the blinkered conservatism of Sarmatian traditionalists was mocked. Yet there were important barriers to reform. Poniatowski, ex-lover of Catherine II, had been elevated to the throne with Russian support; although he was determined to reform, he soon split from his uncles in the Family, who wished to direct operations. Early initiatives failed when they came up against the reluctance of Prussia and Russia to risk any substantial change in the political system, and the opposition of magnate malcontents angry at the apparent triumph of the

The first, hesitant steps towards reform ended in disaster. Malcontents led by such dubious figures as the atheist churchman Gabriel Podoski and Karol Radziwiłł, an empty-headed sot whose numerous vices were dwarfed only by his pride, formed the Confederation of Radom in 1767 to block reform, and turned to Russia to help them. Catherine duly stepped in, demonstrating her attachment to enlightened values by demolishing much of what had already been achieved. Repnin, the Russian ambassador, forced through his own programme, which outraged many malcontents, partly because it removed restrictions on the participation of religious dissidents in public life. The result was the Confederation of Bar (1768–72), an anarchic movement in the best Commonwealth tradition, directed against Poniatowski in the name of noble republicanism, but also aimed at Russian interference in Polish politics, of which Poniatowski was a symbol. The subsequent chaos led directly to the First Partition of 1772 and a new political settlement which deprived the king of much of his scope for independent initiative, including the power to distribute starosties, but was mainly intended to ensure that the Commonwealth was kept quiet in the interests of Russia.

The Partition Settlement was an important watershed. Although it seemed initially that politics had reverted to sterile factional manoeuvring, the political landscape had fundamentally changed. Poniatowski was tied more closely than ever to Russian power, exercised through the new body of the Permanent Council, and the influence of Repnin's successor, Stackelberg. As Radom and Bar had made clear, supporters of the old order could no longer look to Russia to support the republican system, as it had long claimed to; indeed, reform was now essential to preserve it. The remnants of the Family had split definitively from Poniatowski over the Partition – to form an improbable alliance with such benighted conservative republicans as the Polish hetmans, Seweryn Rzewuski and Ksawery Branicki, who were virulently opposed to a new order which had encroached substantially on their powers. Although they shared very different views of what the Commonwealth should become, all were agreed that the present order must be overturned. When Russia was attacked by the Ottoman Empire in 1787, and Sweden in 1788, and Prussia began to slip away from its alliance with Austria and Russia following the death of Frederick II, it provided the opportunity for a fundamental change which all desired, from Poniatowski to the 'Patriot' supporters of Sarmatian republicanism.

A series of social, economic, political and cultural developments had ensured that by 1788 it was no longer sustainable. The attachment to birth as the essential defining characteristic of a noble had been steadily eroded from the mid-seventeenth century; indeed, as early as 1568 Wawrzyniec Goślicki, who commended the laws of the Rhodians for depriving of their property those sons of virtuous fathers who lived viciously, had argued that ‘it is not a Hall painted full of proude Armes or badges, but vertue which maketh a man Noble'.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-102">102</a></sup> Noble status in the Commonwealth had never been primarily determined by relationship to the means of production, but was a legal and cultural construct; nevertheless, the idea of the nobility as essentially a landed Estate had always lain at the heart of that ideal. Yet, despite the domination of the political system by the szlachta, the law had proved incapable of bridging the gap between ideal and reality: noble birth could not guarantee a noble lifestyle. The bonds of solidarity felt by middling nobles with the swelling band of the penurious, boorish, frequently illiterate noble proletariat were steadily weakening, especially since poor nobles and those renting or leasing land from wealthy magnates formed the vast bulk of the obedient footsoldiers in the magnate clientes which allowed magnate factions to manipulate and obstruct the political system.

From the mid-seventeenth century, in consequence, a steady process of change had begun to separate out those privileges of nobility which were attached to the person, and those enjoyed only by landholders. Some of these – exemption from taxation and customs privileges and the inviolability of property – could of their very nature only be enjoyed by property owners. Increasingly, however, a number of privileges in civil law were restricted to property owners, including personal freedom from arrest and the ability to hold public office. A number of sejmiki began to exclude non-property owners from their deliberations; a larger number began to question their right to participate. All that remained of the panoply of privilege to a growing number was the right to acquire landed property, which was usually beyond the means of the poor szlachta, and preferential treatment regarding admission to ecclesiastical office, military service or the bar.<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-103">103</a></sup>

Noble smallholders, however tiny their hereditary plot, kept their citizenship, while large numbers of noble leaseholders, many of whom were better off, were deprived of theirs. Nobility might be eternal – though even that was now in question – but it no longer guaranteed the citizenship which had been its most important attribute; that was now determined by property, however subject to life's vagaries that might be. The way was now open into the nobility for commoners – over 400 of whom were ennobled in three weeks in 1790 – although it was unclear whether those who did not choose to enter it would win a greater place in the political system. The constitution, however, was never put to the test, since the spectacular collapse of noble solidarity helped bring about the destruction of the Commonwealth: there was no shortage of conservative magnates prepared to appeal to both the noble dispossessed and the Commonwealth's neighbours, who immediately condemned the new order. Within four years, the szlachta, citizens and non-citizens alike were subjects of three alien powers with very different traditions of nobility. It was easy enough for middling and wealthy landowners to be accommodated within systems in which landed nobilities were so dominant; nevertheless, the spell of the old Commonwealth remained strong, and many among the szlachta, from magnates to humble smallholders or noble leaseholders whose status remained unrecognized, refused to be reconciled to the new order and engaged enthusiastically in movements which sought to overturn it, from Kościuszko's rising in 1794 against the Second Partition, to the January Rising of 1863, often losing life, liberty or lands in the process.
