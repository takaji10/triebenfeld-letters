<div class="lit-summary" markdown="1">

Janina Muszyńska-Zygmańska's book is a full account of Greater Poland
(Wielkopolska) under Prussian rule and in the Kościuszko Insurrection of
1794, written in Poznań in 1947 from the South Prussian administrative files,
the contemporary press, memoirs and the printed sources. It is a work of its
time: the judgements are stated plainly, and the author writes as a Polish
historian of the province's Polish past.

**The Second Partition and Prussian rule.** The book opens in 1792, with
Prussia and Austria failing against revolutionary France and Frederick
William II demanding compensation in Polish lands. It follows the diplomacy
of Mainz and Merle, the Prusso-Russian convention of 23 January 1793, the
Prussian declaration of 6 January, and Möllendorff's six columns entering
Greater Poland on 24 January. The Targowica Confederation protested in vain;
the weak Greater Poland division under Byszewski withdrew behind the Pilica
and the Bzura after small clashes at Sieraków, Kargowa and Gniezno. The
occupation was complete with the fall of Częstochowa on 5 March; the
notification patent of 25 March, the formal takeover in April and the homage
of 7 May at Poznań followed. The new province of South Prussia was organised
in two departments, Poznań and Piotrków, with Prussian chambers, courts,
schools, Landräte and tax inspectors; Poles were removed from office, noble
and clerical land tenure was reduced, and the edict of 24 July 1793 confined
landed property to the nobility. The king's autumn tour found the population
cool, and the chapter closes by asking why the Poznań burghers, so active in
the movement for the Constitution of 3 May, offered no resistance in 1793.

**The outbreak of the Insurrection.** The second chapter follows the
conspiracy from spring 1793 in the army, among the Warsaw "Jacobins" and in
the emigration. The moderates wanted preparation, the radicals immediate
action; Kościuszko, the expected leader, repeatedly told the delegates to
wait, because the army was weak, the peasants were not mobilised and French
help was uncertain. In March 1794 the Russian-ordered reduction of the army
and the arrests in Warsaw forced events: Madaliński's Greater Poland brigade
marched out on 12 March, Kościuszko proclaimed the Insurrection at Kraków on
24 March and won at Racławice on 4 April, and Warsaw rose on 17-18 April.
The author explains why Greater Poland held back: Prussian troops were
scattered through the country, and Kościuszko's policy toward Prussia was
conciliatory. He avoided a war on two fronts, negotiated through Zakrzewski
with the Prussian envoy Buchholtz, and did not use the Greater Poland
delegates. Only after the defeat at Szczekociny on 6 June did he turn openly
against Prussia.

**The rising and Dąbrowski's expedition.** The third and longest chapter
begins after Szczekociny. Zajączek is defeated at Chełm, Kraków capitulates,
the attempts to break the Prussian cordon on the Narew fail, and the
Prusso-Russian army besieges Warsaw from 13 July to 6 September. In
mid-August Bielamowski rides out through the Kampinos forest to the Bzura,
Gąbin and Włocławek, raids Prussian treasuries and shows how thin the
garrisons are. Greater Poland rises in the second half of August: Mniewski at
Brześć Kujawski and Włocławek, the assembly at Kościan and the act at Rąbiń,
Lipski's Gniezno group, and risings in the Sieradz, Wieluń, Ostrzeszów,
Kalisz, Notec and Dobrzyń lands. Prussia has about 8,000 troops in South
Prussia and answers with columns under Manstein, Dossow, Bonin, Elsner,
Schwerin and Székely; the insurgent moves are patchy, with the surprise at
Morkowo, the failure at Jutrosin and the sack of Sieradz. The risings tie
down Prussian troops and help persuade Frederick William II to lift the siege
of Warsaw. On 9 September Kościuszko sends Dąbrowski with a small regular
corps and Wybicki as plenipotentiary; Dąbrowski crosses the Bzura at
Kamionna, marches by Gąbin and Koło to Słupca, merges the Greater Poland
levies, enters Gniezno on 25 September, beats off Székely's night attack at
Łabiszyn and storms Bydgoszcz on 2 October, where Székely is mortally
wounded. His detachments reach Świecie, Nakło and Chełmża; the march on Toruń
is called off when Ledivary's relief column arrives; news of Maciejowice
forces the retreat toward Warsaw. The chapter also follows the parallel
partisan war, Schwerin's plundering march to Poznań and the Prussian panic
there. Its judgement is that the rising was a real strategic diversion that
saved Warsaw, that Dąbrowski's expedition was skilful and disciplined in the
Frederician manner, and that the local partisan movement was courageous but
short-lived and badly led.

**The fall.** The fourth chapter turns to the war elsewhere: the collapse of
the Lithuanian forces, Suvorov's destruction of Sierakowski's division,
Fersen's crossing of the Vistula, and Kościuszko's defeat, wounding and
capture at Maciejowice on 10 October. Wawrzecki becomes Commander; the
Supreme National Council issues proclamations to keep up morale. Dąbrowski
learns of the disaster, leaves Bydgoszcz with prisoners and a large baggage
train, withdraws by Gniewkowo, Włocławek and Gąbin, and crosses the Bzura at
Mistrzewice unopposed on 23 October; he digs in at Brochów and goes to Warsaw.
The author praises the expedition for diverting Prussian forces, damaging
Prussia's position in the partition talks and exposing the incompetence of
the Prussian commanders. Warsaw's last councils reject Dąbrowski's plan to
move the government and army into Greater Poland; Suvorov storms Praga on
4 November and Warsaw capitulates on 6 November. The army dissolves after
Radoszyce, and Wawrzecki and others are taken by the Russians on
18 November. Insurgent bands in Kujawy, Sieradz, Wieluń, Gniezno and
Grodzisk keep fighting into December. The closing assessment is that nobles
and clergy drove the rising and the peasants held back, and that weak
organisation, discord among leaders and the failure to concentrate forces
undid it; the Greater Poles are credited with a real diversion behind the
Prussian lines.

**After the fall.** The last chapter opens with the diplomatic collapse:
France refuses the loan, the Committee of Public Safety distrusts a "noble"
rising, and the late French, Turkish, Swedish and Austrian hopes come to
nothing. It traces the final partition negotiations of 1794-95, the
Austro-Russian convention of 3 January 1795, the Peace of Basel, the
Prusso-Russian treaty of 24 October 1795 and the new Prussian provinces of
South Prussia, New East Prussia and New Silesia. It then follows the
prosecution of the insurgents: the special commissions, the patents and
amnesty offers, Hoym's plan, arrests, sequestrations, fines (with a list of
the largest payments) and confiscations, and the dismissal of officials. No
death sentence was passed. The Prussian regime is described in its courts,
language, Landrecht, control of the clergy, nobility (incolat), towns, Jews,
censorship, taxation, domain management, colonisation and grants to Germans,
and schooling. The book ends with a patriotic coda on the survival of the
Polish spirit, 1848 and the Nazi occupation.
