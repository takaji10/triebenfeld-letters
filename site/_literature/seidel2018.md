<div class="lit-summary" markdown="1">

A biography of Friedrich Ludwig, Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen (1746-1818), called "FL" throughout, by Hans-Joachim Seidel, who grew up in Ingelfingen. It is drawn above all from the letters and papers in the Hohenlohe Central Archive at Neuenstein, many of them quoted at length, from Adolf Fischer's history of the House of Hohenlohe (1871) and from Gerlinde Kraus's study of the Upper Silesian estates (2001). Twenty-three chapters, with "excursuses" on the background, eight appendices and a list of sources; the notes (pp. 257-266) are not transcribed in the edition's copy.

### Ingelfingen and the family (chapters 1-2)

The little town of Ingelfingen on the Kocher and the Ingelfingen line of the House of Hohenlohe, founded by Count Christian Kraft at the beginning of the eighteenth century; FL's father Heinrich August, who introduced primogeniture in 1774. FL is born on 31 January 1746, taught by tutors at Ingelfingen and Öhringen, and made as a boy to keep account of his pocket money, without lasting effect. In 1782 he marries Marianne, Countess Hoym zu Droyßig, seventeen years younger and a great heiress; Frederick II had pointed her out to him. Seidel lists their eight children (August, born 1784, the successor; Adelheid, Emilie, Auguste; Adolph, born 1797, later briefly Prussian minister-president; Alexander, born 1798; two who died as infants), the marriage settlements, and Marianne's mother, Princess Christiane von der Osten-Sacken, who managed the Hoym fortune. Marianne leaves husband and children in 1799. An anonymous epistolary novel of 1792 about a "Marianne von Ingelfingen" and her brutal husband is discussed and set aside. An excursus gathers what the archive holds on FL's children born outside the marriage: a son born in Berlin in 1782, a daughter of about 1775 for whom he settled a house and an annuity that later lapsed and was restored in 1806, and three children raised by a foster mother who came forward after his death.

### The Mariannenvorstadt (chapter 3)

As Hereditary Prince, from 1782, FL founded a suburb of craftsmen at Ingelfingen, named after his wife: free building land, building subsidies, ten years free of dues and of billeting, settlers chosen with care (drunkards refused, a Catholic admitted in 1787 for the sake of tolerance), houses planned by his director of buildings and salines J. G. Glenk and built in two phases to about 1806. Further plans (a jewellery factory, a river bath, a grain market, mills) mostly came to little; a Latin school, an "industrial school" (1792) and a drawing school were set up.

### The general (chapters 4-6)

FL's career: a captain in the Franconian Circle regiment at seventeen, in Prussian service from 1766, colonel, major general, inspector general in Silesia, governor of Breslau from 1791, with the King at Pillnitz in 1791. In the war against revolutionary France he led the advance guard and won at Kaiserslautern (1794). After the Peace of Basel (1795) he watched the demarcation line from Frankfurt, kept the French out of the city, which made him its first honorary citizen, and won neutrality for the Hohenlohe lands; he was close to Hardenberg. Chapter 5 gives Marianne's letters to him from Berlin in 1792 and his letters to the young children (1798-1806), among them one of 1799 asking that an enclosure be sent to the war councillor von Triebenfeld, and his letter from Dresden of September 1806 on taking command of the Prussian-Saxon army. FL became reigning Prince in 1796 with a programme for agriculture, trade and commerce, but lived at Breslau as governor: a town palace bought in 1794, and at Alt-Scheitnig a summer house and a park he opened to the public, where he received the King with illuminations and festivities. In 1805 he succeeded his childless uncle at Öhringen; in August 1806 he had his son August declared of age, handed the principality to him and resigned; Hohenlohe passed to the new Kingdom of Württemberg.

### The separation (chapter 7)

The "Transact" of 9 and 19 November 1799, drawn up by Princess Sacken for her daughter: an allowance for Marianne, Schloss Oppurg, her possessions, the provision of the children and the House, the cession of Slawentzitz in Upper Silesia to FL, and an inventory of FL's own estates, among them the South Prussian ones (the Konin estates, the Kujavian and Meseritz estates). The divorce papers of 6 August 1804, in which Marianne accepts the blame for leaving. And her "word of trust" of 12 October 1811 to her sons and the guardians, found by Kraus: she had left, she wrote, to save her children's fortune from his debts, after being made to sign for enormous sums again and again. She married Count Friedrich von der Osten-Sacken and had two daughters.

### 1806 and its judges (chapters 8-10)

FL's letters to August (1800-1805) on Breslau, careers and August's studies at Erlangen. Jena and Auerstedt, the retreat by Magdeburg in growing disorder, and the capitulation at Prenzlau on 28 October 1806, by which FL saved the lives of his men and became for ever a co-loser of 1806; his letter from captivity of 3 November; Massenbach's own account of his mistake before Prenzlau. FL is held prisoner on parole at Öhringen until the end of 1808, then made to leave Württemberg; he reaches Slawentzitz on 4 January 1809. Chapter 10 weighs his contemporaries: Friedrich von Cölln, who blamed his debts, his wife and his advisers (Triebenfeld, Grattauer, the broker Goldstecker) and his profit from the South Prussian grants, and who says Triebenfeld received eight estates valued at 51,000 thalers and sold them for 750,000; Friedrich Buchholz (1808); an unnamed officer (1831) who praised him; the memoirs of his aide-de-camp von der Marwitz (Dresden 1805, Tsar Alexander, the King's incomprehension of Prenzlau); and Goethe, whom FL met in the campaign of 1792 and at Jena in October 1806, where Goethe stopped the printing of Massenbach's manifesto against Napoleon.

### Upper Silesia, Glenck (chapters 11-12)

Slawentzitz, from the Hoym inheritance, as his seat in old age; his offer of service to the King in March 1813, declined in eight lines. His estates and works in Upper Silesia (Koschentin, bought in 1804 for 925,000 thalers, Landsberg, Bytkow, Lassowitz), farm buildings, mills, ironworks and mining with the English expert Baildon; the great wealth of "Hohenlohehütte" came only to a grandson. Chapter 12 follows the salines of Weißbach and Niedernhall and the search for coal financed by shares, with the father and son Glenk/Glenck, which failed; their letters to FL run from 1802 to 1817. The younger Glenck later found salt at Stotternheim (1828, with Goethe's interest) and in Basel-Land, and appears among FL's creditors.

### South Prussia (chapter 13)

The second and third partitions, the province of South Prussia with its chambers at Posen, Kalisch and Warsaw, and the King's settlement of German colonists on church and crown lands. The "squandering of estates": a founding board of Hoym, Bischofswerder, Triebenfeld and the King's chamberlain Ritz; Hans von Held's denunciation (1800) and the fall of Minister Hoym (1798). FL's own share, quoted from Breyer (1938): in 1796, the church estates of the lordship of Zagórów and the lordship of Trąbczyn; a hereditary lease concluded by Triebenfeld with eighteen German colonists, dated 18 May 1805 and approved by the Kalisz chamber on 28 January 1806; and the German villages named after FL's children and Hohenlohe places (Sophiental, Ludwigslust, Friedrichsfelde, Adelshof, Adolfsberg, Ingelfingen for Anielewo, Emilienheim for Michalinów, Augustinhof, Schwentz for Święcia, Kolonweiler for Wrąbczyn, Klein-Öhring), with Breyer's map. FL's correspondence with the King in 1798 about the lordship of Krotoszyn, and his help to Massenbach, who was granted Białokosz.

### The debts (chapter 14)

FL left a mountain of debt: 280,000 florins in 1806, loans from the Duke of Courland (300,000 thalers, 1790) and the Schlabrendorffs (1798), and the purchase of Koschentin. In a letter of 25 September 1806 he puts the net yield of Zagórów and Trąbczyn, confirmed by the Kalisz chamber, at 40,247 thalers and proposes to borrow 480,000 on the two estates to pay all his debts. From local accounts Seidel adds that five estates (Trąbczyn, Nowa Wieś, Osiny, Łazy, Szetlewek) were worth about 80,000 thalers and that half was sold to Weigel of Kopojno. After 1807 the Polish estates yielded nothing. Triebenfeld's letter of 14 March 1808 tells of his arrest by French gendarmes for 5,000 thalers of interest owed to the Invalids' Fund, which the banker Oppenheimer had undertaken to pay, and of Minister Zastrow's surety. Further: Triebenfeld's activity report of 7 March 1809 from Kontopp (appendix 6), the suit of the Invalids' Fund before the Kammergericht in 1811 (appendix 7), a payment owed in 1813 by a "Count Tercinoky" as owner of Zagórów, and Triebenfeld's lists of debts. Massenbach and Triebenfeld were in debt too.

### The last years and after (chapters 15-23)

The new Kingdom of Württemberg and its curbs on the mediatised princes (15). The letters of the former tutor Kahlert, 1807-1818: a press digest from Breslau on the quarrel over 1806 (the "Feuerbrände", Cölln, Massenbach, a defence of Prenzlau), then books and news (16). Marianne's letters to Cosmar (1811) and to August (1816-1817), with her troubles over a mortgage of 43,000 thalers held by Hardenberg on Bellin (17). FL's sister Sophie Christiane, who brought up the youngest children and nursed him at the end (18). The last letters to him, from the King, Kahlert, his nephew Eduard and his sons (19). FL dies at Slawentzitz on 15 February 1818; creditors come at once; the South Prussian estates are sold; Zagórów now lies in Congress Poland, and Triebenfeld seeks compensation at Vienna. Marianne dies in Mecklenburg in 1840 (20). The sons: August II, Württemberg politician and later of Slawentzitz; Adolph; Alexander (21). An excursus on Christian von Massenbach, FL's quartermaster general and contrary figure: his writings, his radical speeches in the Württemberg diet of 1817, his arrest and fortress sentence (22). A closing portrait: the loyal general of the Prussian kings, no patron of the arts, a caring father after the separation (23).

### The appendices

A table of FL's military career; a map of Upper Silesia and of Poland after the third partition; a list of his estates with their German names; Triebenfeld's statement of the debts (366,000) and a list of 1817 (among the creditors Oppenheimer and Glenck); a transcription of Triebenfeld's letter of 7 March 1809 from Kontopp, thirteen powers of attorney given for the South Prussian estates through the justice burgomaster of Zagórów (Seidel reads "Habich"; the edition's Hawich), against the sequestrator, the banker Oppenheimer, a lessee and the colonists, which stands in the edition as letters 48 and 302 of Oe 1 Bü 9454; extracts from the proceedings of 1811, in which the Invalids' Fund, having lent Triebenfeld 50,000 thalers in 1805 on the security of FL's bond of 1 June 1804 for 250,000 on the Zagórów estates, seeks to attach a legacy of 80,000 rumoured to have come to FL from Princess Sacken; and a list of the wages owed to FL's servants in 1817.

### Where the edition's documents say otherwise

The edition follows its documents. Trąbczyn, with Szetlewek, Nowa Wieś, Łazy and Osiny, came to FL by the King's deed of gift of 9 August 1796 as estates fallen to the crown, that is Prusimski's confiscated estates (Oe 1 Bü 14525, doc. 1), not as crown or starost land; Zagórów came by a deed of grant of 19 June 1797 (I. HA Rep. 162 Nr. 295, doc. 3), not in 1796. The edition's copy of the colonists' lease lacks its beginning and is dated by its engrossing, 21 March 1806 (APP 53/968/0/-/801, doc. 3), so the 18 May 1805 can be neither confirmed nor ruled out; the chamber's consent of 28 January 1806 (doc. 2) and Triebenfeld's bond of 28 January 1805 for the 50,000 thalers (Rep. 162 Nr. 295, doc. 1) agree.
