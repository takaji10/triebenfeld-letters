Three entries of *Pinkas Hakehillot Polin*, Yad Vashem's encyclopedia of the Jewish communities of Poland (vol. I, Łódź and its region, 1976), in the English translations Morris Gradel made for JewishGen. Each opens with a table of the general and the Jewish population and closes with the Holocaust.

### Zagórów (p. 103)

The table runs from 1808, when 21 of the 827 inhabitants were Jews, to 1921 (807 of 3,715), with about 630 Jews on 1 September 1939. The entry dates the town's status to 1368 and the first Jewish settlers to the turn of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the nineteenth century Jews owned flour mills, dealt in farm produce and leather and worked as hatters, tailors, shoemakers, carpenters and tinsmiths; later they made blanket cloth. A new synagogue was built in 1882, a provident fund founded in 1889, with charities for the poor, for brides and for the synagogue's lamp. Zionist groups appear before the First World War, a library in 1915 and a branch of the Zionist federation in 1917. Between the wars the Jews grew poorer under Polish competition; several Zionist parties were active and the rabbi in the 1930s was Shalom Grodzinski. At the fair of February 1936 a crowd of some 3,000 peasants attacked the Jewish traders, looted and wounded six; 55 people were tried, among them local nationalist leaders. A boycott closed fifteen Jewish businesses in 1937, and Jewish traders were refused stalls. The Germans took the town on 6 September 1939 and imposed forced labour, confiscations and armbands; in February 1940 men were sent to labour at Bochnia; in the spring of 1940 the Jews were shut in a ghetto, joined in June and July by Jews from Konin, Słupca, Ślesin and Golina, about 2,000 in all early in 1941. In late summer 1941 some 450 men went to the salt mines of Inowrocław; in October 1941 the rest were taken to the forests near Kazimierz Biskupi and murdered.

### Pyzdry (pp. 202-203)

The table gives 454 Jews in 1764-1765, 1,221 of 3,412 inhabitants in 1808, then a decline to 406 in 1921 and about 500 in 1939. Pyzdry, a town since 1247 and once a trading centre, declined from the fifteenth century, lost its town rights in 1870 and regained them in 1919. Jews are recorded there from 1387; a fifteenth-century rabbi mentions the community, and its leaders sat in the Council of the Four Lands. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the Jews lived in their own quarter and built mills, tanneries and weaving shops; the entry counts the Jewish craftsmen of 1764 and names the rabbis from 1782 to the twentieth century. Between the wars the community became poorer under antisemitic pressure; in 1936 nationalists murdered Marcus Nelken, owner of a Jewish farm near the town; Zionists and Agudat Israel shared the community board. After the German conquest in September 1939 a Judenrat had to list the community and supply forced labour; on 17-18 July 1940 the Jews were deported to Rzgów, Grodziec and Zagórów and shared the fate of the Jews of the Konin district.

### Konin (pp. 235-238)

The longest of the three, in four parts. **To 1918:** a town since the late thirteenth century, with Jews recorded from 1397, probably come from Kalisz and under its community until the end of the eighteenth century; a Jewish street from 1633; fires, epidemics and the Swedish wars; the grain trade, the fairs (from 1425), and in the nineteenth century Jewish wholesale trade, breweries, building materials, flour mills, an agricultural machinery plant and a bank (1913). The rabbis from 1810 to the Second World War, the Great Synagogue (1766), a Beit Midrash, Hasidic prayer houses and charities; Jews in the rising of 1863; Zionists, Poalei Zion and the Bund from 1905; a Jewish mayor in 1916; schools, a library (1901) and Yiddish theatre; the hardships of the First World War. **Between the wars:** markets and trades much as before, nearly every Jewish party and youth movement, a Hebrew-Polish gymnasium, an ORT school, a library of 8,190 books in 1936, painters and writers from Konin, sports clubs. **The Holocaust:** hostages and executions in September 1939; on 30 November 1939 about a thousand Jews were rounded up and on 3 December deported to Ostrowiec; in July 1940 most of the rest were sent with the Jews of the district to Grodziec and Rzgów, a kind of rural ghetto, and to Zagórów; in March 1941 most of the Jews of Grodziec went by way of Łódź to Józefów Biłgorajski, where they were killed in 1942; in October 1941 the Jews gathered at Zagórów, Rzgów and Grodziec were murdered in the forests of Kazimierz Biskupi, and their bodies burned in 1945. A labour camp at Konin held Jews from Gostynin and Gąbin from March 1942; its prisoners rose and set fire to it in August 1943, and Rabbi Yehoshua Moshe Aaronson's diary of the camp survived in part. Konin Jews fought in the Warsaw and Częstochowa ghettos. After the war 46 Jews returned (1945); by 1965 two remained. **Notes:** the translator's glossary of Hebrew and Yiddish terms.

### Where the documents say otherwise

The Zagórów entry dates the town's status to 1368. The document is King Władysław II's privilege of 19 November 1407 allowing the abbey of Ląd to found a town at its village of Zagórów under Magdeburg law (Kodeks Dyplomatyczny Wielkopolski; KDW, doc. 14). The entry's Jews settling about 1800 agrees with the parish count of 1791 (ADWł 85, doc. 2), which lists none in the town. Against the Encyclopedia of Camps and Ghettos (2012) the entries differ on details of 1940-1941 (where the Jews of Słupca were sent, the numbers in the Zagórów ghetto, the month of the murder); neither is a document.
