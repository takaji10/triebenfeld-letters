Pohlen war wie bekannt durch Jahrhunderte nie mit das Haus Brandenburg verbunden. — Es herrschte vielmehr zwischen diesen Ländern oder Unterthanen ein immerwährender Nationalhaß. Es ward daher in früheren Zeiten mit dieser rauhen Nation eine Art Handel, wie mit halbnomadischen Völkern, getrieben, wobei jedoch der Deutsche sein Conto fand.

Erst Friedrich der Große fing an, diese rauhe Nation ins Auge zu fassen, und zog die bedeutendsten Vortheile aus diesem Lande; durch die mehrmalige Theilung fiel endlich ein bedeutender Theil dieses Reichs an das Haus Preußen. Friedrich der Große und Weise verwandte sofort große Summen an die ihm zugefallenen Bezirke und bildete durch rastlose Thätigkeit der dahin gesandten Personen, ein glückliches Ländchen. Ein gleiches Loos aber wurde denen in der zweiten und dritten Theilung Pohlens unter Friedrich Wilhelm II. zufallenden Pohlen nicht zu Theil. Dieser herzensgute Monarch wollte zwar das Beste seiner neuen und alten Unterthanen bewirken, durch das allzu große Zutrauen aber, welches der biederste Monarch in seine Minister setzte, blieb sein wohlmeinender Wille unerfüllt. Durch

<div class="lit-en" lang="en" markdown="1">

A sketch of how Poland is made use of by Prussia, and of how Prussia can at least obtain her interests for the twenty-seven million thalers lent to that country.

Poland, as is known, was never for centuries joined with the House of Brandenburg. — Rather there reigned between these lands, or their subjects, a perpetual national hatred. In earlier times, therefore, a kind of trade was carried on with this rude nation, as with half-nomadic peoples, wherein yet the German found his account.

First Frederick the Great began to take this rude nation under his eye, and drew the greatest advantages from that land; through the repeated partitions a considerable part of that realm fell at last to the House of Prussia. Frederick the Great and Wise at once expended great sums on the districts that had fallen to him, and formed, through the tireless activity of the persons sent thither, a happy little land. A like lot, however, did not befall the Poles that fell to Prussia in the second and third partitions under Frederick William II. This warm-hearted monarch indeed wished the best for his new and old subjects; but through the all-too-great trust which the most honest of monarchs placed in his ministers, his well-meaning will remained unfulfilled. The saddest event that befell the province was that Minister von Voss became its administrator. — He at once ordered, under the supervision of the Privy Finance Councillor Schulz, the classification of the estates, or of all lands. This was fitting, and must necessarily precede any true organisation. But when, as was the case here, self-interested superiors held the tiller and chose the dregs of mankind for their helpers, not only was the good purpose lost; the foundation-stone was thereby laid of the insurrection that soon followed. Before this the Pole had the highest idea of all Prussian constitutions, and never believed that a Prussian official could be bought; but when the classification commissioners not only allowed themselves to be bribed, but gave themselves up besides to the vice of drink and all manner of excess, then the Pole loaded not only these but the whole new government with the deepest disdain and swore in his heart revenge against it. The conduct of these men was indeed too striking; for in former times the Poles gave of their substance 10 per cent; under the Voss or Schulz classification commission the dues of many estates, where for example a War Councillor v. Reisewitz and others of his kind had made the classification, would scarcely have amounted to 5 per cent against the old rate. The money-fine was indeed little fitted to turn the Poles to more loyal sentiments, and Hoym was anxious in what manner he should raise money, organise the province, and yet keep the Poles in good humour.

By Herr Schulz's classification and all the self-interested classification commissioners not the half was brought out that the Poles had formerly given in the fourth groschen.

An honest man (War Councillor v. Triebenfeld) helped Hoym out of this need. He called upon the Poles, a great part of whom still sat in Breslau, to forestall a new classification and to declare quickly how much they would add to the 10 per cent. The patriot represented to them that the Silesians gave 28 per cent and were happy with it. The Poles therefore declared voluntarily 14 per cent more, and so 24 per cent, and to maintain besides iron magazines, whereby all the troops could be fed, and without demanding for it a single groschen.

Count Hoym had wished only 5 per cent more, and was very much surprised when the honest man brought him the Poles' resolution; nay, he was so moved that for the present he would have nothing said of the tax in kind.

Hoym wollte nicht ungerufen nach Berlin gehen, Schulenburg gewann dadurch Zeit, er drängte sich bei des jezigen Königs Majestät ein, sein erstes Ziel war, Hoym zu stürzen. Er ließ mit Struensee durch Held Pasquille auf Hoym, Goldbeck und Triebenfeld verfertigen. Es war dies der pöbelhafteste Unsinn und nichts als plumpe Verläumdung. Indeß ward das Pasquill allewege gelesen. Der Monarch aber, beseelt von eben der erhabenen Herzensgüte wie sein höchstseliger König Vater, lieh dem Neid nicht ganz das Ohr, und Hoym blieb dennoch Minister; Schulenburg nahm daher seine Zuflucht zur List — er schrieb Hoym, daß der König mit seiner südpreußischen Administration unzufrieden sei, daß der Monarch es gern sehen würde, wann er das südpreußische Departement von selbst abgeben möchte. Hoym, den auch manche Schwäche drückte, ging in die Falle und bat den König, ihm das Departement quaest. abzunehmen. Nun war Schulenburg Sieger, er verfolgte diesen Sieg arg, Hoyms Todfeind, der Minister v. Voß, erhielt sofort das südpreußische Departement wieder, begleitet mit dem rothen Adlerorden und mit einem Kabinetschreiben, welches öffentlich in den Zeitungen bekannt gemacht wurde, wodurch man Voß bis in den Himmel erhob und Hoym bis zu den Antipoden warf. Zu spät sah Hoym seinen Irrthum ein und ärgerte sich darüber, daß Schulenburg ihm Meister in der List geworden war. Hoym ermannte sich indeß bald und drehete durch einen gewissen Gentz die Sache so, daß er nach Berlin berufen wurde. Er opferte

Moschin starosty, and so forth. This alone was a bold stroke, and all these men, who received nothing, afterwards breathed fire and flame.

The warm-hearted king died in 1797. Hoym did not care to go unsummoned to Berlin; Schulenburg thereby gained time; he pressed himself into the presence of the present king's Majesty; his first aim was to overthrow Hoym. He had Held make pasquils against Hoym, Goldbeck and Triebenfeld, with Struensee. This was the most rabid nonsense and nothing but clumsy calumny. Yet the pasquil was read everywhere. The monarch, however, animated by the same exalted goodness of heart as his late majesty the king his father, lent envy not quite his ear, and Hoym remained minister nevertheless; Schulenburg therefore took refuge in stratagem — he wrote to Hoym that the king was displeased with his South Prussian administration, that the monarch would gladly see him give up the South Prussian department of himself. Hoym, weighed down too by sundry weaknesses, fell into the trap and begged the king to take the department off his hands, quaest. [i.e. for money]. Now Schulenburg was victor; he followed up this victory harshly. Hoym's deadly enemy, Minister v. Voss, at once received the South Prussian department again, accompanied by the Red Eagle order and with a cabinet letter which was made public in the newspapers, whereby Voss was exalted to the heavens and Hoym cast to the antipodes. Too late Hoym saw his error and chafed that Schulenburg had become his master in craft.
