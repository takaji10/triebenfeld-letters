JAN WĄSICKI

KOLONIZACJA NIEMIECKA W OKRESIE PRUS POŁUDNIOWYCH 1793 – 1806

1. Kolonizacja wiejska.

Zagadnienie kolonizacji niemieckiej ziem polskich II i III zaboru pruskiego nie zostało do chwili obecnej wyczerpująco opracowane. Prace burżuazyjnych historyków niemieckich, w szczególności zaś dzieło Maxa Beheim-Schwarzbacha<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-1">1</a></sup>, omawiające całokształt działalności kolonizacyjnej Hohenzollernów, pełne pochwał i uwielbienia dla ich poczynań w tym kierunku, tylko w małej części opiera się na materiale archiwalnym, głównie wykorzystując poprzednie i fragmentaryczne opracowania. W tej na wskroś tendencyjnej pracy odrzucone zostały ponadto te wszystkie opracowania, które w sposób krytyczny omawiały akcję kolonizacyjną prowadzoną przez królów pruskich. Kolonizacji w okresie Prus Południowych poświęcił Beheim-Schwarzbach jedynie bardzo krótki rozdział. O jego niepełności i bezkrytyczności świadczyć może fakt, że autor oparł się głównie na pracy Holschego<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-2">2</a></sup>, nie ukazując całokształtu tego zagadnienia. Krytyczną ocenę pracy Schwarzbacha dał już Johannes Ziekursch, wykazując na przykładzie kolonizacji Śląska, prowadzonej w czasach Fryderyka Wielkiego, że nawet i te nikłe materiały archiwalne, użyte bezkrytycznie przez autora, są nie tylko ułamkowe, ale i mało wiarygodne<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-3">3</a></sup>.

Z nowszych prac historyków niemieckich, zajmujących się ubocznie tylko problemem kolonizacji w Prusach Południowych, wymienić należy prace Augusta Müllera<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-4">4</a></sup> i Antoniego Pytlaka<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-5">5</a></sup>. W przeciwieństwie do pracy Schwarzbacha obydwaj autorzy korzystali z materiałów archiwalnych, pochodzących głównie z Tajnego Państwowego Archiwum w Berlinie oraz z Archiwum Skarbu Królestwa Polskiego w Warszawie. Zawarte w tych pracach materiały pozwalają, w dużym przybliżeniu, ustalić ilość i miejscowości, gdzie zakładano kolonie, co jest szczególnie cenne, gdy nie ma możności bezpośredniego korzystania ze zbiorów wspomnianych archiwów. Z wydawnictw źródłowych do tego okresu na uwagę zasługują publikacje z tajnego archiwum państwowego w opracowaniu Stadelmanna<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-6">6</a></sup> i Prümersa<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-pl-7">7</a></sup>.

<div class="lit-en" lang="en" markdown="1">

JAN WĄSICKI

GERMAN COLONISATION IN THE PERIOD OF SOUTH PRUSSIA 1793 – 1806

1. Rural colonisation.

The question of the German colonisation of the Polish lands of the second and third Prussian partitions has not, to this day, been exhaustively studied. The works of bourgeois German historians, and in particular the work of Max Beheim-Schwarzbach<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-1">1</a></sup>, which treats the whole of the Hohenzollerns' colonising activity and is full of praise and adulation for their undertakings in this direction, rest only in small part on archival material and mainly use earlier and fragmentary studies. In this thoroughly tendentious work, moreover, all those studies were rejected that treated critically the colonising action carried out by the Prussian kings. To colonisation in the period of South Prussia Beheim-Schwarzbach devoted only a very short chapter. Its incompleteness and lack of criticism may be shown by the fact that the author relied mainly on the work of Holsche<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-2">2</a></sup>, without presenting the question as a whole. A critical assessment of Schwarzbach's work was already given by Johannes Ziekursch, who showed, on the example of the colonisation of Silesia carried out in the time of Frederick the Great, that even the scanty archival materials the author used uncritically are not only fragmentary but also unreliable<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-3">3</a></sup>.

Of the more recent works of German historians that deal only incidentally with the problem of colonisation in South Prussia, mention should be made of the works of August Müller<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-4">4</a></sup> and Antoni Pytlak<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-5">5</a></sup>. Unlike Schwarzbach's work, both authors used archival materials, coming mainly from the Secret State Archive in Berlin and from the Archive of the Treasury of the Kingdom of Poland in Warsaw. The materials contained in these works make it possible, in broad approximation, to establish the number of colonies founded and the places where they were founded, which is especially valuable when there is no possibility of using the collections of those archives directly. Of the source publications for this period, the publications from the secret state archive edited by Stadelmann<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-6">6</a></sup> and Prümers<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-7">7</a></sup> deserve attention.

Of Polish studies not relating strictly to the period that concerns us, the work of Stefan Komornicki<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-8">8</a></sup> should be cited, which rests mainly on the results of the work of Beheim-Schwarzbach mentioned above, and that of Kazimierz Zimmermann<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-9">9</a></sup>. Although this last author deals in principle with Frederician colonisation, he also presents very briefly its course in the years 1793—1806 in South Prussia. Finally, the most recent study of the question is the book of Z. Kaczmarczyk<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-10">10</a></sup>. I do not list here other, smaller contributory works scattered through various periodicals, in particular in the Deutsche Wissenschaftliche Zeitschrift f. Polen.

The source basis of the present article is the materials in the Voivodeship State Archive in Poznań, in the section S. P. Z. Although on their basis an exhaustive picture of the principles and course of colonisation in the whole province of South Prussia cannot be given, because of a certain fragmentariness of the material, the action carried on in the years 1793—1806 can nevertheless be reconstructed approximately, especially with regard to the department of the Poznań chamber.

\*

After the second and third partitions of the nobles' Commonwealth the former voivodeships of Poznań, Gniezno, Kalisz, Sieradz together with the land of Wieluń, Rawa, Łęczyca, Brześć Kujawski, the remainder of the voivodeship of Inowrocław left after the first partition, and the part of the voivodeship of Masovia lying on the left bank of the Vistula were joined to the state of the Hohenzollerns. From these lands a new province of the Prussian state was formed under the name of South Prussia. From part of the lands of the second and third partitions, that is from the northern parts of Masovia, Podlasie and part of Lithuania, a new province was organised after 1795: New East Prussia. A small strip of the voivodeship of Cracow was joined to Silesia under the name of New Silesia. After the third partition of the nobles' Commonwealth the frontiers of the Hohenzollern state ran in the east along the river Pilica, the middle Vistula, the lower Bug and the middle course of the Niemen.

\*

Before proceeding to discuss the colonisation carried on in South Prussia by the absolute Prussian monarchs, it must be stressed that it was nothing new, unknown until then in this former province of the nobles' Commonwealth; on the contrary, it was only a further prolongation of the action carried on for a long time by the Polish feudal lords. The "Olęder" settlements that arose in large numbers in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, founded mostly on uncultivated land that needed clearing and draining, were dictated by considerations of an economic nature. The private initiative of the Polish feudal lords until then was, after the taking of Greater Poland, taken over and carried on, however, by the Prussian authorities.

Podobnie kształtowała się sytuacja w Prusach Południowych, mimo że w tym okresie starano się, choć w pewnym stopniu, uniknąć błędów kolonizacji fryderycjańskiej. Często osiedlano po dwie lub trzy rodziny pośród ludności polskiej. Motywem więc uzasadniającym kolonizację była dążność do zagospodarowania terenów dotąd nie uprawianych i zwiększenia ilości rąk do pracy w domenach i na folwarkach. W tych więc wypadkach jest rzeczą bardzo wątpliwą, czy ta najuboższa warstwa ludności wiejskiej, zatrudniana w charakterze pracowników najemnych na folwarkach, mogła spełniać ową „misję kulturalną“, jak to szumnie głosili niemieccy historycy. A i o innych kategoriach kolonistów oraz ich znajomości uprawy roli nie było lepszej opinii, nawet wśród współczesnych. Immediately after the taking of the Polish lands in the second partition, Frederick William II thought of colonising his new acquisitions with settlers brought from the Reich. He wrote on this matter to the president of the Poznań chamber, Buggenhagen, that he was very anxious to increase the population of the newly taken province and to raise the standard of agriculture in South Prussia<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-11">11</a></sup>. It would follow from these royal plans that colonisation was to be undertaken mainly for economic and population reasons. Undoubtedly it is from these two aspects that the colonising aims of the Hohenzollerns in the period of South Prussia should be viewed. For the colonisation carried out cannot be identified — as was done until recently — with an action of Germanisation, all the more so since in the times of the nobles' Commonwealth settlers, mostly German, were drawn to these lands. T. Ladenberger also drew attention to this in his work "On Frederician colonisation in Silesia", discussing both the opinions of Polish and German historians on the matter and the course and results of the colonisation of that period. Bourgeois German historians presented the colonising action of the Prussian kings, both in Silesia and in Pomerania, as a "work of culture", and regarded the colonists as "bearers of culture". T. Ladenberger, showing the real aim of colonisation, states at the same time, and rightly, that "colonisation served above all the royal treasury and the great estates"<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-12">12</a></sup>, and that its cultural effects were downright lamentable.

The situation in South Prussia took a similar shape, although in this period an attempt was made, at least to some degree, to avoid the mistakes of Frederician colonisation. Colonies continued to be founded mainly on uncultivated land requiring clearing or draining. A considerable part of the settlers who arrived, as I shall show below, were settled on the royal domains, on the manor farms of private feudal lords and in the forests as hired labourers. Often two or three families were settled among the Polish population. The motive justifying colonisation was thus the wish to bring into use land not cultivated until then and to increase the number of hands for work on the domains and the manor farms. In these cases it is therefore very doubtful whether this poorest stratum of the rural population, employed as hired labourers on the manor farms, could fulfil that "cultural mission" so loudly proclaimed by German historians. Nor was there a better opinion of the other categories of colonists and of their knowledge of farming, even among contemporaries. Holsche himself, who had the best knowledge of contemporary conditions, states that the Württembergers, who settled in the greatest numbers in South Prussia, were bad farmers and that the whole action did not pay at all<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-13">12a</a></sup>. The settlers who arrived, and especially those who had been given small pieces of land, farming in hard conditions, left South Prussia when the prospect opened before them of obtaining better conditions of settlement in another country. For colonisation in the period of South Prussia was carried on on principles different from those of Frederician colonisation. Large farms, relying on the work of hired labourers, gave a guarantee of survival and development, all the more so since, as we shall see, the legal position of the colonist, who paid only a rent and was not bound by any other ties of feudal dependence, differed from the conditions prevailing at that time both on the nobles' manor farms and on the royal domains.

The Germanising factor was not the most important in colonisation in this period. For not only colonists from the Reich were settled here, but also colonists from Bohemia, inhabitants of South Prussia, and in New East Prussia even Tatars and Lithuanian peasants. This does not mean, however, that the Hohenzollerns did not try at this time to Germanise the Polish nobility. They did so through the administrative and judicial offices, the schools, and by drawing the nobility into military and civil service.

Frederick William II in his first instruction expressly warned that the colonising action should not cause a loss of population from the provinces of the Prussian state. It followed clearly from the royal order, therefore, that above all newcomers from the German Reich were to be recruited and settled as colonists. To encourage colonists to settle in the new lands certain concessions and financial help from the state were usually provided, and in addition exemption from military service. This last point was especially important, since the newcomers were well aware that in the militaristic Prussian state the duty of military service would be their heaviest burden. Had permission been given for colonists from the provinces in the hereditary possession of the Hohenzollerns to settle in South Prussia, there would undoubtedly have been too many willing to improve their conditions of life and to obtain the benefits due in such cases. This outflow of people, however, mainly peasants, on whom rested the whole burden of tax obligations and military service, would for some time have been a great loss for the Prussian state, and so their settling was forbidden from the very start.

From the exchange of letters between minister Voss and the Prussian envoy in Warsaw, Buchholz, it can be established that Buchholz put forward a proposal to appoint a special official to whom colonisation matters would be entrusted. These tasks were in the end entrusted, however, to the war and domains chambers, and minister Voss for his part issued an order that settlers arriving in future in the administrative districts of the chambers be given proper care and suitable help in settling<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-14">13</a></sup>.

It might seem from the minister's first orders that Voss would at once set about putting the royal commands into effect; this, however, was not so. As we shall see further, Voss and the other ministers took an unwilling attitude to the whole action and set about putting it into effect only on the king's express order.

At the very outset Voss rejected the offer of the notary Hassold of Hamburg, made less than a year after the taking of the province, who wished to undertake the recruiting of colonists for South Prussia<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-15">14</a></sup>. The minister gave as the ground for his position the fact that no preparations had been made in this province to begin a colonising action. Such preparations would necessarily take a long time, to make suitable land ready for settling the arriving settlers. Besides, he considered the method of recruiting unsuitable in this case, because already now, and certainly in future, there would be many willing, chiefly from Mecklenburg, Lüneburg and other provinces of the Reich, who would wish to settle in the new province. In this particular case, then, Voss wished to depart from the method of obtaining colonists by recruiting, as practised by Frederick II.

Minister Voss's unfavourable attitude in this matter must also have been affected by the fact that in none of the royal commands was there the slightest mention of the amount of the fund provided for the colonising action, and without a fairly large sum of money set aside for the purpose the whole action could not have the slightest chance of success. Wishing to begin the colonising action, Frederick William approved a project laid before him in July 1793, under which the nobility receiving the South Prussian indigenat, necessary for acquiring estates in this province, was obliged, in proportion to the value of the estates acquired, to settle a certain number of colonists (one family for every 2000 thalers), but it is not known whether this project was put into effect<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-16">15</a></sup>. The royal edict issued on the indigenat<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-17">16</a></sup> says nothing of this condition. Since, however, the grant of the indigenat depended solely on the king, the project of settling colonists on the occasion of the acquisition of estates in South Prussia by the Prussian nobility might have had a chance of success, and the king would still make attempts to carry it out in 1804. Until then, judging from the king's enquiries addressed to minister Voss, the royal commands were not strictly observed.

From these initial plans it is clear that according to the king's intentions the colonising action was to go two ways: one carried on by the state, the other by the private purchasers of estates in the new province, which in fact happened later.

It was also possible — according to the instruction — to move peasants from farms lying next to the manor farms to the abandoned farms, and to fill their places with arriving colonists classed as cottagers and smallholders, or else to incorporate the land into the manor farms. In this way an attempt was made on the one hand to create larger peasant farms and on the other to assure the royal domains of a labour force for which the main, indeed the only, means of support would be work on the manor farm.

Similar principles were to be applied in founding colonies on land near the towns. The chambers were to see to the building of rented houses in which artisans, day labourers and Lassbauer (lassyci) would find lodging.

This care to settle free hired labourers on the one hand, and then to create large farms for colonists, bore witness to the far-reaching changes already under way in rural conditions. The feudal economy had at this time begun to outlive itself and slowly, first on the royal estates and then on the nobles' manor farms, to take on the forms of a capitalist economy. The transition to new forms of cultivation, the clearing of forests and the draining of marshes required the settling of a large number of free cottagers, gardeners and smallholders, compelled to sell their labour to the owners of manor farms. And so, although in principle there was constant talk of settling colonist-farmers, at the same time especially many hired labourers were settled, so needed on the manor farms. The greatest trouble for Schrötter was the question of settling the families classed as Büdner (Büdnerfamilien). For in the colonies being created there was no room for them, because of their fairly numerous inflow despite the restrictions applied, and founding special colonies for this category of settlers seemed unjustified to Schrötter. Until then the obstacle to abolishing this obligation had been the lack of hands for work on the manor farm. As in South Prussia, so in this province too the lessees of manor farms were to be obliged to settle day labourers at their own cost. In this case the principle that they must possess means of their own of 60 thalers was even departed from, since in Schrötter's opinion too they could not be sent back.

Colonisation in New East Prussia made ever more visible progress, although there was no such express command and care for this action on the part of the king and the General Directory. In 1803 a larger number of colonists was settled in New East Prussia than in the Warsaw department of South Prussia<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-65">64</a></sup>.

The "Reichs-Kolonisten-Werbungs-Büreau" founded by Captain Nothardt at Ohringen near Heilbronn in Württemberg also carried on a lively activity. The funds set aside for this purpose thus grew more and more. Seeing, however, the very marked progress of this action in New East Prussia, carried on moreover at lower cost, the Prussian king decided to abolish the recruiting office at Ohringen, whose upkeep involved rather high costs. Captain Nothardt was placed at the head of the Kalisz chamber with the special task of supervising the course of the colonising action in all the departments of South Prussia. The recruiting of colonists was entrusted to the Franconian chamber at Ansbach<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-67">66</a></sup>. Already before this, however, namely in May 1802, a more detailed instruction on this matter than before had been drawn up for South Prussia, differing fairly considerably from the one issued for the chambers and Capt. Nothardt<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-68">67</a></sup>. For it took account of the points on which minister Schrötter had expressed his reservations.

The data cited by Hoffmann, that by 1804 four colonists of groups V and VI had been settled, are therefore not confirmed<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-73">72</a></sup>.

We have no figures for how this matter stood in the other departments, but it may be supposed that it did not depart from the general principles. Thus, although the procedure and principles of colonisation changed fairly substantially compared with the first instructions, and although, contrary to the king's reservations, attempts continued to be made to limit the number of settlers of the first, and even the second, category, it was precisely these colonists who flowed into South Prussia in the greatest numbers. For settling as many hired labourers as possible on the domains was in the king's interest. Minister Schrötter criticised this kind of procedure and the colonisation orders issued for South Prussia, since these categories of settlers, in the opinion — justified, moreover — of the administrator of New East Prussia, showed the strongest tendencies to move beyond the frontiers of the Prussian provinces when they were promised better settlement conditions elsewhere. Already in 1803 the chambers' reports sent to the General Directory very often contain complaints of colonists' secret flight. The cause of this frequent abandonment of both South Prussia and New East Prussia by colonists who had only just arrived was the possibility of moving to Russia<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-74">73</a></sup>. For in this period a very lively colonising action was being carried on in Russia. The appeal of Tsar Alexander I caused many families of colonists, chiefly smallholders, to begin moving to the new lands, where they were undoubtedly given better settlement conditions. The colonists secretly sold the stock allotted to them, so that the chambers had to announce a prohibition on buying these things from colonists without their consent. It is difficult to give general precise figures for the number of colonists settled in South Prussia. Some light is thrown here only by minister Voss's report, laid before the king. Relying on the list drawn up by the Poznań chamber in 1804, giving both the number of colonies founded and of settlers who arrived, we can state that their number for the Poznań department was 2324 persons in all. It should be supposed that the colonising action in the years 1805—1806 did not increase, but rather weakened, because of the lack of funds and the difficulties the chambers' officials met in settling colonists. From A. Pytlak's lists it cannot be concluded whether, as in the Poznań department, part of the colonists were settled on the manor farms as hired labourers. Nor can we establish what area of land was allotted to them. Since, however, the same principles applied in settling colonists, it may be supposed that the situation in the other departments took a similar shape to that in the department of the Poznań chamber. Probably the colonists in the Warsaw department received larger allotments of land because of its poorer quality, which was in any case mentioned in the instructions sent to the chambers by the central authorities.

The colonisation of the period of South Prussia might properly be called a Württemberg colonisation. For the great majority of the settlers who arrived came from that part of Germany. After the occupation of the left bank of the Rhine by the revolutionary troops of France the inflow of colonists from those lands increased fairly considerably. There were relatively many colonists from the Hohenzollern provinces, despite the difficulties made at first over settling them. The table cited shows the origin of the colonists settled in the Poznań department up to 1805. (See below.)

The principles and course of colonisation in South Prussia in the years 1793—1806 differ fairly clearly from the colonisation of Polish lands carried on by Frederick II both in Silesia and in West Prussia. Frederician colonisation is well known, since it has received many exhaustive studies, and the work of T. Ladenberger cited at the beginning sums up the results of the research carried on in this direction. It does not therefore seem to us justified to present it again. It is necessary only to show the differences in the principles and course of colonisation in its two periods.

The fundamental aim of Frederician colonisation was to increase the population of the country; the question of the newcomers' usefulness in the province did not play the most important part here. For these reasons the element arriving here had the worst possible reputation, both professionally and morally. In point of farming technique the newcomers stood no higher than the Polish population. Hence the opinions of bourgeois German historians "on the cultural mission of the colonists" remain empty phrases. In the period of colonisation in South Prussia, as we have been able to establish, newcomers wishing to settle on the land were required to have professional training in farming and to possess suitable stock. The colonists were divided into categories according to property criteria, and the state's help was made to depend on the amount of the means brought in.

The colonists of the period of Frederick II's colonising action were in principle allotted small areas of land, so that after the expiry of the free years, unable to live on it under relatively heavy tax obligations, they fell into poverty. The Büdner could earn a living in West Prussia in the Netze district only while fortification works, the building of canals and the like lasted; after these works were finished they no longer had any possibility of supporting themselves by wage work. In South Prussia an attempt was made to create fairly large farms, from at least 20 morgen to 6 łany, which had a chance of surviving and developing further. Day labourers, on the other hand, were settled beside the royal domains, forests, nobles' manor farms and larger colonies or near towns, in order to assure them steady wage work, which was to be the basis of their livelihood. The changes in agriculture, the growing industrialisation of the manor farm, required an increase in the number of hands for work, and that is why the largest number of colonists of this category was settled in South Prussia.

The costs of setting up colonies in the period of South Prussia rose by half compared with the costs of Frederician times, for an attempt was made to assure the colonists better conditions of life. Yet the colonisation of the years 1793—1803 too was a failure. The newcomers were allotted waste land or land requiring clearing or draining, which undoubtedly put off many of them, who had expected to receive in this province land already prepared for cultivation, and more of it. For this reason a large number of colonists either returned to the Reich or left for Russia, where the conditions of settlement were more favourable.

The colonising action was also carried on by private landowners. On the possessions of the Prince of Orange preparations for settling colonists were already being made in 1799, and Frederick William III assigned 50,000 thalers for this purpose<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-83">81</a></sup>.

It is necessary, however briefly, to look more closely at the colonising activity of the Prince of Orange, since this will let us see how this action was carried on on private estates. William Frederick (later King of the Netherlands), Prince of Orange, married to the eldest daughter of Frederick William II, King of Prussia, Frederica Louisa Wilhelmina, acquired in South Prussia the Wydzim estates near Wolsztyn, then the Stęszew estates, consisting of the town of Stęszew and the villages and manor farms of Chmielniki, Witobel, Dembno, Kremplewo, Tomice and Mirosławki. His estates thus stretched from Wolsztyn to the neighbourhood of Kościan and further eastward along the Warta to the district of Pyzdry, grouping themselves finally around Stęszew.

For the teacher, the sołtys and the cemetery 21 morgen were allotted<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-88">86</a></sup>. The colonists came mostly from the Mark and Pomerania. We have no exact information on how long the German villages and colonies founded there lasted. Breyer reports that already in 1806 two families of colonists asked to be allowed to leave Mały Las and move to East Prussia, and in 1818 forty families from the village of Święcia came forward with the same request. The villages founded thus did not last long<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-89">87</a></sup>.

We also have information that Count Łubieński founded 11 colonies in his starosty of Guzów, settling 167 colonists, for which he was decorated by Frederick William III with the Order of the Red Eagle<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-90">88</a></sup>. Konstanty Milewski, too, owner of the Borszewice estates in the district of Lutomiersk, concluded a contract with 10 colonists from the Netze district and allotted them 10 łany of forest<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-91">89</a></sup>. Attempts by the lessees of royal estates to settle cottager colonists did not succeed, since both the lessees themselves and the settlers, who found worse conditions there, were unwilling about this action because of the considerable money costs. The attempts at settlement ended most often in failure<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-92">90</a></sup>.

2. Urban colonisation

Parallel with the conduct of rural colonisation, efforts were made to settle colonists in the towns. From the first instruction, however, it appeared clearly that what was meant was not to attract specialists for the further development of the manufacturing industry existing in Greater Poland, but only specialists in building, such as bricklayers, carpenters and the like. This by no means means that the settling of other arriving artisans was abandoned, but this came about when a fairly considerable number of newcomers from the left bank of the Rhine flowed into South Prussia.

Already in 1793, immediately after the entry of the Prussian troops, when the action of organising the new new province was in full swing, the chamber in Piotrków came forward with a proposal to settle in the towns colonists from the branch of crafts mentioned above, the lack of whom was particularly felt at that time<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-93">91</a></sup>.

In the order issued on 16 January 1794<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-94">92</a></sup> the General Directory promised help and financial support for colonists settled in the towns. This help depended on whether the colonist came from outside the frontiers of the Prussian kingdom or from the Hohenzollern provinces, and finally on whether he settled in royal or private towns. In principle colonists could settle only in those towns where there was a lack of specialists in the branch of craft the colonist practised. According to the instruction, newcomers from outside the Prussian state received: 1) free citizenship, 2) a free licence to carry on a craft, or the right of mastership, 3) a three-year rebate of excise, 4) six years' exemption from the service, that is from payments for the upkeep of the night watchman and the fire-fighting equipment and other town payments and personal burdens such as watch duty and the like, 5) the colonist, together with his sons and the journeymen coming with him, was exempted from the duty of military service. Like the colonists settled in the villages, the town colonists too received travel money at the rate of 2 groschen a head for each mile. Besides, they were granted a special allowance for the transport of their movable property. The state's financial help also took the form of paying the yearly rent of their lodging and partly reimbursing the costs of setting up the workshop and buying tools that could not be brought with them. The order stresses once again that this help is intended exclusively for colonists settling in towns where there is a lack of this category of artisans. They were not forbidden to settle in other towns, but in such cases the state's financial help fell away. They were granted as a benefit only free citizenship, a licence or the right of mastership, and exemption from the cantons. Still greater restrictions were applied to artisans coming from within the Prussian state. They received, it is true, the concessions listed above, but were not exempt from the cantons.

The General Directory, agreeing to the proposals of the South Prussian chambers, recommended that settlers be brought above all to the seats of the provincial colleges, that is Poznań and Piotrków. Newcomers were promised greater financial help in the form of a special payment of 10—15 thalers a year. This applied only to masters and journeymen practising their trade independently.

As to private towns, the General Directory considered that their owners ought in their own interest to strive to settle artisans in them and to build on the empty plots. Colonists in these towns were to be granted the right to build freely on a plot, three years' exemption from all land taxes and obligations to the lord of the town, and finally a year's exemption from paying for lodging. Special credits were to be set aside for building houses.

Such were the first instructions on settling colonists in the towns sent by the General Directory to the chambers in Poznań and Piotrków.

The chambers, however, were concerned mainly with the newcomers from Württemberg, a relatively large number of whom had come to South Prussia, and for them too they tried to obtain increased financial help from the state. To these efforts, however, they received a negative answer from the General Directory, which instructed the chambers that the purpose of the fund set aside for colonisation was help for all the colonists settling, without making particular distinctions<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-102">100</a></sup>. In the particular case of the colonist joiner Baltazar Schiedt, settled in Krotoszyn, the General Directory listed the benefits due to him under the order of January 1794 on this matter.

Those colonists, however, deserved special support who possessed some means, thereby giving a guarantee that after settling they would be able to maintain the workshop they founded by themselves without the state's help. At first the amount of means a colonist should possess to receive the state's financial help was not exactly fixed. Only in 1802 was the amount of means a colonist must possess to obtain permission to settle fixed at 200 thalers. Only this category of persons, then, was entitled to the state's financial help needed for setting up a workshop. All the other colonists were entitled to the benefits listed in the order of 1794, but without financial help. They received a free licence and the right of mastership only after passing the appropriate examination.

As follows from the facts cited above, then, the Prussian authorities did not attach great importance to urban colonisation. This was undoubtedly bound up with the whole policy of the Prussian authorities towards the towns, industry and crafts in the period of South Prussia. The strong development of manufacturing industry in the other Prussian provinces, and especially in Silesia, did not allow the Prussian authorities to settle specialists of this branch in South Prussia, so as not to create dangerous competition for the Prussian provinces. For these reasons, too, urban colonisation was confined to only a small number of building craftsmen. Only the increased inflow of settlers from the lands on the left bank of the Rhine brought about the settlement here of a certain number of weavers, chiefly in Dąbie on the Ner<sup class="lit-fn"><a href="#n-en-103">101</a></sup>. Unfortunately we cannot give the exact total number of town colonists who arrived and settled in South Prussia. In Voss's reports, tables and lists there was not the slightest mention of it. It must therefore be thought that their number was negligible, since the main stress was laid on rural colonisation.
