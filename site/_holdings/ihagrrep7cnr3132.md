Nine years after the Polish rising of 1794, two of its Kuyavian participants were still abroad. In January 1803 Antoni Prusimski, the former Starost whose estates Prussia had confiscated and given away, and the canon Xaver Jezierski petitioned King Friedrich Wilhelm III from Dresden to be allowed home to South Prussia. The file follows the petition through the two ministers who reported on it, the Grand Chancellor Goldbeck and Count Haugwitz, to the King's grant of 10 May 1803 and the orders that carried it out. Their report is the fullest account in the edition of the case against Prusimski and of what became of his estates: Trąbczyn to the Prince of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, Pawłów to General von Sanitz, Smiłowice to General von Bischofswerder. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

After the rising was put down, the Prussian authorities in South Prussia, the province formed from Prussia's share of the second partition, proceeded against its participants; those who had fled and did not return were sued for the confiscation of their property, and the confiscated estates were given to Prussian officers in 1796. Goldbeck, as Grand Chancellor, headed the justice; Haugwitz was a cabinet minister; Otto Karl Friedrich von Voss was the minister for South Prussia, who countersigned the order to the Chamber at Poznań. The papers are a ministry file: letters received, the drafts of the replies with their addresses and dispatch notes in the margin, and copies of the report and of the King's orders. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The petition referred, February 1803.** The King sent the petition of "a former Starost von Prusimski" and "one Xaver Jezierski" to Goldbeck and Haugwitz for a report; Goldbeck had the Government at Poznań asked for the files on their offences and their property <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. In April the Government sent the files of the confiscation proceedings in six volumes, and Goldbeck passed them to Haugwitz with his draft of the joint report <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/2/' | relative_url }}">2</a>]</span>. Haugwitz signed it, adding only that he expressed no opinion on whether the confiscation must stand, since the point lay outside his department <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/3/' | relative_url }}">3</a>]</span>.

**The joint report, 30 April 1803.** Prusimski was accused of having gone from his estates near Kalisz to his Smiłowice estates in Kuyavia on 2 August 1794, of having received the neighbouring nobility there daily, and of the Kuyavian insurrection having been concluded with him on 21 August, its act of confederation drafted and executed; he was also said to have been among the South Prussian noblemen who met at Dresden in the winter of 1793/94. The ministers note that these accusations rested mostly on an anonymous Memoire raisonné and had not been closely examined. The Government had brought only the confiscation proceedings against him, not the criminal proceedings against absent offenders. Summoned in public, he had held out hope of his return from Italy, where his wife was said to have had to stay for her health and where she also died; when he did not appear, the sentence of confiscation was passed, and his Trąbczyn, Pawłów and Smiłowice estates were given to Hohenlohe, Sanitz and Bischofswerder. Jezierski, canon of Włocławek and parish priest at Neustadt, was said to have drafted the rising's proclamations, raised men and sat on its security commission; his benefices were confiscated. Neither had lost his nobility. Punished by the confiscation and nine years' banishment more harshly than many who had returned and paid a fine, and not dangerous, the two might, the ministers advised, be allowed to return <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/5/' | relative_url }}">5</a>]</span>.

**The grant, May 1803.** On 10 May the King, agreeing that they had been punished hard enough, granted the return <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/6/' | relative_url }}">6</a>]</span>. On 11 May the ministers sent the Government at Poznań the files back with a copy of the order, and told the two at Dresden that they might return, on the assumption that they would conduct themselves as [loyal?] subjects; the confiscation stood, and Jezierski remained deprived of his offices and benefices, but they need fear no further inquiry or punishment <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/7/' | relative_url }}">7</a>]</span>. Goldbeck sent Haugwitz the order and the drafts <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/4/' | relative_url }}">4</a>]</span>; a slip bound after his letter notes how the fair copies were to be signed and sent <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/4/' | relative_url }}">4</a>]</span>. On 30 May Haugwitz asked Voss to countersign the notice to the Chamber at Poznań and returned the papers to Goldbeck <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/8/' | relative_url }}">8</a>]</span>, and the Chamber was commanded to take the two men's oath of allegiance and report that it had been done; the order went out by post on 8 June <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr3132/9/' | relative_url }}">9</a>]</span>.
