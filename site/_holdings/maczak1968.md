A scholarly article, not a primary source: Antoni Mączak, "Export of Grain and the Problem of Distribution of National Income in the Years 1550-1650", printed in *Acta Poloniae Historica* 18 (1968), pages 75 to 98. The journal is the Institute of History of the Polish Academy of Sciences' review in western languages; the article is in English, translated by Ewa Tymowska. Its author, the Polish economic historian Antoni Mączak (1928-2003), wrote it from the registers of the river tolls, the accounts of royal estates and the tax registers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The edition gives the whole article: its text and notes, its seven tables as text, and its map and its chart as pictures. It is the edition's second holding of published literature, and its one document is the whole article. <!-- context -->

### Why it is here

The editor added the article as background to the farming of the edition's estates, and above all of the Olęder settlers, free farmers on contract whom the owners of Trąbczyn had planted in its woods by the 1750s (<a href="{{ '/sources/app53170koningr136/' | relative_url }}">53/17/0/-/Konin Gr.136</a>). How useful it is will be judged later (the editor). The article names neither Trąbczyn, nor the district of Konin, nor the Olęders. What it describes is the trade their grain went into: the grain floated down the Vistula to Gdańsk, and what that trade did to lords, gentry and peasants. <!-- context -->

Three passages come nearest to the edition's estates. Great Poland, the article says, exported grain "at least from the regions near the Vistula, and a little along the Warta river", the river on which Konin lies. Its Table 1 counts "Cuyavia and east Great Poland" among the regions whose grain passed the toll at Włocławek, with a share of between 11 and 20 per cent. And of Royal Prussia it writes that "the intensive agricultural and dairy farming of the Dutch colonists grew dynamically": the settlers of the Vistula delta, from whom the Olęders took their name <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/maczak1968/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

### Contents

The article asks who gained and who lost by Poland's grain trade in the century from 1550 to 1650. Landowners answered the Dutch demand at Gdańsk by enlarging their demesnes and the serf labour that worked them, at the cost of the peasants, whose holdings and yields fell. From the registers of the water toll at Włocławek, which survive for 15 years between 1537 and 1576, the article shows how each region's share of the grain sent down the river rose and fell with the harvests (Table 1, and a map of the provinces and the toll stations) <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/maczak1968/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

It divides the grain by its senders: magnates, rich and medium gentry, merchants and clergy. The great estates sent the largest and the steadiest part of it (Table 2), sold at prices taken from the accounts of the royal estate of Malbork (Table 3), and two families of Little Poland, the Tęczyńskis and the Firlejs, sent between them 15 per cent of all the grain of that region in the years counted (Table 4). The grain registered at Warsaw, chiefly from Little Poland, rose to 25 976 lasts a year in 1615-1619 (Table 5) <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/maczak1968/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

The profits of the trade, the article argues, let the magnates buy up the land of the lesser gentry: in the voivodship of Cracow the owners of ten villages or more held 8.7 per cent of the villages in 1581 and 29.8 per cent in 1629 (Table 7), and Table 6 counts the manors that changed owners. It follows the money through Gdańsk, whose merchants took their margin between the river boats and the ships, and estimates each region's balance of grain. It ends by setting Little Poland, where the growth of the surplus impoverished peasants and lesser gentry, beside Royal Prussia, where peasants could compete with their lords, and Masovia, split into small properties; a chart compares prices and wages at Gdańsk, Cracow and Warsaw <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/maczak1968/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

The editor supplied the article as a PDF of the digital copy held in RCIN, the digital repository of the scientific institutes of the Polish Academy of Sciences, where every page carries the repository's stamp. The PDF has the whole article, pages 75 to 98, and nothing else of the journal. <!-- context -->

**The text.** The PDF's own text layer is a machine reading with many faults, and it was not taken over. The article was read from the page images in the working session, page by page and by paragraph, and the reading was then compared word by word with the machine reading to make sure nothing was left out. Each numbered line of a page is a paragraph, a heading, a footnote, a caption or a row of a table; the running heads, the page numbers and the repository's stamp are left out, and italics are not marked. The footnote marks are the raised figures of the print, and each page's notes stand at its foot. The print is given as it stands, its slips included ("landlors", "differenciated", "the sale or rye", and the year 1663 in Table 6, which the text gives as 1653). A word broken across the end of a page is given whole on the page where it begins. There is no mark of doubt. <!-- context -->

**The tables.** The seven tables are given as text, each row a line with its cells between upright strokes; a figure the print sets across two columns stands in the first of them, with "<" in the second. The reading view draws them as tables. The figures were checked against their own sums where the table has them (Tables 2 and 4). In Table 2 italic figures are percentages and upright figures lasts of grain; the italics are not marked, as the note under the table explains the difference and the figures show it. <!-- context -->

**The pages.** Only the map (page 81) and the chart of prices and wages (page 97) are shown as pictures, cut from the PDF; their captions and labels are also given as text. The other pages are given as text only. <!-- context -->

**No translation.** The article is printed in English, the translation of 1968 by Ewa Tymowska, so the edition does not translate it again; its page has no English tab. <!-- context -->

**The summary** was written in German and English and each statement held to the article. <!-- context -->
