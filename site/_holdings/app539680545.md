Albert Breyer (1889-1939), a teacher at the German school in Sompolno, studied the German settlements of central Poland and what became of them. In the 1930s he collected evidence on one question: what happened to the German surnames of the country around Zagórów when the families who bore them became Polish. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The German settlements of the Trąbczyn country – the Olęder colonies of Łazińsk and Emilienheim, the Hauländer villages – kept their language and their parishes into the twentieth century, and their surnames with them. After Poland's restoration the state offered a legal way to change a name, and many German families took instead the shorter way their clergy and commune heads showed them: a German name made to look Polish. Breyer, who collected among these settlements in the 1930s, gathered the evidence on these sheets. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The names.** In the commune of Zagórów, from as early as 1843 and 1855, German surnames had been given Polish forms: Bobel became Rybicki, Grüning Zielinski, Breitkreutz Breitkrejc, Schmeckel Szmigielski, Stange Sztanga, Niemann Nowak <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>, <a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/2/' | relative_url }}">2</a>]</span>. Breyer lists some fifty such names, from Arminski to Wilmański <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/3/' | relative_url }}">3</a>, <a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/5/' | relative_url }}">5</a>]</span>. Others were not Polonised but translated: Zech became Czech, Rolle Rola, Sack Zak, Blum Kwiatkowska, Erdmann Ziemiecki, Hoppe Chmielewski, Bohrer Swiderski <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/5/' | relative_url }}">5</a>]</span>.

**An old practice.** From a Breslau print of 1741, George Schlag's "Polnisch-deutsche Briefe", Breyer copied eight Polish names with their German equivalents, Wojciech Wilkotek as Albrecht Bärwolff, Jakub Wierzbrecki as Jacob Weidner: the exchange of names between the languages was as old as the German schools of Silesia <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/4/' | relative_url }}">4</a>]</span>.

**Breyer's essay.** Whoever walks through Warsaw, Lublin or a market town, Breyer writes, meets German names on the shop signs whose bearers may not be addressed as Germans. Changing a name by law, announced in the state newspaper "Monitor Polski", was slow and costly; commune heads and evangelical pastors, among them Pastor Winkler in Wieluń and Pastor v. Boerner in Zduńska-Wola, took the simpler way of putting the whole name into Polish, so that a Jahn became Koguziński and a Blum Kwiatkowski. "Thus German family names are mutilated", he concludes, "fitted to the language-feeling of the majority people" <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/7/' | relative_url }}">7</a>]</span>. Pastor Winkler is presumably the "P. Winkler" whose list of names opens the collection <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

**The schools.** A separate essay records that the German secondary schools of the Łódź region, at Łódź, Pabianice, Zgierz and Sompolno, had agreed on one twelve-year course of study <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/6/' | relative_url }}">6</a>]</span>.

### Transcription

The five list sheets were read by Claude from enlarged bands of the images. The editor settled the doubtful readings, and their readings replaced most of them. The three prose sheets are the editor's own transcription, kept as typed.

### Translation and summaries

Claude translated the documents into English. In the lists every name stays as written and only the headings and the common words are translated. Claude wrote the summaries, and a separate pass checked every statement against the document; a statement the document does not support was cut or weakened.
