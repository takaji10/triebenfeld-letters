Ten photographed sheets from the papers of Albert Breyer (1889-1939), a teacher at the German school in Sompolno and a historian of the German settlements in central Poland, kept in the State Archive in Poznań among the material the archive calls "Polonisierta Namen [gmina Zagórów - nazwiska mieszkańców]". They are research sheets on one question: what became of the German surnames of the country around Zagórów when their bearers turned Polish. Five sheets are lists of names, three are prose notes, and the tenth is shown with the holding and carries no transcription. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The German settlements of the Trąbczyn country – the Olęder colonies of Łazińsk and Emilienheim, the Hauländer villages – kept their language and their parishes into the twentieth century, and their surnames with them. After Poland's restoration the state offered a legal way to change a name, and many German families took instead the shorter way their clergy and commune heads showed them: a German name made to look Polish. Breyer, who collected among these settlements in the 1930s, gathered the evidence on these sheets. <!-- context -->

### Contents

The first sheet <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span> is headed "P. Winkler Polonisierte Namen" and lists nine pairs of a German surname and its Polonised form – Bobel – Rybicki, Grüning – Zielinski, Breitkreutz – Breitkrejc, Schmeckel – Szmigielski, Stange – Sztanga among them – the first dated 1855, the last line "1843. Szuberski" <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. Under the first pair stands "Gem. Zagórów", the commune these names belong to. The pastor of the heading is the Pastor Winkler in Wieluń whom the seventh document names by name as one of the two clergymen who had German names transferred into Polish wholesale <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/7/' | relative_url }}">7</a>]</span>.

The second sheet <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/2/' | relative_url }}">2</a>]</span> lists nineteen numbered pairs under the head "Polonisirte Deutsche Namen", from Niemann – Nowak to Brodel – Brudel; the third <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/3/' | relative_url }}">3</a>]</span> is an alphabetical list of the Polonised surnames from Arminski to Wilmański, the letters C, E, T, U, V and Z left empty. The fourth <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/4/' | relative_url }}">4</a>]</span> is Breyer's excerpt of a Breslau print of 1741, the "Polnisch-deutsche Briefe" of George Schlag, morning preacher at St. Hieronymi and moderator of the Polish school there: eight pairs of a Polish first name and surname against a German one, from Wojciech Wilkotek – Albrecht Bärwolff to Jakub Wierzbrecki – Jacob Weidner – evidence that the exchange of names across the languages is as old as the German schools of Silesia <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/4/' | relative_url }}">4</a>]</span>.

The fifth sheet and its continuation <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/5/' | relative_url }}">5</a>]</span> carry a numbered list of fifty-three Polonised surnames written across three columns. Between entries 42 and 43 stand seventeen pairs in which a German name and its Polish translation stand against each other – Zech – Czech, Rolle – Rola, Sack – Zak, Blum – Kwiatkowska, Erdmann – Ziemiecki, Hoppe – Chmielewski: names translated, not Polonised. Entries 5 and 52 are themselves pairs (Riewe – Riwa, Madaliński – Mude), and under the last entry stands one more pair, Bohrer – Swiderski. Under it a block headed "Themen:" lists topics – among them "Polacy-ewangelicy" – and a sentence fragment on what a mouth has swallowed: "(Verschlungen hat ihr der polnische Mund")", the Polish mouth has swallowed, the doubtful word read against Breyer's own hand on the same sheets <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/5/' | relative_url }}">5</a>]</span>.

The three prose sheets are essays in Breyer's hand. The first <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/6/' | relative_url }}">6</a>]</span> is about the German middle schools of the region of Łódź – at Lodz, Pabianice, Zgierz and Sompolno – which have agreed to work the same course through in twelve school years, and about the directors' conference that settled it and the subject conferences of the teachers that are wished to follow it. The other two <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/app539680545/7/' | relative_url }}">7</a>]</span> are on the German surnames of the towns: whoever walks through Warsaw, Lublin or a market town comes upon German names on the shop signs whose bearers may not be addressed as Germans. The legal change of name advertised in the state newspaper "Monitor Polski" is slow and costly; the simpler way found by commune heads and evangelical pastors – Pastor Winkler in Wieluń, Pastor v. Boerner in Zduńska-Wola – is the complete transfer of the name into Polish, so that a Jahn becomes Koguziński and Blum becomes Kwiatkowski. A closer examination, the essay continues, finds three kinds of altered name, and a list of some fifty Polonised surnames follows – many of them the same names the lists of this holding collect. The essay closes: thus German family names are mutilated, fitted to the language-feeling of the majority people – and breaks off. The tenth sheet, which continues it, is shown with the holding but has no transcription.

Breyer's own spellings and slips are kept as written (Schultypußes, kulturtellen), and his Polish spellings vary from list to list (Bartmański and Barmański, Szulborski and Schulborski). No sheet is dated; the years they name – 1741, 1843, 1855 – are the dates of the material, not of the sheets. From the schools essay and from Breyer's work at Sompolno, which lies in the same country, the sheets are of the 1930s. <!-- context -->

### Transcription

The five list sheets were read by Claude from enlarged bands of the images. The editor settled the doubtful readings, and their readings replaced most of them. The three prose sheets are the editor's own transcription, kept as typed.

### Translation and summaries

Claude translated the documents into English. In the lists every name stays as written and only the headings and the common words are translated. Claude wrote the summaries, and a separate pass checked every statement against the document; a statement the document does not support was cut or weakened.
