After the uprising of 1794 failed, Antoni Prusimski, the last Polish owner of Trąbczyn, went abroad, and Prussia confiscated his estates. In December 1796 the Prussian resident at Venice reported that he had certified that Prusimski, ill and broken by his wife's death, could not travel home. Berlin passed the news to the ministers who governed Prussia's Polish provinces. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

Antoni Prusimski, Starost of Niszczewice, owned the Trąbczyn and Kamionna estates in the part of Poland that Prussia took in 1793. The Prussian authorities named him among the leaders of the Polish rising of 1794, and he left the country. His estates were declared forfeit, and in August 1796 King Friedrich Wilhelm II granted them to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen. Prussia's share of Poland was then administered in two parts, each under its own minister in Berlin: South Prussia under Count Hoym and New East Prussia under Baron Schroetter. Prussia kept a resident, a diplomatic agent, at Venice. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**The resident's report, 14 December 1796.** Antoine Prusimski, a Polish gentleman, had come to Count Cattaneo, the Prussian resident at Venice, in ruined health and dulled almost to stupor by the death of his wife, and had asked for a certificate that he could not travel at present and in that season. Cattaneo did not think he could refuse it: a man exhausted by going from one house to another could not go from one country to another <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr1414/2/' | relative_url }}">2</a>]</span>.

**Berlin, January 1797.** On the instruction of its councillor Raumer, the department of foreign affairs sent copies of the report to the two ministers for the Polish provinces, Hoym and Schroetter, and to the Grand Chancellor, von Goldbeck, the head of the Prussian judiciary. The letter was signed by Haugwitz and by a second minister whose initial is A, probably Alvensleben <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr1414/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>.

**Schrötter's reply, 29 January 1797.** Schrötter passed the news on to Hoym, since it was not established whether Prusimski belonged to New East Prussia or to South Prussia <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/ihagrrep7cnr1414/3/' | relative_url }}">3</a>]</span>.

Why Prusimski wanted the certificate comes out in a later document. The statement of his daughter's claim that the Russian envoy handed to the Prussian government in 1818 says that the Prussian authorities had summoned Prusimski in October 1795 and February 1796 to justify his leaving the country. The summonses reached him at Venice only months later; he sent a certificate, legalised by the Prussian legation councillor at Venice, Count "Cathane", and asked for more time to answer, and he died before he obtained it (<a href="{{ '/sources/iiihamdaiiinr12366/' | relative_url }}">III. HA MdA, III. Nr. 12366</a>, document 2). That statement calls it a certificate of his wife's illness and death; the resident speaks of Prusimski's own condition. <!-- context -->
