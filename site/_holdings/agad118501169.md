In 1834 the Council of State of the Kingdom of Poland had to decide who could rule on a mortgage of 30,000 écus that the Wrocław banker Karol Weigel claimed on the Trąbczyn estate, one of the debts Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had left on the land. The courts had struck the mortgage out; the government's finance commission wanted the courts' judgment set aside as beyond their powers. The Council's reporting committee advised that the courts had been entitled to judge, and that the finance commission's demand should be rejected. <!-- context -->

### Historical background

The Kingdom of Poland, set up by the Congress of Vienna in 1815 out of the Duchy of Warsaw, had a Council of State which, among other things, decided conflicts of jurisdiction: cases where a court and a government department each claimed a matter, or each denied the other the right to decide it. Its Commission of Instruction prepared the reasoned report on which the Council decided. <!-- context -->

Prussia confiscated Trąbczyn in 1796 and gave it to Prince Friedrich Ludwig of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen, who in 1800 borrowed 30,000 écus against it from a Sieur Grotowski. In 1807 the Governing Commission, the provisional Polish government Napoleon set up, gave the estate back to Michalina Prusimska, the daughter of its last Polish owner, married first Dąbska and later Miączyńska; the Court of Appeal of the Department of Poznań registered her as owner, reserving the rights of third parties. Weigel bought Grotowski's claim, had it registered in his own name in 1808, and in 1810 sued her before the Tribunal of Kalisz for the interest owed. The Tribunal struck the claim from the mortgage register instead, holding that the return of the estate had extinguished the debts charged on it while it was confiscated. The Court of Appeal set that judgment aside in 1820 because the Prince, who had borrowed the money, had not been summoned; a sovereign decision of 4 September 1821 laid down that the courts were to be left to decide; and in July 1826 the Supreme Tribunal confirmed the Kalisz judgment. In 1829 the Council of Administration, the Kingdom's government, allowed the Commission of Finances and the Treasury to claim that the courts had had no right to decide the matter, and that commission laid its case before the Council of State in July 1830. <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span> <!-- context -->

Weigel was one of the great creditors of the Hohenlohe estates; his claim to compensation in 1830, and Prussia's protests on his behalf, run through the letters of the Prince's agent in the 1820s. <!-- context -->

### Contents

**Two roads.** The Kalisz tribunal had held that the decree of 1807, a special grace of the sovereign, returned the estate free of what had been charged on it, and the Supreme Tribunal had agreed <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. Meanwhile Michalina had petitioned the King of Saxony, then Duke of Warsaw, in 1811; in 1818 the commission settling the Duchy's debts proposed to Prince Czapski, minister of the Kingdom, that her claims be put to the King of Prussia first; and in 1819 Prussia answered by interceding for Weigel and asking that the question be taken from the courts <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. <!-- context -->

**The finance commission's case.** It argued that the courts had read the decree of 1807 wrongly ("ironically", in its word) and so infringed the "Treaty of the State", and had overstepped their powers by founding a judgment in a private suit on an act of state <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. <!-- context -->

**The answer.** The Commission of Instruction replies that the finance commission shows at most that the courts judged wrongly, never that they had no right to judge, and only the second could found a conflict of jurisdiction. A claim on a mortgage belongs to the civil courts by its nature, and the sovereign's reading of his own decrees does not take away the courts' right to apply them. None of the conditions the law sets for a conflict of jurisdiction is met: what the finance commission wants is a further appeal against a final judgment, and the Council of State is not a court of appeal. It advises that the demand be rejected as resting on no principle of law, and submits a decree to that effect <span class="cite">[<a href="{{ '/documents/agad118501169/1/' | relative_url }}">1</a>]</span>. <!-- context -->
