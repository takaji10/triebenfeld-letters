C. The individual branches of the Olęder economy

The full sweep of the economic activity of the Olęders will show itself most completely against the background of their share in the social income of a given estate.[^65-1] The division of the Olęders' income according to the different branches of production will lead to a picture of the individual branches of their economy; the division of income between the Olęders and the lord will be connected, on the one hand, with the distinguishing of the income from their own land and of the profits from the lord's land (the servitudes), and, on the other, with a discussion of the manorial dues. The share of the state and of the church in the Olęders' incomes will present itself to us as the public and ecclesiastical dues. Finally, the division of income between the individual settlers will require, among other things, an examination of their endowment with land and of the social structure of the individual occupations. In discussing the economic activity of the Olęders it will be indispensable to distinguish its twofold character: technical and socio-economic.

I. Agriculture. a. The agricultural technique of the Olęders, like the forms of settlement, developed along two paths. In the lowland settlements it consisted, in the first period, in the improvement of the land through drainage, in the second in the keeping, in a state fit for cultivation, of land once drained. In the settlements of the second type it consisted in the clearing of forests and thickets, and subsequently in raising the fertility of the land thus stripped of wood and in defending it against the renewed invasion of weeds and scrub. The considerable number of Olęder settlements justifies the supposition that the Olęders were able to rise to their task. They were enabled to do so by the multi-year exemptions from all dues granted them by the lord (the wolnizny),[^65-2] while the prospect of a free, independent existence upon land bought by themselves doubtless spurred the Olęders on to greater efforts. Not always, however, did the settlers receive as their lot the improvement of the lands allotted to them by the lord. In many instances they took over arable lands already manured before them by the Polish peasant, sometimes even already with buildings and crops. In column 6 of Annex I there is described the economic character of the land in the individual settlements at the moment of the founding of the Olęders. Only certain cases, clearly discussed in the founding charters or in other sources, have been recorded. The terms “village”, “folwark”, “ready-made arable land” signify that the Olęders here took over land already prepared for cultivation, whether after a village or folwark existing there before, or else were settled upon part of the lands of a settlement still continuing in existence. Returning to the first kind of restriction, it must be said that these always had the character of a payment for the pasture servitudes; for whenever the Olęders were obliged to pay dues for the keeping of sheep in general, there belonged to them the right of pasturage. The pasture servitudes and the payments connected with them will be discussed elsewhere. Here we may remark that dues payable upon the total number of sheep were rare; as a rule a certain contingent was entirely exempt from dues, and only upon the surplus were additional rents paid. On the average the limit was the figure of 50[^73-1] sheep. It could of course happen that the relative restrictions, in the form of additional rents for stock-raising, had in fact an absolute character, when they were set so high that, their existence notwithstanding, stock-raising presented no interest whatever. As a rule the Olęders were allowed to keep[^73-2] [—] sheep upon one huba. Exceptionally permission was given for the keeping of 60 or even 100 sheep,[^73-3] while sometimes the norm was lower: 30, 25, 20 sheep, for example.[^73-4] In the Olęders of the Sieraków estate it was at first free to keep sheep without any dues; but in order to prevent abuses on the part of the smallholders, [the free contingent was fixed] in proportion to the area of the farms. Probably the number of sheep possessed by the larger farmers was decisive. Sheep-keeping there attained a great flowering; according to the inventory of 1759 there fell, upon the average, 22 sheep and 9 lambs[^73-5] to the Olęder włóka. In 1776 dues were introduced in the amount of 15 gr from every sheep above 20 head; it is not known, however, how this affected the extent of the keeping.

The pasturing of the Olęder sheep was as a rule in the hands of a common communal herdsman. Sometimes there was kept in addition a separate herdsman for the cows, for the pigs, even for the horses.[8] The income of the Olęders from stock-raising was thus diminished by the remuneration of the herdsmen. The sources, however, give no information as to the kind and amount of this remuneration. Nor is it easy to compute the herdsmen's remaining incomes. 2. Bee-keeping. Bees in hives the Olęders might keep in any number, without any dues.[^74-4] Sometimes, however, they were forbidden to keep bees in tree-hollow hives (barcie).[^74-5] The products of this keeping (honey, wax) they might dispose of at will. Exceptionally, in the Ol. Złotkowo they were bound to sell the wax exclusively at the manor.[6][^74-6] The income from bee-keeping did not, however, constitute a serious item in the Olęders' economy. A case such as that of the sołtys in the Ol. Kliny, who possessed 11 bee-hives, the sources quote as a kind of curiosity.[^74-7] Professional bee-keepers there were none among the Olęders.

3. Hunting and fishing. The right of hunting game upon their own land did not belong to the Olęders. They were permitted only to hunt birds. They might indeed also organize hunts of wild boar, bears and wolves, but the animals killed they were bound, on pain of severe penalties, to deliver to the manor. The lord on his part promised them in return a certain reward, not more closely defined.[^74-8] Only in the Ol. Ujskie was the right of hunting unrestricted.[^74-9] The Olęders' income from hunting, within limits thus curtailed, presumably had no great significance. It is obvious that the relations described above were not favourable to the formation, among the Olęders, of a group of professional hunters.
