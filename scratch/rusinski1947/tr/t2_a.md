Introductory remarks

A. The subject and scope of the study. Literature and sources

The task of the present study is to present one of the forms of settlement in old Poland, falling in the XVI–XVIII centuries, known under the name of Dutch settlement or, in its Polonised rendering, of Olęder settlement. The territorial scope of the study covers the former voivodeship of Poznań within its borders before the first partition, that is, the then counties of Kościan, Poznań and Wałcz, and the Wschowa land.

The question of “Olęder” colonisation has so far received no broader treatment in Polish scholarship. The study of J. T. Baranowski,[^1-1] published in 1914, rests chiefly on material concerning the areas along the Vistula, while the more recent work of Inglot[^1-2] does indeed give an exhaustive survey of the literature, but takes no account of the sources. The more numerous German studies are fragmentary in character; apart from Schmidt's work,[^1-3] already outdated as regards some of its results, mention must be made here of the more recent studies of W. Maas[^1-4] and W. Schulz.[^1-5] These works show considerable discrepancies, and moreover are not always based on sufficiently secure documentary foundations. There thus arose the need to base the study, as far as possible, on source material alone, and that both material already known and material hitherto unexploited. The literature was utilised above all for comparative purposes.

B. The origins of “Olęder” settlement[^3-1]

The beginnings of “Olęder” colonisation in the territory of Wielkopolska are rooted in the earlier Flemish-Dutch settlement, which already in the Middle Ages was developing on the territory of Germany proper, and in the XVI century came to life again with new force in the area of Ducal and Royal Prussia. Traces of colonising activity issuing from the Netherlands appear in Germany already at the turn of the XI and XII centuries. Settlement then embraced the whole of North Germany, in the first place the surroundings of Bremen, southern Holstein (especially Wagria, after its occupation in 1140 by[^3-2] Count Adolf of Holstein), and further the duchy of Mecklenburg and Brandenburg. The colonisers Henry the Lion and Albert the Bear settled Netherlands emigrants there in large measure at the expense of the Slav population.[^3-3] Soon the colonisation spread also to Central Germany, taking in part of Saxony and of Lusatia.
