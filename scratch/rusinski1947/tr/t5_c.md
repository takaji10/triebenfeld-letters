G. Church obligations

Olęder settlements, from the moment of their foundation, entered within the parochial administration of the Catholic church. In connection with this, for the most part[^104-4], on a par with the other parishioners, the Olęders were obliged to pay certain dues to the parish church. Catholics and evangelicals alike were made liable to these dues. The latter had, in return, just like the Catholic population, a right to the religious ministrations of the parish priests[^104-5]. The church dues paid by the Olęders can be divided into fixed dues and dues for the individual religious services. The fixed dues were the tithe and the meszne[^104-6]. Usually either the tithe or the meszne was paid; exceptionally a certain sum was paid on account of both dues at the same time[^104-7]. CHAPTER V

The spiritual culture of the Olęders

A. Psychological type

The Olęders, as has already been said, apart from the first comers from the Netherlands, came from various parts of eastern Germany and western Poland. They were thus, at the moment of the location of the settlements, neither uniform in the racial respect, nor united by ties of character, customs and culture. In the later generations, however, the common conditions of existence largely levelled their cultural and customary distinctiveness, and the self-governing constitution of the communities, above all the institution of the joint liability of all the members of the community in external relations, closely bound together the fortunes of the individual settlers and contributed to the formation among them of a kind of group consciousness. This may be regarded as equivalent to the formation of a distinct psychological type, that of the “Olęder”. Contemporaries did not speak with enthusiasm of the character of the Olęders. A serious observer such as Stenger writes of them as follows: “It is a certain thing that with the ancestors of our present-day Olęders the best part of the German nation did not leave its fatherland; for, although below we tried to find the reasons why the Olęders have become worse, their present corruption could not be explained if one were to assume that they brought with them good customs and character. Diligence and dexterity, as the children of poverty, were probably their only dowry: if only they had at least preserved these qualities intact! The Olęder is not narrow-minded, but by no means intelligent: he is cunning when he fears an attack, and he holds so strictly to old prejudices and habits that, blinded, he does not notice his own obvious advantages; to the most convincing arguments he gives no hearing at all, for the innate fear of everything that is new makes him deaf to it. Further, he is extraordinarily suspicious: the husband does not trust his wife, the father his children, but all unite when it is a matter of distrust towards the lord or the superior. The Olęder is also extraordinarily stubborn, audacious and un-

[p. 112]


obliging: he does not willingly do anything that he need not do; finally, he has no religion at all” 1. Elsewhere the author charges the Olęders with litigiousness. This matter has already been discussed above[^112-2]. As a favourable trait of the Olęders the author emphasises their strong feeling of communal solidarity (social sense), and states also that sobriety generally prevailed in the Olęder settlements. These last two remarks must be recognised as correct. It is true that taverns, or the tapping of the lord's liquors, were to be found in almost all the settlements, and for the labour dues called “tłoki” the settlers received their remuneration in beer, possibly[^112-1] in spirits; this proves nothing at all, however, that the use of liquors was practised among the Olęders habitually. In a certain contradiction with the feeling of communal solidarity stood the sometimes strikingly weak attachment of the Olęders to their land 1 and their inclination to move from one settlement to another[^112-4]. It seems to follow from this[^112-3] that the Olęders, often newcomers from abroad, were accompanied even in later generations by a feeling of a certain strangeness. The lord also cared for good morals in the community, threatening sometimes even with the expulsion from the settlement of individuals without moral principles and character.

B. Schooling

According to the Prussian classification records for the part of the Poznań county taken in the first partition, in every third Olęder settlement there existed an evangelical school[^113-1]. Probably the number of schools in the settlements with an evangelical population was still greater in the remaining part of the former voivodeship, where there was a greater number of populous settlements able to maintain a teacher. On the other hand it must be taken into account that in the Catholic Olęder settlements there were no schools at all[^113-3].[^113-2] In any case, however, the estimated figures given in Table II must be regarded rather as minimal. The Olęder schools were typical denominational institutions (Kantorat-, Lektoratschulen). They were conducted by church singers or other persons who at the same time performed religious ministrations in the community. They stood in for the evangelical clergymen for the Olęders. Hence the inhabitants of a new settlement usually tried to secure for themselves the religious ministrations of a cantor or lector, striving with the lord for permission to found a school and for its endowment. It is obvious that in schools of this kind the first place was held by religious preparation. With regard to some schools the source notices even raise the doubt whether anything other than the teaching of religion 3% was taught there. Similar reflections are suggested also by the circumstance that there were no schools either in the Olęder settlements with a Catholic population, which had easy contact with their clergymen and the possibility of using the parochial schools, or in those evangelical settlements where the pastor was on the spot, or in the nearest neighbourhood (e.g. in the settlements of the Wieleń estate).

The school was usually housed in a separate building, erected with the lord's consent. This building was at the same time the local house of prayer. Concluding remarks

In the foregoing considerations the political position of the Olęders has not been dealt with, because the question of a separate attitude of the state and of private individuals towards peasants of Polish 1 and German nationality in the old Commonwealth did not exist. National affiliation was, moreover, at that time, in the period of the continual mingling of nationalities and of two-way processes of assimilation, a notion that could not be established even in rough approximation. The pursuit, therefore, of a national policy, even if tendencies in that direction had existed, was a perfectly impossible thing. All attempts, therefore, at writing about the discrimination of German settlers in relation to Poles must be regarded as an undertaking doomed in advance. On the contrary, the gap[^121-1] between the favourable economic situation of the free Olęders and the incomparably worse position of the subject kmiecie continually deepened. The sources contain likewise no mention whatsoever of any steps on the part of the landowners towards the polonisation of the German Olęders subject to them. Probably processes of polonisation of the settlers did exist, but they took place spontaneously, without any purposeful action on the Polish side.

Since they were not szlachta, the Olęders took no part in the political life of their time. Probably to the constitution of the Olęder settlements, and to the resulting absorption solely in the affairs of the community, together with their estrangement from the neighbouring settlements, must be ascribed the fact that the stratum of Olęders, in contrast to the burgher stratum, produced no personality of any outstanding eminence for those times.

In order to grasp what the Olęder colonisation meant for the old Wielkopolska, we must consider it separately from the economic and separately from the political point of view. As an economic phenomenon the settlement must unquestionably be judged
