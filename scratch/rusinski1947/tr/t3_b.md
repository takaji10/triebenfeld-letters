D. The origin of the settlers and the causes of the colonization

The origin of the settlers stands in connexion with their nationality. 3,[^34-3] It is understandable that the fatherland of the first settlers, the Holendrzy, was the Netherlands. As for the Polish Olęders, the Catholic part was recruited from the local population, whereas the Evangelicals, who appeared most numerously in the settlements of the southern part of the Poznań voivodeship, in all probability came from neighbouring Silesia. Such names as the Polsko-Czeskie and Niemiecko-Czeskie Olęders likewise testify that a certain number of settlers arrived from the south (in this case from Bohemian Silesia or Moravia). The coming of the German Olęders likewise remained connected with religion. The Catholics came in all probability from the interior of the Empire, from Bavaria or Swabia. Conditions of life, in the understanding of the time, meant above all the economic and religious situation. CHAPTER III

The legal relations of the Olęder settlements

A. The Olęders and state law

There were no legal norms concerning the Olęders specifically, issued by the public authorities, in Poland before the partitions. Only the tax universały of the times of Stefan Batory and Sigismund III, as it were, take note of the existence of Olęder settlement, assessing taxes also on "pasture włókas, which the Olęders and others lease for pasture".1 This concerned in the first place the Holendrzy in Royal Prussia, who, sitting on lowland lands, carried on livestock farming on a broad scale. Likewise in the scope of other public-law duties the same obligations weighed on the Olęders as on the remaining peasant stratum. There remains to be decided in turn the question to what extent[^37-1] the Olęders had capacity for legal acts, that is, whether they had the right of appearing before the public court against the lord and third persons, and the right of appeal from the lords' judgments. This matter takes a twofold shape, depending on whether we are dealing with settlements founded in the royal estates or in noble or clerical estates. In the royal estates the Olęders, like other peasants, always had the right of seeking justice from the king. Their affairs, whenever damage to the royal estate came into consideration, were attended to by the crown instigator, and complaints against the possessors of the royal estates, or appeals from their judgments, were heard by the referendary court. Charters issued to the Olęders by the starostas were confirmed by the king, thereby guaranteeing their force and effect. Baranowski, who studied the records of the referendary courts, maintains that the Olęders availed themselves of the rights belonging to them quite often and to full effect. The same is proved also by the decrees of the Crown Referendary concerning the okupniks (tenants who had redeemed their land) (among others also Olęders) of the starostwo

B. Manor law in the Olęder settlements. 1. The legal relation to the lord. a. The charter.

While the public authorities issued no legal norms concerning Olęder settlement at all, the system of norms concerning this settlement issued by the lord was broadly developed.[^40-1] They regulated a range of matters falling either within the domain of public law (the lord's judicature, communal and church self-government) or within the domain of private law (personal status, the law of things, succession). They concerned, therefore, the relation of the Olęders to the lord, the mutual relations of the Olęders within the settlement, and finally the relations of the Olęders to institutions and third persons (among others the church). The basis of the legal relation to the lord was the common charter (contract, deed — Latin privilegium, ius, German Gerechtigkeit), issued to the Olęder community by the lord, most often after the surveying of its lands, more rarely at a later period.[^40-2] Exceptionally the proper charter was replaced by its surrogates, for example a provisional preliminary agreement,[^40-3] or else the parties accepted as binding the charter of another community. Although the charter formally was not a contract but a unilateral act, issued and signed[^40-4] by the lord, in fact its content was settled by way of agreement between the lord and the settlers.[^40-5] As a rule the charter was written on parchment, in a more or less ornate form, with the lord's seal pressed in wax directly on the document, or hanging on a ribbon or cord threaded through a cut in the lower part of the parchment. More rarely the charter had the humbler form of a sheet of paper with the lord's signature and seal. The language of the charters was mostly Polish; of the 142 known charters 108 were issued in Polish, 32 in German,[^40-6] and 2 in Polish and German.
