The Olęders' total income from fishing is made up of two items most closely linked with one another: the incomes from their own fish-farming and the incomes from the fishing servitudes. The separation of these two items is here extremely difficult to carry out. For it is not known whether, and in what cases, the possession of Olęder land carried with it the right of fishing in the bordering waters (rivers, lakes, marshes, ponds). The same doubts are raised by the ponds, which often stood upon Olęder land, and which either did or did not enter into the area of the individual farms. While it seems a certain thing that the adjoining waters, which separated the lord's lands from the Olęders', and likewise ponds lying indeed upon Olęder land but not entering into the composition of those lands, remained the property of the lord, and the right of using them must be regarded as a fishing servitude, it is so much the harder to decide whether, in cases where, for example, the Olęders received lands upon a river or lake bordering upon another estate, they entered into the existing manorial rights to those waters, or whether these belonged to them likewise only in the character of servitudes. The sources rather support the second possibility, for they distinguish fairly clearly the waters lying upon Olęder land and entering into the area of those lands (ponds[^75-1] dug by the Olęders, the seasonal floods upon the riparian lands),[1] and the remaining waters. On this assumption the lion's share[^75-2] of the Olęders' fishing income will fall to the fishing servitudes. The rule was that the lord permitted fishing in all the bordering waters, the manorial ponds upon Olęder land excepted. The Olęders were not, however, permitted to set up wears upon flowing waters.[^75-3] Sometimes the lord forbade the taking of fish in the spawning season.[^75-4] In exceptional cases, when the lord reserved to himself the exclusive right of fishing, he gave the community priority in letting it out on lease.[5][^75-5] The sources make no mention of professional fishermen among the Olęders.

4. Industry. a. Tavern-keeping. Taverns stood in almost all the Olęder settlements. They were, however, always, as it were, branch establishments of the manorial breweries (mielcuchy), for the propination monopoly was in force in the Olęder settlements and the innkeepers received ready-made manorial liquors, which they were bound to retail in the settlement at a price fixed in advance.

Exceptionally in Ostrówek the settlers, to whom Kerol Maciej Grudziński in the founding document of 1750 made over the brewery with its requisite equipment and a granary for the storing of malt, themselves engaged in the brewing of beer. Production was here organized in the following manner. The individual settlers were bound to deliver to the brewery, from every huba,[^76-1] [—] wierteles of barley and the quantity of hops needed for the brewing of [—] barrels of beer.[^76-2] They were obliged to haul the wood in turn in the amount of 2–3 sążni; for this they received the aforementioned 10 barrels of beer. The rest was presumably retailed by the innkeeper, whom the lord freed from rent, while his remaining dues the sołtys, the ławniks and the community were bound to take upon themselves. The incomes from the tapping of liquors (the production of vodka was similarly organized) the sołtys and the ławniks were bound to divide evenly among all the members of the community. We have here, then, to do with a typical co-operative organization.[^76-3] The organization of the sale of the manorial liquors presented itself in the Olęder settlements as follows. At the founding of the settlement the lord set aside ½ of a włóka (huba), free of rent, for the innkeeper. One of the members of the community paid the okupne, settled on the land,[^76-5] and even before the tavern was built began the tapping of the manorial liquors[^76-4]. His remuneration, besides the aforementioned ½ of a włóka free of all rents,[3] often embraced also the so-called świętojanka, that is the 21st barrel or, respectively, the 21st garniec of the beer or vodka tapped.[4] In rare cases other advantages were granted to the innkeeper. F. Manorial dues

I. Rents. All the Olęders were rent-payers. Labour dues had only a slight significance. Among the rents the principal part was played by the money rent. Independently of the division of rents into money and into kind, there draws itself quite clearly a division of the Olęder rents into dues from the individual branches of the economy (from the land, from the individual servitudes and advantages). As at the colonization under German law, so the Olęders did not pay rent immediately upon settlement, but enjoyed a certain number of so-called free years. The wolnizny had for their aim to make it possible for the Olęders to maintain themselves on the spot so long as the land had not yet been cleared and had not begun to yield incomes. As a rule there was required of the Olęders that in the free years they should erect the needed farm buildings.[^84-4] In settlements founded “from the new root” the wolnizna amounted most often to 6–7 years,[^84-5] whereas at the founding of settlements upon the site of deserted villages there were either no years of freedom at all, or they ranged from 1 to 3 years, according to the state of the lands and of any buildings still existing. In settlements founded upon lands partly overgrown there existed a differentiation of the years of freedom according to the state of the individual pieces of land. Independently of the exemptions in the initial period of the settlement's existence, the lord as a rule promised the Olęders an additional year of freedom in the event that, having abandoned their farms in consequence of the upheavals of war or of other elemental calamities, they should later wish to return to their seats.[^84-6]

2. Payments. Besides the money rents the Olęders paid various dues. They differed from the rents in this, that they were paid only in certain circumstances (e.g. upon the sale of a farm), whereas the rents were paid always, in a fixed and defined amount. On the border between rents and dues stood the already discussed payment for the pasture servitudes, paid for each individual sheep. The proper dues paid by the Olęders may be divided into 2 groups: dues of an economic character and administrative dues. To the first category of dues belonged the okupne (for land, for buildings), then the eventual market dues. The amount of the okupne fluctuated within very wide limits and it is difficult to give here any average figure. In 2 settlements founded at the close of the seventeenth century the okupne came, for the huba, to 40 thalers, reckoning the thaler at 15 szóstaks (== 120 zł),[^88-1] and 100 thalers at the rate of 5 zł to the thaler (== 500 zł).[^88-2] At the beginning of the eighteenth century there was paid for the huba: 150 zł (in 1701),[^88-3] 30 zł (in 1707),[^88-4] 10 zł (in 1709),[^88-5] 100 tymfs (in 1712).[^88-6] In connexion with the fall in the value of money the sum of the okupne rose nominally, reaching about 1730 exceptionally even 600 zł.[^88-7] On the other hand, in the same period we have to record okupne in the amount of 6 zł,[^88-8] and somewhat later (in 1752) 1 red złoty from the huba.[^88-9] For a longer time the amount of the okupne settles down at approximately 100 zł from the huba. In the last quarter of the eighteenth century the okupne is higher. For the huba there is paid: 900 zł (in 1771 — arable lands with buildings),[^88-10] 24 zł (in 1773),[^88-11] 30 red zł. (in 1775),[^88-12] 900 zł (arable lands with buildings — in 1776 and 1781),[^88-13] 10 red zł. (in 1783),[^88-14] 1,800 zł and 1,500 zł (arable lands with buildings — in 1785),[^88-15] 150 zł (in 1786),[^88-16] 20 red zł. (in 1787).[^88-17]
