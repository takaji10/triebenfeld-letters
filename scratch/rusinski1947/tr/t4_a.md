CHAPTER IV

The economic conditions of the Olęder settlements
A. Topography of the settlements. Forms of settlement. Building

I. Topography of the settlements. Olęder colonization was in its entirety agricultural colonization. The configuration of the ground on which the settlements were founded, and the character and productivity of the soil, exercised a direct influence on their economic life, and at the same time exercised a formative influence upon certain manifestations of the material culture of the Olęders, such as the manner of settling (the forms of settlement), building and the standard of agriculture. In view of the foregoing factors, all the Olęder settlements can be divided into two categories: lowland settlements, founded in river valleys and on the banks of standing waters, and settlements situated on dry ground, brought into being by clearing. Intermediate forms, of course, also occurred. The morphology of the lands occupied by the settlements presents, on the whole, a uniform appearance. Almost all the settlements seated themselves upon the outwash plains and dunes of the frontal moraine, frayed by the waters of the ice age. Only around Nowy Tomyśl did the reach[^55-1][^55-2] of settlement embrace also a part of the flat ground moraine. The soil of settlements of either type was of no great economic value. In the lowland settlements it was, on the side of the water basin, as a rule marshy, more rarely alluvial (the so-called mady), while on the slopes of the valleys it passed into a system of sandy or stony dunes and elevations of the glacial moraine. The soil of the settlements of the second category, sandy and barren over almost its whole extent, only exceptionally rose to medium quality. This fact finds its explanation in the historical development of settlement within the area here discussed. The fertile lands had long since been settled by the local population, which, growing in numbers, occupied later, in part, also the less fertile lands. The colonists of the Middle Ages likewise chose lands as fertile as possible. The stock of land left at the Olęders' disposal therefore constituted the least valuable remnant remaining after the selection of land carried out by the settlement of many centuries.

2. Forms of settlement. In correspondence with the topographical conditions there developed two basic forms of Olęder settlement: the type of lowland settlement presenting itself as a street village, and the type of settlement[^56-2]. In both kinds of settlement the arrangement and disposition of the farm buildings is bound up closely with the character and shape of the piece, or pieces, of land belonging to the individual farms. The lands of the farms comprised in a lowland settlement consisted of a series of elongated strips running at right angles to the edge of the waters or of the morass. Such a strip of land began at the lowest point, by the body of water, and, running up the slope, ended upon dry ground lying higher. Here, at the top, parallel to the edge of the waters or of the bog, ran the road linking together the whole series of strips of land lying next to one another and belonging to the individual farms. Along this road, at the highest-lying end of the land, the Olęders raised their farm buildings. As a rule these stood on the same side of the road on which the land lay, but they could also stand on the opposite side, in which case the road separated them from the lands of the farm. Typical settlements of this kind are the oldest Olęders of the Wieleń estate. In the later period street villages occur also upon dry ground, and the lands are there laid out less regularly.

Beyond the two described categories of settlement there occurred Olęder settlements of other types. These arose in a twofold manner. In the first case, when an existing village was transferred to Olęder law, or the Olęders bought up a village left empty together with its buildings, or else, taking over a deserted village already without buildings, restored its original shape, there arose an Olęder settlement in the form existing previously. In the sources we find scanty information on the manner of erecting the farm buildings in the Olęder settlements, on their shape and dimensions, and on the material used in their construction. In principle the complex of buildings belonging to one farm consisted of a dwelling cottage, a barn and a byre. Cart-sheds are met with more rarely. The byre was often built on to the cottage; exceptionally a second byre, or a separate granary, entered into the complex of buildings. The cottage was built either of logs or of sawn boards, or as a clay-walled cottage, and so likewise the byre and the barn. There also occurred buildings simply put together out of brushwood.[^57-2] Part of a building might be of timber, while the remainder was plastered with clay. For the most part the buildings had a covering of straw (sheaves), more rarely of reed, and exceptionally the more considerable buildings were roofed with shingles.[^57-3]

B. Occupational and social composition

The occupational composition of the Olęders. Among the population of the Olęder settlements we must distinguish: 1. the Olęder farmers, who sat on lands received from the lord or bought up, and who stood with the lord in a direct economic and legal relation regulated in the founding charter; 2. the remaining population, economically dependent upon the Olęders[^58-5], such as, for example, certain craftsmen, the journeymen etc.; 3. it is also possible that in one and the same settlement there dwelt persons who did not belong to the communal society of the Olęders. Such was the case in the instances of mixed settlements already discussed. It could also happen that within an Olęder settlement there lived someone belonging to the manorial household service.[^58-6]

Of course such inhabitants did not belong to the Olęder community, and consequently do not form the object of our considerations.

The population of the Olęder settlements was differentiated by occupation only to a small degree. Nearly all the members of the community were employed in agriculture, whether as their principal occupation or at least incidentally. Exceptionally this did not apply to the communal herdsmen, whose basis of subsistence was the rearing of domestic animals; it must nevertheless be emphasized here that stock-raising in the settlements did not pass beyond the bounds of a subsidiary occupation alongside the proper agricultural economy; among the Olęders we nowhere meet with farms devoted specially to stock-raising. Likewise only exceptionally do we encounter craftsmen who at the same time were not engaged in agriculture.

The most important source for the occupational composition of the population of the Olęder settlements of Kościan county is the Tol. It was drawn up according to the scheme laid down by the Crown Treasury Commission, which provided for the division of the rural population into the following groups: 1. farmer peasants (gospodarze rolnicy), 2. cottagers, 3. lodgers, 4. manufacturers, 5. craftsmen,[^59-1] 6. tapsters and innkeepers, 7. journeymen and servants, 8. the unattached “busied with work”, 9. beggars. In practice there was usually indicated as well the economic and legal relation of the inhabitants to the lord, there being added as need arose: “okupnicy” (holders by purchase), “czynszownicy” (rent-payers), “kontraktowi” (contract-holders). In the Olęder villages there was usually given: “Olęder farmers”, “okupni”, “okupnicy”, “czynszowi (czynszownicy)”. This whole scheme leaves much to be desired, for it mixes occupational with social categories, and is thus incomplete. Further, the figures contained in the table inspire far less confidence than, for example, the figures of an inventory or of a lustration, for they are nowhere explained in detail. The categories of persons ad 1, 2, 3, 7 and 8 in the Olęder settlements were without doubt occupied with agriculture. At point 4 we have a term that is rather obscure; the occupational allegiance of the persons classed here must be decided in each individual case. Point 9, as one may easily perceive on glancing into the table, the lustrators interpreted very broadly. Besides undoubted beggars there were assigned to this group all the old persons incapable of work who remained at the charge of the community (widows, orphans), and presumably also other persons who could not be brought under any of the preceding categories. These persons we too have counted among the agricultural population, as occupationally passive. Besides, this column supplements the column “the unattached at work”; the lustrators, as may be gathered from the table, did not in the majority of cases themselves very well know which of the two columns to give precedence.[1] On approximately the same principles the hearth tables were at the same time drawn up.
