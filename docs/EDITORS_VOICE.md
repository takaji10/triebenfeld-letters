# The editor's voice

The editor, 2026-10-20, gave three samples of their own writing so that
their voice is documented "for a later examination of the text on the
site". Nothing on the site was changed for it. This file is the analysis
and, at the end, a two-paragraph brief to measure the site's prose against.

The samples are not kept in the repository, which is public:

1. **The preface** to the editor's book on the Trąbczyn estate and its
   Olęders: how their research began, from a family archive to the court
   books (809 words).
2. **The introduction** to the same book: the estate's history from 1746
   to 1945 in outline, and what each part of the book covers (1,871
   words).
3. **A statement of purpose** from the editor's university studies in
   architecture (333 words).

The figures below were counted by script; the rest is a reading.

## Sentence length

| Sample | Words | Sentences | Mean | Median | Shortest | Longest | 35 words or more |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preface | 809 | 28 | 28.9 | 27 | 9 | 60 | 9 |
| Introduction | 1,871 | 64 | 29.2 | 28 | 8 | 55 | 16 |
| Statement | 333 | 12 | 27.8 | 29 | 15 | 35 | 2 |

- **Long, even sentences.** The mean is about 29 words in all three
  samples, and about one sentence in four runs to 35 words or more. They
  are built by adding, not by nesting: a main clause, then a "which" or
  "who" clause, a participle ("leading to its reorganisation and eventual
  liquidation"), or ", and" with a second main clause. The reader is
  carried forward, usually in time order.
- **Short sentences are rare and placed.** Five of 104 have fewer than 13
  words. Each closes a paragraph or turns it after a long sentence: "The
  Zelmers had been directly caught in the centre of this." "Change came
  again in 1815, when the area fell under Russian rule." "Today the area
  reflects a strong Polish identity."
- **Paragraphs** have three to five sentences. One runs much longer, where
  a single story is told whole (the border dispute, 1771 to 1782).
- **Sentence openings vary**: a time ("In the previous semester", "After
  the establishment of the Zagórów parish"), a participle ("Growing up in
  this environment", "Not being particularly educated about Poland"), a
  clause ("As my research expanded", "While there are few historical
  documents").
  The historical outline starts most sentences on its subject ("The
  Olęders came to Trąbczyn...").

## Vocabulary and preferred words

- **Register**: plain, educated English, mostly everyday words, with a
  few formal ones: "encompassing", "protracted litigation", "afforded",
  "rife with", "the culmination of my endeavours", "enigmas lost to time".
- **Spelling**: Canadian and British: labour, centre, neighbouring,
  industrialise, characterised, colonisation, favoured, endeavours. One
  "traveling".
- **Words of recovery and keeping**: origins (9 times in about 3,000
  words), preserve and preservation (8), remnants (3), uncover (3),
  dormant (2), "untold stories", "long-forgotten stories", "a new
  audience" (2), "simple lives", "turbulent times", "upheaval". Things lie
  dormant, are uncovered, are preserved, and are told again.
- **Recurring phrases**: "I found myself" (4 times in the preface), "the
  many" (the many documents, decades, events, untold stories),
  "particularly" (4), "undoubtedly" (2), "eventually" (2).
- **Foreign terms explained where they first appear**: a pronunciation in
  brackets ("Trąbczyn (pronounced tromb-chin)"), a definition after a dash
  or as an appositive (an Olędry, "a village of free settler-tenants"),
  a modern equivalent ("now the Volyn region of northwestern Ukraine"), a
  later name ("Michalina Prusimska (later Miączyńska)").
- **Figures are exact**: 45 km², 14 communities, 251 households, 235
  descendants, 140,000.

## Openings and closings

- **A piece opens on something concrete, seen from the present**: a person
  at a date (the grandfather who began his research in the early 1970s),
  the place as a visitor sees it today (sandy fields, pine forests,
  overgrown cemeteries, crumbling homesteads), or the writer's own path
  ("As I have navigated through the master's program..."). From there it
  goes back in time, or out to the argument. It never opens on an
  abstraction, a question or a quotation.
- **A piece closes looking forward**: on purpose and hope ("it is my hope
  that this work will inspire future aspiring genealogists"), on keeping
  the past for future generations, on the lesson to do the research while
  parents and grandparents are alive, on the work as a culmination. The
  introduction ends by echoing its opening: the remnants of the first
  paragraph are, in the last, "preserved in the many documents,
  manuscripts, photographs, and metrical records". A quotation from an
  authority (Adam Giesinger) stands just before the close, not at the
  start. The preface ends on a plan of the book.
- **Between them, signposts**, often in the passive: "Close attention is
  given to", "is also presented using material from", "This book also
  examines".

## Evidence and examples

- **The concrete earns the abstract.** An idea is stated in a phrase and
  then made good with named things, often a list of three to five: the
  grandfather's office "functioned very much like a museum", then the
  maps, the charts, "a buffalo molar, wooden animal carvings, model Air
  Canada planes"; the dispute was violent, then "shootings, raids, arson,
  beatings, and theft"; an inn was made unusable, then "its barrels of
  beer, tables, and even doors and windows removed".
- **Every person is named with a role**, every source with its author and
  often its title and dates: "the owner of the Łukom estate, Stanisław
  Chełmski", "church historian Eduard Kneifel", "The Canadian historian
  Adam Giesinger (1909-2004)", Chodyła's book with its full title.
  Attribution is plain: "According to Chodyła's research".
- **History is told as cause and consequence, in order, with dates**, and
  each event says what it meant for the colonists: "which had a direct
  impact on my ancestors' way of life", "The effect on the Olęders living
  on the Trąbczyn estate during this period was dramatic".
- **Primary sources are valued and said to be**: the genealogy "has been
  referenced against primary-source material whenever possible".
- **Generalisation comes last and briefly**, and is about people: why
  origins matter, and that migration goes on.
- **The writer is present in the first person in a preface or a close**,
  candid about their limits ("Not being particularly educated about
  Poland or its language", "a completely new experience for me"), and
  absent from the history itself, which is in the third person.
- **Metaphor is sparse and familiar**: "a little oasis of the unusual",
  "the first spark", "the red thread", "the lens", "a window into".

## What the editor does not do

Counted across the three samples:

- No questions, rhetorical or other, and no exclamations.
- No "you". The reader is "a visitor", or is folded into "we" and "our"
  in a closing reflection.
- No humour, irony or slang; contractions almost never (one "wasn't",
  one "I've").
- No essay connectives: "However", "Moreover", "Furthermore" and
  "Although" do not occur. Time carries the transitions ("After", "Change
  came again in 1815", "Today"), with "Despite this" and "While" for a
  contrast.
- No moral verdicts on the people of the past. Chełmski's wrong is the
  court commission's finding ("determined that Chełmski had unjustly
  claimed land"); the end of the German villages is stated as what
  happened ("many ethnic German colonists in Poland were forcibly
  deported").
- No argument with other historians and no historiography: sources are
  drawn on and named, not disputed.
- No theoretical or archival jargon left unexplained.
- No lists, headings or sentence fragments inside a piece: whole
  paragraphs of prose.
- No sensational adjectives. Violence is listed plainly.

## Habits a reviewer should not count as the voice

These occur in the samples but are slips, not the voice, and a reading
of the site against this brief should not copy them: a phrase repeated
within a few lines ("I found myself", "particularly"); a word doubled in
one sentence ("events of the 18th century, when a long-running border
dispute ... reached a feverish pitch in the late 18th century"; "the
importance of preserving these remnants has never been more important").

## Where the voice and the house style differ

`docs/HOUSE_STYLE.md` governs the site's prose. In most things the two
agree: every person named and placed, terms explained where they first
appear, the concrete before the abstract, events in order, no verdicts,
the documents first. They differ in four places, and until the editor
rules, the house style stands:

- **Dashes.** The samples use the long dash eight times, for an aside or
  a definition. The house style allows none in English prose.
- **The first person and reflection.** The editor's prefaces use them; a
  holding's description on the site is impersonal ("A holding's own
  page").
- **Scene-setting and metaphor** ("A visitor ... will notice", "the red
  thread"). The house style keeps narrative devices and metaphor to a
  minimum.
- **"Undoubtedly"** for an inference about how things were felt. The
  house style marks inference as inference, plainly.

## The brief

The editor writes as a careful family historian: plain, educated English
in Canadian spelling, in long, even sentences (about 29 words on average)
that move forward in time, each adding a clause, a date or a name to the
last, with an occasional short sentence to turn or close a paragraph. A
piece opens on something concrete seen from the present (a person at a
date, the place as a visitor finds it today, the writer's own way into the
subject) and goes back from there; it closes looking forward, on why the
past should be kept and told again. Every person is named with their role
and every source with its author and title; a foreign term is explained
where it first appears, by a pronunciation, a modern name or a short
definition; figures are exact. An abstract idea is stated briefly and then
made good with specifics, often three to five concrete things in a list:
the shootings, raids, arson, beatings and theft of a boundary dispute, or
the barrels of beer, tables, doors and windows taken from an inn. The
words are those of recovery and keeping: origins, remnants, preserved,
uncovered, dormant, untold stories, a new audience.

The warmth is in the subject, not the manner. The people of the estate
and their simple, hard lives are kept in view, and each event is told by
what it meant for them. The writer appears in the first person only in a
preface or a close, candid about what they did not know, and stands
outside the history itself, which is told in the third person, in order,
carried by dates and by "despite this" and "while", never by "however" or
"moreover". The voice does not ask questions, exclaim, address the reader
as "you", joke, use slang or contractions, argue with other historians, or
pass judgement on the people of the past: a wrong is the court's finding,
a deportation is stated as what happened. Hedging is light and honest
("perhaps", "likely"), metaphor is sparse and familiar (a spark, a red
thread, a lens), and the sources, above all the documents, are named
rather than leaned on for authority.
