# Narrative Overview
### The von Triebenfeld / Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen correspondence, 1798–1816

A working chapter skeleton — first-pass synthesis, meant to be built on. Not an exhaustive read of all 317 records: built by sampling across the full chronological span (roughly every 15th–20th dated letter within each period, plus close reading of the pivot points) and cross-checking against the biography and the letter 48/302 collation already done for this project. Treat specific claims below as citation-anchored (letter number + date given for each), but treat the *connective* narrative between them as a draft to verify against a fuller read, not settled fact. Named figures are cross-referenced to `whos_who.md`.

---

## 1. The land swap that fails (1798–99, ~33 letters)

The correspondence opens mid-campaign. Friedrich Ludwig is trying to trade his Südpreußen estates — the Polish lands granted to him after the Third Partition, administered from Zagorów and Trąbczyn — to the Prussian crown for the domains of **Krotoszyn and Polajewo**. His own stated motive, in a letter to the King (L86–88, Dec 1798): to clear his debts without becoming a burden on the state.

Triebenfeld works Berlin relentlessly through this period — **Obrist von Köckritz** and **von Zastrow** as sympathetic channels to Friedrich Wilhelm III, against the obstruction of **Minister von Voss**, whose commissioned report keeps stalling approval (L77–L89, Oct–Dec 1798). The tone swings between triumph — "der Tausch nun so gut als approbirt und abgeschlossen anzusehen" ("the exchange can now be considered as good as approved and concluded," L96, 1 Jan 1799) — and frustration at Voss's foot-dragging (L82, L84, L102).

**Outcome:** the swap does not happen. By 1804 the Prince still holds Zagorów and Trąbczyn, and the correspondence's center of gravity shifts from acquiring land to managing debt against it.

## 2. Debt management becomes the whole job (1800–1806, ~83 letters)

The tone shifts from courtly lobbying to estate triage. Kaemen and Kolno are sold off (L135–136, 1804). A 150,000 Rthl loan is extracted from the **Invaliden-Casse**, credited to Köckritz's intervention (L158, 26 Jan 1805). The estates are progressively colonised and dismembered ("Dismembrirung") to raise revenue against the debt — a detailed formal revenue table for Zagorów and Trąbczyn survives as L179a (25 Apr 1806), listing income tit by tit down to the Rthl/Groschen/Pfennig.

Meanwhile the marriage has broken down — the book's Ch. 7 ("Friedrich Ludwig, der verlassene Ehemann") dates the separation/*Transact* to 1799–1804 — and creditors multiply: **Schlabrendorff**, **von der Lahr**, **Weigel** are all named as claimants against the estates in this period (L105, L188).

## 3. Catastrophe (1806–1809, ~31 letters)

L26 (28 Oct 1806) is the hinge: Triebenfeld writes that the Prince's escape from "the most terrible danger" reached him only through a third party's letter, amid "malicious rumours" already circulating. The timing matches the book's own account (Ch. 9, "Jena und Auerstädt und die Kapitulation von Prenzlau") — the defeat and capitulation that ended Friedrich Ludwig's military career — though the letter itself doesn't name Jena/Auerstädt directly, so treat that identification as strongly supported, not stated in-text.

Tilsit hands Südpreußen to the new Duchy of Warsaw; the estates are suddenly under foreign sovereignty. Triebenfeld is **arrested in Berlin by French gendarmerie**, held under strict arrest for acting as the Prince's agent against the Invaliden-Casse (L42, 16 Jan 1809). The estates are sequestered — **von der Lahr** forces the seizure of Schweiner over a late timber shipment (L60, 4 Aug 1809). By May 1809 Triebenfeld reports he has "not 10 Rthl," no coat on his back, was robbed on the road, and has bailiffs in his house (L57, 7 May 1809).

## 4. Legal war on every front (1809–1812, ~67 letters)

The 7 March 1809 Kontopp letter — corpus letters 48/302, also independently transcribed in the biography's Appendix 6 — is the campaign plan for recovery: **thirteen separate powers of attorney** issued to **Hawich** in one sitting — lift the sequestration, sue Oppenheimer and Wolff over 72,000 Rth in registered claims, dismiss and prosecute the fraudulent sequestor **Honrichs**, collect rent arrears, defend possession at Warsaw.

Running in parallel: **Michalina Prusimska** (later **Dąbska**, per family research — see `whos_who.md`) is litigating as her father Antoni Prusimski's heir to recover Trąbczyn (L1858, letter 28, 5 Oct 1807: "der Frau v Dąbska gebohrnen v Pru[simska]"). By January 1811, Triebenfeld reports **31 lawsuits pending simultaneously** and almost no one paying (L212, 1 Jan 1811). A second track works to offload the Zagorów estates entirely to creditors **Weigel and Stössel** in exchange for extinguishing their claims (L201, 29 Aug 1810; L219, 30 Sep 1811).

## 5. Vienna (1813–1815, ~76 letters — the single densest year is 1815)

Triebenfeld is sent to the **Congress of Vienna** to petition for restitution of the estates. These letters are among the most vivid in the collection: a bankrupt agent in an overpriced capital, itemising his daily expenses — coffee, wine, a wretched room — to justify begging for 300 Rthl (L243, 13 Oct 1814), while tracking **Metternich**, **Talleyrand**, and the Prussian delegation (**Hardenberg**'s circle, via **Stägemann**) through the diplomacy.

The existential question for the estates is the **Prosna river border**: if drawn where Russia demands, "die Trąbczyner und selbst meine Blizanower Güter" fall under Russian sovereignty (L266, 10 Jan 1815). Then Napoleon leaves Elba (L4, L286, Mar 1815) and the whole Congress — and the Prince's case — is thrown into suspension.

## 6. Personal ruin (1815–16, ~54 letters)

The relationship between Prince and agent visibly curdles. The Prince's letters turn cold; Triebenfeld responds with point-by-point defences of his own honour (L273, 15 Feb 1815) while begging for money to feed his children (L8, L23, spring 1815). In December 1815, his daughter dies four days after childbirth, and the recurring figure **Barbe** dies in the same period (L297, 19 Dec 1815) — the correspondence's emotional low point.

## What the register (letter 303) adds

The undated liquidation register totals **1,414,377 Rthl Courant** in debts — a single-document snapshot of the scale of the collapse the letters describe piecemeal across eighteen years. Its single largest private creditor is **"Fr. Charlotte von Triebenfeld," owed 112,000 Rthl** (row 22) — matching, in figure, the 112,000 Rthl advance that recurs as the central financial thread of the correspondence itself (see `currency_normalisation_report.md`).

**This connection is inference, not proof** — the register doesn't say it's the same 112,000, and it hasn't been independently traced. But if it is the same sum, it means Peter Friedrich von Triebenfeld's own household — his wife's money — was bankrolling the Prince, which would reframe the whole relationship: not just an agent managing a patron's debts, but a household financially fused to its master's ruin. Worth chasing down as a specific research question.
