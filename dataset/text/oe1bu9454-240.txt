geſchildert daß dies in ſeinen Eyfer ſchon ſo ermüdet —
das traurigste und für uns das Gefahr volleste ist, daß die Ruße Truppen ſehr murren
und selbst die Generäle den Waffenstillstand verwünschen; mann mögte lieber bis an
der Weichsel zurükkehren. Unſere Lage iſt jezt ſehr Kritisch.
der Herr Gen Leut v. Köck[ritz]: hat ſich mit dem Minſter Hard[enberg]: ſehr überworfen
der Min[ister]: fragt dH v K[öckritz] in Gegenwart des Monarchen. — Von freuen Sie sich nicht das
alles so gut geht?
K[öckritz]: Worüber?
Mitt nun es steht alles sehr gut, der stolze Nap[oleon]: hatt den Waffen stillstand erbettet,
müßen —
K[öckritz]: Herr! ich müste aus den Tollhause entlaufen ſind, wenn ich mir darüber
freute — Es ist umsonst so viel unschuldiges Blut vergoßen — wir laufen

Nun ist im Lande nicht viel mehr Getreide — die Wege sind Grundlos und liegen schon

--- SUMMARY (German) ---
Triebenfeld berichtet, Österreich wolle neutral bleiben und nur vermitteln. Russische Truppen und Generäle murrten über den Waffenstillstand. Köckritz habe sich mit Hardenberg überworfen und vor dem Monarchen dessen Zuversicht verspottet. Die Verpflegung sei ungewiss, Getreide knapp, die Wege grundlos. Zastrow soll die Landwehr in Nysa (55000 Mann) kommandieren, Ortel liege krank. Triebenfeld will über Nysa nach Ząbkowice Śląskie, in die Hauptquartiere und nach Świniary reisen, um Produkte heranzubringen und Geld zu beschaffen.

--- SUMMARY (English) ---
Triebenfeld reports that Austria wants to stay neutral and only mediate. Russian troops and generals, he says, were grumbling about the armistice. Köckritz, he says, has fallen out with Hardenberg and mocked his confidence in front of the monarch. Supplies, he says, are uncertain, grain scarce and the roads bottomless. Zastrow is to command the militia at Nysa (55,000 men), while Ortel lies ill. Triebenfeld intends to travel by way of Nysa to Ząbkowice Śląskie, to the headquarters and to Świniary, to bring up produce and raise money.

Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

It is quite certain, and proved to the highest degree of evidence, that Austria will not join in the fighting; it means to remain neutral and to act as mediator, which is to say, to fish in troubled waters and otherwise do nothing — to the people the advantages of this are painted so brilliantly that they are already so wearied in their zeal — the saddest thing, and for us the most perilous, is that the Russian troops murmur greatly and even the generals curse the armistice; one would rather withdraw as far as the Vistula. Our situation is now very critical. Lieutenant General von Köck[ritz] has fallen out badly with Minister Hard[enberg]; the Minister asks Mr von Köckritz in the presence of the monarch: — Of, are you not glad that everything is going so well?

K[öckritz]: About what?

Mitt: well, everything stands very well, the proud Nap[oleon] has begged for the armistice, must — K[öckritz]: Sir! I should have to have run out of the madhouse, if I rejoiced at that — so much innocent blood has been shed for nothing — we run as far as the Oder, give up Saxony and our lands piecemeal, Brönen then the matter, conclude an armistice for 6 weeks, withdraw our troops, which had the most advantageous position, give N. time to procure himself support, and now let ourselves be driven back beyond the Vistula, and our lands totally ruined. No, the man must have escaped from the madhouse who rejoices at all these events — H[ardenberg]: Now do it better — K[öckritz]: I cannot out-shout the clever gentlemen. H[ardenberg]: Ah, Your Excellency sees everything black — everything nevertheless stands very well, and we shall certainly obtain a very advantageous peace —

I have indeed heard the first said here again. Would to God the Prince of Hohenlohe stood at the head — then things would surely go better — How matters will stand henceforth with the provisioning is a problem — the Russians will have nothing to do with it, and demand impetuously to be provisioned. Now there is not much grain left in the country — the roads are bottomless and already lie covered with dead horses, the green fodder brings the plague among them, God alone knows how this is to end — General Zastrow is again very well liked; he will command the Landwehr which is now being gathered at Nysa, and which consists of 55,000 men — Lieutenant General Ortell lies here in Neustadt very ill of a nasty fever and now meddles in nothing; I therefore go tomorrow from here by way of Nysa to Ząbkowice Śląskie, from there to the headquarters, then to Świniary to see whether I can bring in the produce and procure myself money — from that side I shall write only later again. Reverently I remain as

Your Most Serene Highness's

Neustadt

the 20th of June

1813.

most humble and obedient servant

v Triebenfeld
