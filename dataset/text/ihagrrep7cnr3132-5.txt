--- SUMMARY (German) ---
Eine Abschrift des gemeinschaftlichen Berichts Goldbecks und Haugwitz' an König Friedrich Wilhelm III., Berlin, 30. April 1803, über die Vorstellung des ehemaligen Starosten von Prusimski und des Xaver von Jezierski, die wegen ihrer Teilnahme an der südpreußischen Insurrektion außer Landes gegangen waren und von Dresden aus um die Erlaubnis zur Rückkehr gebeten hatten. Die Akten der Regierung zu Posen zeigen: Anton von Prusimski wurde beschuldigt, sich am 2. August 1794, vor dem Ausbruch der Insurrektion, von seinen Gütern bei Kalisz auf seine Smiłowicer Güter im Distrikt Kujawien begeben, dort tägliche Besuche des benachbarten Adels empfangen zu haben und dass bei ihm am 21. August die sogenannte Kujawische Insurrektion zum Abschluss gediehen, die Konföderationsakte entworfen und vollzogen worden sei; er soll auch zu den südpreußischen Adligen gehört haben, die im Winter 1793/94 wegen der polnischen Staatsveränderungen in Dresden zusammentraten. Diese Beschuldigungen gründen sich größtenteils auf ein Mémoire raisonné aus den Angaben ungenannter vertrauter Polen und Offizianten und wurden nicht genau untersucht. Die Regierung hielt sie nicht für hinreichend für den Kriminalprozess gegen abwesende Verbrecher, wie er gegen einige Hauptinsurgenten geführt wurde, sondern leitete auf Antrag des Fiskals nur den Konfiskationsprozess ein, weil er auf die allgemeinen Verordnungen nicht zurückgekehrt war. Öffentlich vorgeladen, machte er von Italien aus, wo sich seine dort auch verstorbene Ehefrau angeblich ihrer Gesundheit wegen aufhalten musste, Hoffnung auf seine Rückkehr und suchte die Konfiskation abzuwenden; da er nicht erschien, wurde das Konfiskationsurteil abgefasst, und nach seiner Rechtskraft wurden seine Trąbczyner Güter dem Fürsten von Hohenlohe, seine Pawłower Güter dem jetzigen Generalmajor von Sanitz und seine Smiłowicer Güter dem Generalleutnant von Bischofswerder geschenkt; anderes Vermögen besaß er im Lande nicht. Xaver von Jezierski, Kanonikus in Włocławek und Pfarrer zu Neustadt, wurde im Mémoire und von namhaft gemachten Personen als einer derer angegeben, die sich vor der Kujawischen Insurrektion am häufigsten bei Prusimski in Smiłowice aufgehalten und am tätigsten an ihrem Zustandekommen mitgewirkt hatten; er soll die Proklamationen an das Volk entworfen, Mannschaften zusammengebracht und in der Sicherheitskommission der Insurgenten gesessen haben. Auch gegen ihn wurde nur der Konfiskationsprozess eingeleitet; sein geringes Vermögen, die Einkünfte seiner Pfründen, wurde eingezogen und er der Pfründen entsetzt. Keiner der beiden ist des Adels für verlustig erklärt, und keine Lebensstrafe ist gegen sie festgesetzt oder vorbehalten. Die Minister finden, dass die beiden durch die Konfiskation, bei der es bleiben müsse (ein Punkt, zu dem Haugwitz sich als außerhalb seines Ressorts keiner Meinung äußert), und die neunjährige Verbannung härter bestraft seien als viele, die zurückkehrten und sich einer mäßigen Geld- oder Festungsstrafe unterwarfen, und dass ihre Teilnahme sie nicht gefährlicher erscheinen lasse als andere, die blieben oder zurückkehrten; da sie nur um die Rückkehr bitten, raten die Minister, dem Gesuch wohl zu willfahren, und stellen es dem Urteil des Königs anheim. Unterzeichnet Goldbeck, Haugwitz.

--- SUMMARY (English) ---
On 30 April 1803 the Grand Chancellor Goldbeck and Count Haugwitz reported jointly to King Friedrich Wilhelm III on the representation of the former Starost von Prusimski and of Xaver von Jezierski, who had left the country because of their part in the South Prussian insurrection of 1794 and had asked from Dresden for leave to return. The files from the Government at Poznań show: Anton von Prusimski was accused of having gone on 2 August 1794, before the outbreak of the insurrection, from his estates near Kalisz to his Smiłowice estates in the district of Kuyavia, of having received daily visits there from the neighbouring nobility, and of the so-called Kuyavian insurrection having been concluded with him on 21 August, with the act of confederation drafted and executed; he is also said to have been among the South Prussian noblemen who met at Dresden in the winter of 1793/94 on account of the changes in the Polish state. These accusations rest mostly on a Memoire raisonné drawn up from the statements of unnamed trusted Poles and officials, and were not closely examined. The Government did not think them sufficient for the criminal proceedings against absent offenders that were brought against some chief insurgents, but at the instance of the fiscal instituted only the confiscation proceedings, because he had not returned under the general ordinances. Publicly summoned, he held out hope of his return from Italy, where his wife, who also died there, was said to have had to stay for her health, and tried to avert the confiscation; as he did not appear, the sentence of confiscation was passed, and when it had become final his Trąbczyn estates were given to the Prince of Hohenlohe, his Pawłów estates to the present Major General von Sanitz and his Smiłowice estates to Lieutenant General von Bischofswerder; he held no other property in the country. Xaver von Jezierski, canon in Włocławek and parish priest at Neustadt, was named, in the Memoire and by named persons, as one of those who had stayed most often with Prusimski at Smiłowice before the Kuyavian insurrection and worked most actively to bring it about; he is said to have drafted the proclamations to the people, raised men and sat on the insurgents' security commission. Against him too only the confiscation proceedings were brought, and his small property, the income of his benefices, was confiscated and he was deprived of the benefices. Neither has been declared to have forfeited his nobility, and no capital punishment has been fixed or reserved against them. The ministers find that by the confiscation, which must stand (a point on which Haugwitz, as outside his department, expresses no opinion), and nine years' banishment the two have been punished more harshly than many who returned and submitted to a moderate fine or fortress term, and that their part does not mark them out as more dangerous than others who stayed or returned; since they ask only for leave to return, the ministers advise that it might be granted, leaving it to the King's judgement.

--- ENGLISH TRANSLATION (draft) ---

Your Royal Majesty has most graciously been pleased to send us, for a report, the enclosed original representation of the former Starost von Prusimski and of Xaver von Jezierski, who left the country because of their part in the South Prussian insurrection and have now asked from Dresden for leave to return to their fatherland.

The files required from the Government at Poznań having come in, we can now most humbly report the following.

1., Anton von Prusimski was accused of having gone on 2 August 1794, before the outbreak of the South Prussian insurrection, from his estates near Kalisz to his Smiłowice estates in the district of Kuyavia, which now belongs to the department of the Government at Poznań; of having received daily visits there from the neighbouring nobility; and of the so-called Kuyavian insurrection having been brought to completion at his house on 21 Aug., and the act of confederation drafted and executed. He is also said to be among those South Prussian noblemen who met at Dresden in the winter of 1793/94 on account of the changes in the Polish state. All these accusations, however, rest for the most part only on a so-called Memoire raisonné, drawn up from the statements of some unnamed trusted Poles and officials, and have not been closely examined.

Nor did the Government think them sufficient to order against him the criminal proceedings against absent offenders, as was done in respect of some chief insur

gents, but, at the instance of the fiscal, only instituted the confiscation proceedings, because he had not returned in accordance with the general ordinances issued.

The fugitive was duly summoned in public; he did, from Italy, where his wife, who also died there, allegedly had to stay at the time to recover her health, hold out hope of his return and seek to avert the confiscation; but as he did not appear after all, the sentence of confiscation was drawn up, and after it had become final, his Trąbczyn estates were given to the Prince of Hohenlohe, his Pawłów estates to the present Major General von Sanitz, and his Smiłowice estates to Lieutenant General von Bischofswerder. Besides these he held no other property in the country.

2., The second petitioner, Xaver von Jezierski, was a canon in Włocławek and parish priest at Neustadt. He was named, partly in the Memoire mentioned above, partly by persons who were named, as one of those who stayed most often with von Prusimski at Smiłowice before the outbreak of the Kuyavian insurrection and worked most actively to bring this insurrection about. He is said to have drafted the proclamations to the people, to have

raised men, and to have been a member of a security commission set up by the insurgents at the time.

Against him too, who did not return from his flight, only the confiscation proceedings were instituted, and confiscation of his small property was decreed with final effect; it consisted only of the income of his benefices, taken into sequestration and since filled otherwise, of which he was also deprived by the sentence.

Neither of the two petitioners has been declared to have forfeited his nobility, nor has any capital punishment otherwise been fixed or reserved against them.

In proportion to their part in the insurrection, they have been punished, by the confiscation of their property, at which it must remain unalterably /: a point on which I, Count von Haugwitz, refrain from expressing an opinion, since it is not of my department :/, and by a 9 years' banishment from their fatherland, more harshly than many others who returned and submitted to a moderate fine or a term in a fortress. Nor is their part of such a kind that it would mark them out as dangerous men before others who stayed in the country or returned to it later; and since their petition is directed only

at leave to return, we are most humbly of the opinion:

that it might well be granted;

which, however, we most devotedly leave entirely to Your Royal Majesty's most high judgement.

Berlin, 30 April 1803

Goldbeck. Haugwitz

To His Royal Majesty.
