Das von Bau Rath Glenck so gewis versicherte Anlehn bey der Invaliden Casse
scheitert auch, und zwar aus den Gründen. Das Invaliden Departement
verlangte, daß die Ottosche Taxe noch einmahl revidirt und nach Südpreussens
Cammer Principen umgeformt werden mögte. Glenck der den
kürzesten Weg hierzu einzuschlagen glaubte, wandte sich auf anrathen
eines gewißen Hauptman Lange, an den Hoffrath Leugnich, dieser
versprach die Taxa für 25 Fr der schnell umformen zu laßen.
Glenck glaubte dies, reisete ab und ersuchte nur Leugnich die
neue taxa an den H. R Seelmann abgeben zu laßen. Leztere
ist geschehen, zu meinen Schrecken aber erfuhr ich gestern bey meiner
Ankunft, daß die neuern Taxe auf 78/m. rt. herabgesezt worden
und daß solche in der Art übergeben sey.
den Erfolg können Ewr Hochfürstliche Durchlaucht abnehmen — das

furth wird, da diese Leute auf ein Anlehn von 80 bis 100/m rth
zählten, nun alles scheitern, den da Leixner in des Anlehns willen
Euer Hochfürstl. Durchlaucht davon muß, so muß er auch die Instru¬
mente sehen, und auf der Art bekömt er die niederer Taxe in
Gesichte, die man, wenn das Anlehn nicht nötig gewesen wäre,
um vorzeigen dürfen. Erhalte ich nun auch noch woher 60000 rt

schädlich werden und vieleicht gänzlich behindern.
Meine einzige Hoffnung ist nun noch auf Stubenrauch gestürzt

--- SUMMARY (German) ---
Das von Glenck zugesicherte Anlehn der Invaliden Casse scheitert, weil die Ottosche Taxe auf 78/m Rthl herabgesetzt wurde; auch Stubenrauch und das Pupillen Collegio versagen. Beyme ließ Amelang sagen, der König wolle dem Fürsten als Entschädigung für die Brzyce-Güter seine Schulden erlassen. Amelang schlug die konfiszierten Kolziger Güter Schlabrendorffs vor. Triebenfeld will versuchen, ob man auf Konotop und Zagórów 120/m Rthl leiht; gelänge dies, könnte man die Güter von Zagórów und Trąbczyn in Ruhe dismembrieren.

--- SUMMARY (English) ---
The loan from the Invalids' Fund promised by von Glenck has fallen through, because the Otto valuation was reduced to 78/m Rthl; Stubenrauch and the Wards' Board also refuse. Beyme had Amelang told that the King wishes to remit the Prince's debts as compensation for the Brzyce estates. Amelang proposed Schlabrendorff's confiscated Kolzig estates. Triebenfeld intends to try whether 120/m Rthl can be borrowed on Konotop and Zagórów; if this succeeded, the Zagórów and Trąbczyn estates could be broken up at leisure.

Most Serene Prince

Most gracious Prince and Lord!

The loan with the Invalids' Fund, which the building councillor Glenck assured so positively, has likewise failed, and for the following reasons. The Invalids' Department demanded that the Otto valuation be once again revised and recast according to the principles of the South Prussian Chamber. Glenck, who thought to take the shortest road to this, turned, on the advice of a certain Captain Lange, to the Court Councillor Leugnich; the latter promised to have the valuation quickly recast for 25 Fr. Glenck believed this, departed, and only requested Leugnich to have the new valuation delivered to Councillor Seelmann. The latter has been done, but to my horror I learned yesterday on my arrival that the newer valuation had been reduced to 78/m Rthl and that it had been handed over in that form.

Your Most Serene Highness may gather the consequence — but the saddest thing of all is now that by this mutilated valuation the estates are wholly degraded in worth, and that the sale of them will be made very, very much harder. Even in Frankfurt an der Oder, since those people were counting on a loan of 80 to 100/m Rthl,

everything will now fail, for since Leixner must [garbled] Your Most Serene Highness for the sake of the loan, he must also see the instruments, and in that way he gets the lower valuation before his eyes, which, had the loan not been necessary, one [uncertain: um] have been permitted to show. If I now still obtain 60,000 Rthl lent from somewhere on the lower valuation, it will be extremely damaging to the sale and perhaps hinder it altogether. My only hope is now cast upon Stubenrauch, to whom I have promised to furnish eviction and every conceivable guarantee. This evening he intends to give his declaration — if this plan fails, then all here is lost, for all the other projects are not worth a single Groschen either, and are merely drawn up by a certain Dietz, Werner, the Jew Borchart,

Mr Lange and such rabble, who have neither head nor credit, and who only needed written undertakings from Glenck in order to be able to borrow something on them, so as not to perish of hunger. It is a sad thing that Glenck reckoned on the swindles of this brood and so very culpably frittered away the time

God is my witness that I cannot sleep for grief. [uncertain: My] most heartfelt wish is that Heaven might but govern Stubenrauch's heart, for then things will go well — gladly would I pledge him my goods and my poor afflicted soul.

Yesterday Beyme had the Privy Councillor Amelang called to him, and told him that His Majesty could give Your Serene Highness neither South Prussian estates nor money. Your Serene Highness, however, owed the King very considerable sums, apart from a loan from the Duke of Courland, for which the King had hitherto paid the interest. Since His Majesty doubted of recovering a single Groschen of the sums lent and of the interest paid by way of advance, His Majesty was willing, out of particular grace, to make Your Serene Highness a gift of these sums, as also of the interest hitherto paid to the Courlanders, as a compensation for the lost Brzyce estates. In return for which, however, Your Serene Highness might from now on satisfy the Courlanders both as to capital and as to interest. This was not His Majesty's [uncertain: ner] notion. With the best heart, and the love that he bears Your Serene Highness, he had not been able to bring about more than this. He was also to have given Your Serene Highness this resolution at once, but as he did not know whether such would be agreeable to Your Serene Highness, he had

held it back for the present, and wished that Amelang might first sound out Your Serene Highness on the matter. Amelang then answered him: he knew that Your Serene Highness wished to have nothing given to you by the King, that you would faithfully pay His Majesty everything that you rightfully owed him, that Your Serene Highness demanded nothing but what you could rightfully claim, and that you believed you had the first right, when you pressed, directly and indirectly, for a compensation for the estates taken from you, for which Your Serene Highness had to pay many thousands in [garbled: Charger Stempel um andere Gebürz], and where in addition you had had much money standing. Amelang then proposed to him that the Kolzig estates confiscated from Count Schlabrendorff might be given to Your Serene Highness as a compensation — to this he replied that the estates had not yet been sequestered, that the King would probably rather keep these estates himself, since His Majesty had a distaste for the frightening away of estates, carried over from the previous government; yet in this matter the first possibility was still present, of obliging the Prince — Amelang knew all of it, however, for it [uncertain: träst] word for word with what the honest General had said. — It was particularly

too plain that through his mediation the fine answer had been held back. All this raised Amelang's courage, and, being prepared, he spoke more freely. Amelang also now lives in the first hope that they will be civil and give no coarse resolution, but will hold it back altogether — and also that they will hasten to execute the confiscation, or the judgment to that effect, in order to be able to confer the Kolzig estates upon Your Serene Highness.

Braun will keep the General Köckritz in play and guide the matter along that road — Amelang will do the like with Beyme in his own manner — but neither knows anything of the other, and according to this plan both must in the end, without having known it, meet at one and the same point.

For the rest, Amelang will draft a letter to Beyme and perhaps to the King, which Your Serene Highness is to execute and send off. I have not yet spoken with the Minister von Hardenberg, but it shall certainly be done tomorrow. The Prince is by no means willing to engage in any war; meanwhile Russia is said not to be willing this time to let the matter rest with empty remonstrances. His Majesty has departed somewhat very ill-humoured to Pomerania for the review, and will be home again on the 5th of next month.

God have mercy! What a perverse creature this Glenck is — now all is over. Stubenrauch has been provoked by the running-in of bad fellows and will have nothing to do with the matter. The Invalids' Fund will give no money — just so, everything is spoiled with the Wards' Board — what am I now to set about?

Sell Konotop? what not then Kolzig — O God, my [uncertain: fine] plan! O had Your Serene Highness but put the fool on a golden chain and had him fed with Strasbourg pies, then his keep would indeed have cost much, but at least he could neither have undermined your peace, still less brought you here to the loss of credit and good name. It is more than shameful that the man has here got himself involved with every low beast of a broker, and has given every knave written undertakings for sums and entered into partnership contracts with them. It will in the end become necessary to have all these slips declared null and void through the public papers, since otherwise Your Serene Highness may yet be burdened with 50 lawsuits.

I grow dizzy when I think of the near term, and when at one stroke the last spark of hope too is extinguished. It is now 1 o'clock in the night — I can neither rest nor repose for anguish, and the beast in human shape sleeps without a care — O righteous God, what is a man without true sense of honour?

O had the unreasonable man but not, to the very greatest misfortune, let the Otto valuation be so destroyed and mutilated, then I, with Amelang — who shows himself active and who now gains credit through Beyme — should still have found much counsel, and could have procured the money on my name or on bills of exchange / since I am held to be rich /. But this prospect too is robbed from me by the destruction of the Otto valuation. I will at any rate try, and within an hour I ride to Potsdam to Pliehen; he told me some days ago that he had 300/m Rthl lying in Maritime Trading Company obligations, and that he wished to make a trial for once whether he might not gain something by a purchase of estates — perhaps God will give good fortune by this road — therefore when I return, more of this. Money would now be to be had. Rietz has money — Prince Ferdinand too is willing to give it through Stubenrauch's mediation — but they demand secure mortgage. Where, though, am I to take this from?

The valuations of the Zagórów estates are only 124/m Rthl, and these are burdened with 56,000 Rthl, thus encumbered above the half — for that the estates are worth four times as much I know — but the valuations tell against it, and consequently it is not to be counted upon. Kolno and Kamionna are bungled, for the present valuation is 118,000 Rthl, and 66/m Rthl have been awarded to Broniewski, so that there too no fund remains upon which money might be taken or demanded with security,

I shall now still attempt this — I shall try whether anyone will lend 120/m Rthl on Konotop and Zagórów, for which I shall further offer a bill of exchange. If this went through, then everything might still be [uncertain: redressed], for then one would have time to conclude the Kamionna business solidly and to see to a good sale, and also to dismember the Zagórów and Trąbczyn estates at leisure. If I accomplish nothing, then I hasten quickly to Frankfurt an der Oder and try there once more [garbled: man heil]. In deepest reverence I remain, until death,

Your Most Serene Highness's

Berlin, the 26th of May 1804

most faithful servant

v Triebenfeld
