Curiously, there was more material on this question in the Polish archives than in the Roman archives, destroyed by the reaction. At Mantua two Jacobins, General F. Rymkiewicz and Captain C. Godebski, edited the first Polish military periodical, La Décade des Légions.

The Polish Legions, which had from 10,000 to 15,000 soldiers under arms, perfectly trained and ready for battle, aroused hopes and fears in Italy: the hope that they would protect the country against Austria, and the fear that, making use of their military strength, they would create their own state on the territory of Tuscany or Apulia. It is true that in 1798 the Consulate of Rome itself proposed to give land in the neighbourhood of Rome to 3,000 Polish legionaries, so that, as military colonists, they would support the new régime. General Dąbrowski laughed at these suspicions and proposals and declared that, beautiful as Italy was, the Poles wanted to live only in their own country. Only the Ionian Islands were at one moment considered, as an independent base from which to set out for occupied Poland by way of Greece.

What remains of this period are the rich official archives of the emigrés and the legionaries, and also private archives, for many legionaries married in Italy. It is enough to recall that the son of the commander of the Legions, in time a general himself, Jean Michel Dąbrowski, married a Genoese, Emilia di Negro. The daughter of General Jean Henri Dąbrowski, Karolina, married a Neapolitan who later became famous, General Joseph Prince Palombini. General Joseph Grabiński married Marie Broglie d'Amera and, as a Bolognese landowner, was appointed commander-in-chief of the anti-papal rising of Bologna in 1830. The examples multiply. Some dozens of memoirs that deal with Italian questions in the years 1794 to 1807 have survived. They throw valuable light on this period. During several stays in Italy I have found, in talking with my colleagues, Italian professors, that these papers were unknown to them.
