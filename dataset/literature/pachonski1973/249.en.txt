# Pachoński (1973), p. 249
# translation by Claude

The supreme command of the Venetian army in the hands of Poles

In 1796 the Prince of Nassau-Siegen came back to Venice with considerable funds. His royalist attitude was so well regarded by the Signoria that in April 1796, when the time came to award the supreme command of the Venetian army, it was offered to him. The other candidates were the Prince de Broglie, a French emigré living at Turin, and the Polish general Jean Komarzewski, called back from Dresden.

The Emperor's first minister, Baron François Thugut, was hostile to the candidacy of the Prince of Nassau-Siegen, and made this known (in June) to the Venetian ambassador in Vienna, Agostino Garzoni. Thereupon it was decided to offer the supreme command to General Komarzewski, whose chief rival was the former colonel of the French Swiss Guards, de Salis[^25]. General Komarzewski stayed at the Albergo Reale from 6 June to the beginning of August, negotiating with the Signoria. A remarkably gifted organiser, head of Stanisław August's military chancery[^26], he was not popular in Poland because of his devotion to the King's person, which forced him to resign in 1788. His

[^26]: Jean Komarzewski (1744-1810), one of the most eminent generals of the age of Stanisław August. He passed from the Prussian army to the Polish. In 1774 he became colonel and general aide-de-camp to the King, in 1776 major-general attached to His Majesty and head of the King's military chancery, and lieutenant-general in 1778. Gifted, cultivated and energetic, he helped to raise the standard of the Polish army. He was entrusted with delicate missions, notably to Petersburg and Berlin. Deputy in 1784 and 1786. But for his devotion to the King he was opposed both by the magnates and by the patriotic parties during the Great Diet, who forced him to resign and go abroad. In 1792 he sent 2,000 ducats from England for the struggle against Russia. During the insurrection of 1794 he drew up a plan for the defence of Warsaw. After the dismemberment of Poland he went with the King into exile. After the King's death he refused to enter the service of Russia. He took up mineralogical studies in Warsaw, and later took his collections to Dresden and Paris. J. Komarzewski held the highest Polish and Russian distinctions; he was a freemason. Of his writings, the study of the graphometer, which could replace the compass (Paris, 1803), deserves attention, as does a Coup d'oeil rapide sur les causes réelles de la décadence de la Pologne (1807), an apology for King Stanisław August. Member of the Warsaw Society of Friends of Learning. He died in Paris on 19 March 1810; cf. Encyclopédie Militaire, Warsaw, vol. IV, 1934.
