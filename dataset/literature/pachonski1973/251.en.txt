Sołtyk himself hesitated: seeing the discord between the groups of P. Potocki and F. Łaźniński, he did not believe it possible to reconcile the Deputation with the opposition. In the end he sided with the Polish Emigrés, when he learned that General Łaźniński was intercepting part of his mail in order to inform the Deputation of it. This decided him not to pass on to the Deputation the full powers of the Centre (contained in the Act of Confederation of Cracow and the Act of Confederation of Żwaniec); he considered that inside Poland the state of the emigration was not known, and that it was the Deputation that bore the responsibility for the dissension.

Thanks to Sołtyk's subsidies Prozor was able to keep the "Palace of the Emigration". P. Potocki, as leader of the moderates at Venice, held that governments abroad made no sense, since they represented only a handful of people brought together by chance, and that only the Great Diet could carry weight. As this view was shared by J. Wybicki too, that is, by the Polish Emigrés, Potocki went to the minister Barthélemy at Basel to sound out the ground, and to learn in particular whether the French government would support the idea of convening the Great Diet.

The Poles of Venice and the Legions

A new situation arose after the arrival in Paris from Warsaw of Lieutenant-General Jean-Henri Dąbrowski, renowned for the victories he had won in the Insurrection, whom the French government authorised to form Polish Legions on Italian territory[^30].
