From the beginning of the twentieth century no new work on our subject has appeared[^4]. The "Black Register" nevertheless found its place, often with an effect worth recording, in German monographs of broader themes[^5].

While in the works of Polish historians the stressing of the rapacious and Germanising aspect of the giveaway of estates dominated, German historiography showed interest chiefly in the economic significance of the operation, interpreting the giveaway of estates as the drawing of Prussian capital into economically neglected Polish lands. The justification for selling off the domainal estates to private owners was the miserable state of the Prussian state's finances. It was therefore more profitable to hand the neglected estates to good husbanders, who would not only pay for them but also invest in them, and then pay taxes to the state treasury. Yet there were also many historians (Mayer, Philippson, Stadelmann, Bissing, Simsch, Beheim-Schwarzbach) who noticed that in practice the giveaway was very far from the ideal presented above. They pointed to corruption, the depriving of the state treasury of its due revenues, speculation in landed estates[^6].

C. Grünhagen, on the other hand, not only came out in defence of the giveaway — as lying in the interest of the Prussian state — but, drawing on sources unknown until then, attacked the whole critical current against Hoym[^7] and his "cabal" and against the rule of Frederick William II. Grünhagen began by forcing the thesis that the donations were a real reward for officers and officials who had deserved well of the Prussian state, and ended by writing of them as an acquisition for the donataries so burdensome and unprofitable that it might have been the king's spite towards his Prussian subjects and a wish to fleece them under the perverse pretext of a gift[^8]. The donataries (Donatarien), as Frederick William II had them called — first had to pay for the estate often the ordinary — in Grünhagen's opinion — price.
