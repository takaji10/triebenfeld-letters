Hence too the whole affair — admittedly with enormous difficulties, but still — made its way into public knowledge. There appeared here charges of corruption, ineptitude and nepotism reigning in the South Prussian administration, whose aftermath was a series of contemporary writings, headed by the publications of Friedrich von Cölln. He asserted that the originator of the whole action of the giveaway of estates was the wife of General Bischofswerder, the king's adjutant[^62]. She was to be gnawing at her husband's head all day long — as Cölln presented it bluntly — that he would "die as a beggar" if he did not exploit the last days of the king to do "something" for his family[^63]. True or not, such information could seem so far probable that in fact Bischofswerder — the king's adjutant, owed everything he possessed to Frederick William II, whose companion he had been from childhood. It is also true that the action covered above all the circle of Hoym's camarilla, and nothing of the spoils fell to the people connected with the minister Otto Karl von Voss, pushed aside in the years 1794-1798[^64], Struensee, Schulenburg, Schrötter, Klewitz, also Stein and Schön, who were only beginning their careers. It must also be said that after the sidelining of Voss Hoym filled the chief posts in South Prussia with his people; then too Buchholtz was appointed to the office of over-president in Poznań. Likewise Wilhelm Anton von Klewitz arrived in South Prussia together with Voss's taking of office, and was dismissed in 1795 by Hoym. Then his return came about after Hoym's fall, in 1798[^65].

The disclosure of the abuses connected with the giveaway of estates came at the highest levels of power. Buchholtz, at odds with Hoym from 1795, on the very day of the death of Frederick William II, 16 November 1797, handed to the heir to the throne a memorial containing in the appendix a list of the South Prussian donations, which then became the basis for the "Black Register". The memorial pointed to it that with million-fold losses to the state treasury Hoym conducted on his own hand "for a song", more precisely for 1/3 of the true value, the giveaway of state estates, taking no account at all of the interest of Prussia[^66].
