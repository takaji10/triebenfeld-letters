# Łukasiewicz (1997), p. 51
# translation by Claude

to assure, had dismissed for financial malversations councillor Schmaling (1805)[^116], wrote in dejection that the Germans distinguish themselves in nothing against the Poles. Part of his officials is wholly demoralised and fit for immediate exchange. It was simpler to exchange Klewitz[^117].

The heaviest charges were thrown by Friedrich von Cölln, Hans von Held, Joseph Zerboni di Sposetti, Wilhelm von Klewitz. Theodor von Schön, at the time connected with the minister of New East Prussia von Schrötter, wrote of it without restraint[^118].

To these charges joined the most notorious of the malversators, Triebenfeld, lamenting with cynicism over the corruption rampaging in South Prussia. Joseph Zerboni disowned the authorship of the „Aktenstücke", but the picture given there is very truthful[^119]. Franciszek Gajewski or Fryderyk Skarbek, people who besides possessed the rare ability to notice how many things we could learn from the Germans and at the same time how numerous were their vices, wrote of the South Prussian times that the German official usually arrived in Poland "as a poor gentleman with a knapsack on his back", "to try his luck in Poland". A moment later the same man was buying himself estates[^120]. When the tax commission appeared at Prince Antoni Sułkowski's in Rydzyna in 1811, its members were all Germans; this however did not prevent the prince, for a small sum, from buying the assessor, for, as he wrote, the official had a wife and a numerous family to keep up, and a miserable stipend[^121].
