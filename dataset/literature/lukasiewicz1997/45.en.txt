With such an extract he went to the bank in Berlin, to draw a low-interest mortgage credit, whose admissible value was 100% of the value of the estate. Here however it turned out that all the cash had already been drawn by the private Jewish banker Heymann Ephraim, who made his living on the intermediation in the granting of loans in Poland. Ephraim disregarded the mortgage papers, knowing how one obtains them, but agreed to accept a bribe of 30 000 thalers, in return for granting credit against them. Triebenfeld moreover mentions in his secret memorial that the Polish nobility too, because of the mortgage credit, sought to obtain in the mortgage register an entry four times higher than the real value[^72]. The credit aspect of the Prussian giveaway throws new light on the question of the famous "bajońskie sums", which were to have been squandered by the Polish nobility. A proportionally considerable share of the borrowers were the Prussian donataries[^73]:

Attempts to limit the speculation in estates found reflection in the legislative activity of the Prussian state. In the "Publicandum" first issued in 1797 in Silesia, and in 1802 in South Prussia, it is announced that there is a fictitious sale of landed estates, aiming at the obtaining of a higher entry in the mortgage registers, thanks to which it was possible to cheat the true buyer, the leaseholder, and above all to obtain a higher mortgage credit. To prevent this the authorities ordered that in all operations the mortgage extract should give not only the last but also the earlier entries of the value of the estate, as well as the basic valuation[^74]. The selection of the donataries, contrary to economic reason, additionally deepened the speculative way of treating the donations. Of the lack of any proper control over the conducting of the action testifies the fact that the post director Goldbeck received outright an estate worth 11 000 thalers by mistake, instead of the son of the minister of the same name[^75]. It did not behove the director indeed to take the estates any more, but the interests of the young councillor Goldbeck also did not suffer. The mistake was put right and he too got his share. Lieutenant General Rüchel for 30 000 thalers bought estates which, without ever having seen them, he sold to councillor Reibnitz[^76] of Kalisz for 130 000 thalers.
