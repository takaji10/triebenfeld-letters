of the Middle Ages knew the ordalias, that is the judgments of God[^35], the backward reference (the invoking of a ruling that had fallen in the same matter in another court), the attestation of the judge, consisting in the confirmation through the judge that a given ruling was given. These means die away gradually with the spread of the court books, in which the rulings are written. The oath of the parties was applied too, the depositions of the usher, the depositions of the witnesses and the proofs from the documents, growing in weight. With time, however, ever greater significance is gained by the official investigation called the skrutynium, conducted by the instygator as an organ neutral and objective with respect to the parties, who in turn did not know the law and were not able to prove their arguments duly, in particular in the criminal matters, where the official authority to make certain findings mattered. In the situation when the defendant did not stand forth at the fatal term (at the first one unexcused, or after four puttings-off), a default hearing was held (called also the non-standing one), in the absence of the defendant the perpetrator[^36]. If still in the 16th century the calls of the usher to stand forth before the court were respected, with time there appeared ever more default hearings. The Commonwealth of the time possessed no organs that could bring the defendant before the court by force, the various offenders avoided the justice effectively, holding the legal order in contempt. To be sure, the default ruling was given to the advantage of the plaintiff, yet given the lack of the execution it was ineffective, and the perpetrators of the crimes often fled beyond the borders of the country, e.g. to the Dzikie Pola or under the protection of the neighbouring rulers. An example of such a behaviour is the case of the kniaz Michał Gliński, who after the death of Aleksander Jagiellończyk sought the power over the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. The lawful heir of the throne offered the usurper an armed resistance and defeated Gliński. That one, however, making as if to amend, laid down his arms and humbled himself before the king Zygmunt Jagiellończyk. Quickly he began again to conspire against the sovereign. The case of the treason of the estate stood before the royal court, and the treacherous traitor was prosecuted as instygator by the Marshal of Lithuania Jan Zabrzeziński. Gliński did not stand forth before the court despite the call, to cleanse himself of the accusations, which were well documented by the depositions

[^35]: The ordalias consisted in the ordeal of the cold water (the participant of the process, tied up, was cast on the water, and if he sank it was held that he won the case, for — as it was believed — the water, as a pure element, did not take the guilty, the unclean), on the ordeal of the hot water (the drawing of the objects out of the boiling water with the hands), and on the ordeal of the iron (the carrying of an object heated to redness).

[^36]: M. Borucki, op. cit., pp. 32–33.
