# Kucharski (2023), p. 248
# translation by Claude

The only institution appointed and entitled to the execution of the rulings was the starost, who, as we know, acted in the name of the royal power. The starost took into the prison the perpetrators, whom the wronged had caught and delivered at once to his office. The prisoner sat in the arrest only as long as the interested wronged party or his family delivered the means for his keep. Curiously looked the practice of the execution of the rulings against immovables. The starost's service met enormous obstacles in the carrying out of such rulings, but the situation was easier all the same than in the case of the pursuing of the criminals condemned to death. The mighty noblemen disposed of a numerous service and let no representatives of the judicial powers into their estates. In such a situation the starost organised an armed raid on the estates of the disobedient convict. The whole nobility of the district had the duty of the delivering of the victuals and the support for such an expedition. The sejm statutes of the beginning of the 17th century already clearly laid such a duty on the nobility, foreseeing the punishment of 100 marks for its not being carried out. The person against whom such a raid was organised had the status of a banite, that is of one taken out from under the law, and so his murder went unpunished. The estates of such a person could be plundered, and after their taking the feasts lasted at the banite's cost. The life of such a nobleman was not at all easy, for one condemned by a judicial ruling to banition could be killed unpunished by anyone whom he offended, caught and given to the starost for the carrying out of the punishment. He was moreover deprived of honour, which at that time bound itself with a civil death. No self-respecting nobleman would give him his hand, nor wish to enter any relations. A person held for unhonourable, and such was an *infamis*, counted for nothing socially and had no right to stand at the sejmiks.

The court acts of the age deliver numerous examples of the people of noble birth troubling themselves with the common criminal misdeeds. A rather frequent practice was the meting out of the justice on one's own hand, not believing in the effective execution of the rulings fallen, which the condemned held in contempt and ignored. A known Kraków rich merchant, Walerian Montelupi, a descendant of the Italian immigrants, held a landed estate in the village of Piersice near Sandomierz. One day in the year 1609, staying at his manor, he was fallen upon by an armed band under the command of Zygmunt Łaziński. The merchant was taken, beaten, bound and stolen away in a stolen coach to Łaziński's village of Siniawy in the district of Sanok. There the Italian was shut in a dungeon, and to the family there were sent letters written by Montelupi with the demand of a ransom for the freeing of the kidnapped, under the threat of his being tortured
