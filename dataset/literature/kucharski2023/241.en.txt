The judicial practice of the time cared for the highest securing of the rooms of the chancellery, which were located in buildings of brick bought and kept specially for that purpose, with the aim of ensuring the perpetual durability of the documents. With the fixing of the new structures of the courts and of the practice of the administration of justice of the noble age, separate buildings appear, where the land courts have their seats and at them the court chancelleries are located, where the court documents are kept. The authorities cared in particular for the proper securing of the archives, through the providing of solid gratings in the windows and of doors covered with a thick sheet iron with locks, for that was indispensable for the assurance of the certainty of the legal dealings through the securing of the court documentation. The court books at the beginning were not surrounded by any particular care, for it was accepted at the time that they were the property of the starost, and as such were treated, after his death, as parts of the inheritance. Only the winning of the right of perpetuity[^27] by the courts changed that situation, in particular after the taking of the sejmik constitution of the year 1496, where it was resolved that "in case a starosty falls empty, the books are to be laid in the town halls, in separate chests, under the keys of the voivode, of the land judge and of the starost in office"[^28].

# 3.5. The usher

The technical and the order outfitting of the courts of the time was provided by the usher, who did not have to be a nobleman, which caused that in that role there appeared many people of peasant origin. The name of the office came from the function of the carrying of the documents of the princely chancelleries as a carter. They were nominated by the voivode, and in the later period by the sejmiks; they took the oath, after which their symbolic hair-cutting was done, which symbolised the submission to the power of the voivode, who performed that rite in person.
