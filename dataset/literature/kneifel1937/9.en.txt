This had as its consequence, in the territories named, the rise of numerous German-evangelical villages, which in turn, in the course of development, led to the founding of congregations. After 1890 hundreds of colonists emigrated from the Kalisch land, but especially from Kujawien, to America.

The ecclesiastical gathering of the evangelical population settled in the villages and towns often met with considerable difficulties. At first it was chiefly economic reasons that stood in its way. The processes of settlement and of the putting down of roots by the colonists extended for the most part over one or two decades. How difficult these beginnings were the archives bear witness again and again. Thus Pastor Grimm-Stawiszyn writes, for example, of his congregation members that they "dwell in huts of turf." These huts of earth were precisely emergency forms of settler life, which played itself out in merciless narrowness and hardness[^p9-7] Only after the German peasants, in laborious years-long work of clearing, had wrested from the often meager soil bread for themselves and their families could they set about building up a cultural and religious life of their own. Already at the laying out of a colony it was the custom to set aside a plot of land for school and house of prayer. After the distressful time of putting down roots had been overcome, simple school and prayer houses, mostly thatched with straw, were erected, and a teacher and cantor was called (often a landless farmer or a craftsman). "In most places, especially in the voivodeship of Kalisch, the evangelical schools came into being in this way: when one, two, or three evangelical families settled in a place remote from all evangelical churches, they chose out from among themselves someone who would instruct their children and hold edification hours for them on Sundays . . ."[^p9-7a] Through the union of the school with the house of prayer there arose everywhere in central Poland, and later also in Volhynia and other territories, that cantorate system so characteristic of the Evangelical-Augsburg church in Poland, which in all probability represents an intermediate form between the Swabian "Stunde" and the "pre-state" type of school and is an original creation of our colonist people.
