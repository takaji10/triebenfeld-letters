The Act of Kraków of 24 March, as we know, proclaimed a settling of accounts by force of arms with Frederick William II, when it expressed indignation at the falsehood, hypocrisy and deceit of Prussia and Russia and announced the restoration to the fatherland of its integrity and independence. To carry out that announcement, however, a struggle on two fronts was necessary. Yet Kościuszko, although he was devoted with his whole heart to the cause of liberty, would gladly have avoided that struggle. For that reason, no doubt, he passed over the best opportunity of striking at the Prussians right at the beginning of the uprising, when their mobilisation was only in its initial stage and when Lieutenant-General Schwerin felt himself so weak that he did not dare to heed the repeated summonses of Igelström and of the War College in Berlin to attack Kościuszko, in conjunction with the Russian forces stationed west of the Vistula, and crush the uprising in the bud. Soon the situation became so unfavourable for the Commander (his being pushed into a corner by the Vistula) that he could act in no other way than by proclamations. He then abandoned the idea of fighting for the western borders of the Commonwealth, and the work of the emissaries, supposedly already dispatched to the Sieradz and Łęczyca, Kalisz and Poznań regions in order to extend the movement in those parts and to carry it over into Silesia, could for the time being produce no result[^81].

The policy of the Supreme Commander towards Prussia is characterised most eloquently by the instructions he gave to the president of Warsaw, Zakrzewski. He ordered him, namely, to come to an understanding with Buchholtz in secret and to declare that it was "only a war declared on him for policy's sake", and that therefore "let his Court give peace to our borders, and we shall not touch him[^82]".
