The Supreme National Council, in this almost last hour of the Commonwealth's existence, sought ways of rescue in new military combinations, though of little realism[^376]; it offered a large reward to the corps that should be able to "clear the river Bzura of the enemy and open up Greater Poland"[^377]. At the council of war in Warsaw on 27 October, Dąbrowski put forward, as yet another way of rescue, the abandonment of Praga and Warsaw, which in his opinion were not capable of defending themselves. He advised "marching into Greater Poland with the whole strength of the Commonwealth and with numerous artillery", with the king and the Supreme Council, and there, despite the fall of the capital, to see the winter through with the surviving army and government, awaiting French intervention or a falling-out on the part of one of the neighbouring states[^378].

The premises of the plan were sound and rested on rational foundations. For winter was approaching, desertion was beginning to creep into the Prussian ranks, diseases were rampant, and there was considerable "discontent" there on account of the unsuccessful campaign. The corps left behind in Greater Poland by Dąbrowski were "growing immensely". Major Bielamowski's detachment numbered 600 horse, and the Łęczyca, Sieradz and Kuyavian risings were already 3,000 strong. If, therefore, the main army, advancing westwards, had taken the line of the Bzura, the Prussians would have been caught between two fires, and it would then have been easy to cut all their communications together with the supply of provisions. Pressed from two sides, with an army partly disorganised, they would have retreated behind the Oder, or at least behind the Warta. The Russians' pursuit of the Polish army would have been very difficult and slow owing to the lack of means of subsistence[^379].
