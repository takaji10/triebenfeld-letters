General Dąbrowski then invited everyone to a soldiers' dinner, where, with a modesty becoming to republicans, they made merry until evening[^215]". On the same day Dąbrowski and Madaliński issued a proclamation in which they acquainted the population at large with the plunder and cruelties committed by Col. Székely. They declared that all this had happened on the express order of Frederick William II, as was irrefutably proved by a royal letter, intercepted between Łowicz and Gąbin and now in their possession. They announced that in this letter the king expressly recommended to Székely that he set up gallows for the nobility and imprison them, and burn the letter "C" into the hands of the peasants, and that only the fear that a similar fate might befall the Prussian officials taken prisoner had kept Székely from carrying out the ruthless order. Yet this fear, as they demonstrated in the proclamation, was in vain. For Székely, as a "barbarian", did not know the magnanimity of the Poles, who abhor such deeds. Finally, the proclamation appealed to the Poles of the First Partition to return to the bosom of the fatherland, joining those who had come to rescue them, and to the inhabitants of both banks of the Oder, the former Slavs, the Brandenburgers and the Prussians, "subjugated for centuries by the house of Brandenburg[^216]".

On the following day, early in the morning, the Polish army left Gniezno[^217]. Dąbrowski sent a detachment of 200 horse under the command of the intrepid partisan Bielamowski toward Poznań, "to put it into the enemy's mind"[^218] that the Poles intended to attack that city.
