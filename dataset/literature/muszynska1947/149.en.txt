The numerous landless nobility, residing with the more powerful landowners or loitering about the towns, was regarded as an element especially susceptible to revolution; the Prussian government accordingly extended watchful control over it. Within six weeks of the date of publication of the said patent, every individual of this kind was obliged to report to the district authority, present a certificate of origin, name his settled acquaintances, friends and relatives, and declare whether he intended to remain in the country or to go abroad. The emigration of this nobility was highly desirable to the government, which therefore announced that "permission for it will be neither refused nor made difficult"[^498]; on return, however, a passport and various credentials were demanded, and every effort was made to make it as difficult as possible[^499].

The Prussian government likewise paid close attention to the clergy, for the clergy had "in the last unfortunate insurrection justly drawn upon itself the suspicion" of being guilty of "genuine revolts, of personal partiality and active support for harmful conspiracies, and even of secret associations and agreements aimed at the overthrow of the government of the country, and of other culpable undertakings from which every feeling of respect for law and moral order recoils[^500]."
