In order to safeguard the Greater Poles against undesirable influences, a severe censorship was introduced. The author was answerable for the content of a book even after it had been censored. Publication of a work without censorship entailed a fine[^512].

Simultaneously with issuing new regulations, the Prussian government sought to extract from Greater Poland the greatest possible material benefits. It paid particular attention to agriculture and forestry, since the main part of its revenues flowed from them[^513]. It forbade the division of the so-called knightly estates (Rittergüter) of no more than 10 włóki (the włóka being c. 17 ha), which, as a result of the incessant partitioning among the members of expanding families, were falling more and more into decay[^514]. In addition to the ecclesiastical estates, in 1796 it also seized for the treasury the non-castle starostwa[^515]. Both kinds formed the so-called domains, whose administration was directed by the Chambers. The government raised taxes, increasing the ofiara to 24 groszy, and the hearth tax by one half. To improve communications it cleared the Warta and made it navigable[^516].

The Prussian government likewise spared neither effort nor expense in order to entrench the German element in Greater Poland. For this purpose it brought in a multitude of officials, sent in colonists and peasants for public works, and leased the domains only to Germans. In South Prussia alone, within 4 years under the administration of Minister Hoym, 241 estates were distributed among 52 grantees. The starostwo of Międzyrzecz, with a number of picturesquely situated manor farms, was received, as a token of the royal "satisfaction" for services rendered to Prussia over many years, by Lucchesini[^517].
