On 26 August a detachment of insurgents entered Żnin [Znin], removed the Prussian eagles, seized the treasury, induced the citizens to swear an oath of loyalty to the Commonwealth, and issued a proclamation summoning the owners of estates to appear at Gniezno together with their subjects[^145], bringing arms and provisions. These proclamations, as elsewhere, were announced from the pulpits by the parish priests. From Żnin the flame of revolt began to spread farther and embraced Szubin [Schubin], Barcin, Gąsawa, and even Sępolno [Zempelburg] in Pomerania[^146]. The Prussian authorities, seeing no other way of checking the spreading insurrection because of the small number of troops, issued proclamations calling on the population to remain faithful in their subjection and to seize the insurgents, and also recalling the benefits that flowed to it from Prussian rule. A circular was also issued at Kwidzyn [Marienwerder], warning the clergy against supporting the rising and forbidding clerics from South Prussia to enter West Prussia. For the Prussians considered priests very dangerous. They were, after all, above all the ardent propagators of the insurrection, and through their frequent journeys they could easily spread the watchwords of revolt. These measures were, however, ineffective. The rising spread, and even in Piła [Schneidemühl], so thoroughly Germanised later, it had its supporters. Everywhere the clergy and the poor nobility sincerely favoured it. Thus in the small town of Kcynia [Exin] Krupecki was a zealous champion of the insurrectionary idea. Seeing the sluggishness of the magnates toward the national cause, he compelled several of them to take part in the insurgent movement.
