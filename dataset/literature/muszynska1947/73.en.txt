In carrying out the commissions of his monarch, the old Schwerin proved extraordinarily cautious and timorous. He was kept in this state by rumours reaching his headquarters which bore only too eloquent witness to the grave situation of the Prussians. It was said that the 4,000-strong corps of Prince Józef Poniatowski and Zajączek had halted near Kamionna, that an army of 5,000 had encamped at a distance of 2 miles from Błonie which might threaten Łowicz or Sochaczew, and that another detachment of Polish troops, numbering 3,000-4,000 soldiers, had pitched camp, as Jews reported, near Nadarzyn. All these rumours so affected Schwerin that he still held Frankenberg back from attacking Kamionna, ordering him to await further events, while he himself, in order to preserve for the king the whole of his acquisitions, saw no other way out than to withdraw his troops from the Sandomierz and Kraków lands or to obtain reinforcement of the army by fresh relief forces. On the strength of new royal instructions he tried to operate as circumstances required, but this too was not easy. Communications, increasingly obstructed, made proper liaison with the separate corps impossible. With Berlin there was a connection only by the road into Silesia through Częstochowa, and with the centre of Greater Poland it was at times completely broken. The commanders of the individual corps in this sector, receiving orders late because of the interruptions in communications, and unable themselves to send reports in good time, acted on their own and therefore rather chaotically[^206].
