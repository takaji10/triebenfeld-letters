The rising in Greater Poland, which could not be so easily crushed by force of arms, which checked the plans of the Prussian king and hampered the movements of his troops, caused the Prussian king to try to master the situation not only by arms but also by means of proclamations, now threatening the Greater Poles, now, for a change, containing gentler turns of phrase.

Accordingly a patent[^321] appeared, dated 24 September at Wrocław, issued during the stay there of Frederick William II on his way to Berlin, in which this monarch stated "with just displeasure" (mit gerechtem Unwillen) that in carrying out the intention of bringing happiness to Greater Poland, from the moment of the occupation of this province, "various restless heads " had stood in the way. For the breaking of the "sacred bonds of fidelity and obedience" the patent threatened the participants in the insurrection with imprisonment and even death, and the nobility moreover with the loss of noble status and of estates[^322] that were to fall to the share of "faithful and deserving vassals". Finally the king expressed regret for those of his South Prussian subjects who, in fear for their lives and property, had taken their places in the ranks of the "troublemakers", causing, together with them, alarm among the peaceful inhabitants of the province; he refuted the rumours of persecution of Catholics and of an increase in taxes, and, wishing to win over the peasant estate, he abolished the tax on the so-called "Musikzettli"[^a106].
