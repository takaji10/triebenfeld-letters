It promised all participants in the uprising release "from every and any penalty, whether of life or of body, or of confiscation of estates and other penalties on estates", as well as complete amnesty, "every pardon and remission", if within a period of two weeks from the date of publication of the patent they laid down their arms and returned to their homes; those who would not comply with the appeal were threatened by the government with severe penalties[^460]. The Landrat of the Bydgoszcz district, Twardowski, who had been an active insurgent at the time of Dąbrowski's expedition, placed his trust in this appeal, but was nevertheless arrested on 25 November 1794[^461]. For in the meantime the king had issued to the West Prussian commissions an order to mete out punishments to the "rebels" with complete ruthlessness[^462]. On 10 December 1794 a proclamation appeared concerning the treatment of the insurgents of South Prussia. It ordered the release of Polish prisoners. Former officers were to give a written undertaking that they would not enter service in any army that might take the field against Prussia. The common soldiers were to be incorporated in part into the Prussian army, or else sent out into the province as settlers. The nobility and clergy who had taken part in the uprising were threatened by the appeal with the loss of their estates and dignities if they did not present themselves before the appropriate authorities by 1 January 1795; the principal "rebels" and leaders of the insurrection it ordered to be arrested and delivered for interrogation before the commissions in Wrocław, Głogów or Brzeg[^463]. A publicandum (public notice) of 10 February 1795 extended the term for the appearance of the insurgents from South Prussia to 1 June 1795, threatening otherwise punishments as before.
