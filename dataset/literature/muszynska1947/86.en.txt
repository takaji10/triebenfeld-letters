After this speech all proceeded to the parish church, and only in one of the rooms did the arrested men remain, under the guard of 8 soldiers. In the church the citizens swore loyalty to the Commonwealth, after which Wybicki went once more to the board of the Chamber, where in the presence of the officials he called the Prussian king a "tyrant". At that moment the arrested councillor Broskovius approached him, asking him to release his countrymen. Wybicki, in his excitement, interrupted him and called him an "unreasonable man, a slave who does not perceive the yoke of his bondage". Broskovius, however, did not give up and presented his request for the release of the arrested men a second time. This time Wybicki heard him out, but soon flared up again and called Székely a brigand, threatening that, should the Prussian king wish to hang any of the imprisoned Poles, ten Prussian officials would immediately meet the same fate[^255].

When everything had calmed down, Dąbrowski visited the wounded Colonel Székely. Székely feared that the Poles would take revenge on him, but Dąbrowski assured him that he could be at ease and that he would endeavour "to sweeten his lot as much as possible". At the wounded man's request doctors and a priest were sent to him, and Dąbrowski gave him money. Other Poles came to visit him as well[^256], among them Mniewski, "a general worthy in every respect of admiration", in whose house Székely had formerly been a guest, and whom during the insurrection he had robbed "first of all".
