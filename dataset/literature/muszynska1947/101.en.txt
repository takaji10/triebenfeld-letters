Lenartowicz, head of the rising in the Sieradz region, soon joined Korycki, and under Stokowski's supreme command expeditions were mounted to the neighbouring small towns of Wieruszów and Bolesławice[^305] and to the Opatów estates. Psarski, marching with the troops, set up commissions for civil matters in the several localities, took the oath of fidelity, and on the estates of Count Maltzahn ordered the labour dues to be reduced by half; he ordered the supply of recruits and provisions. It was also intended to extend the rising to the Silesian border towns, such as Byczyna [Pitschen], Rychtal [Reichthal], Międzybórz [Neumittelwalde], Wołczyn [Konstadt], Oleśnica, Bralin, Namysłów and Syców [Groß Wartenberg], and to send appropriate summonses there from the camp at Węglowice. The owner of Ligota, a village lying on the border, Wiewiórski, received these appeals, but forwarded them, still unopened, to the Landrat (district commissioner) Teichmann in Wartenberg. The armed movement in the neighbourhood of Wieluń and Ostrzeszów could not, however, assume any greater proportions, since many inhabitants of that land did not believe from the outset in its success, and the Jews were slow in supplying arms and ammunition. It even happened that the mayor of Wieluń, Olszanowski, taking advantage of the insurgents' having left the town, ordered the scythemen keeping guard in their absence to go home, and instructed the town servants to destroy their weapons[^306].

In another part of old Greater Poland, in Dobrzyń Land, the fortunes of the rising likewise took an unfavourable turn, because the citizens there lacked perseverance in the undertaking once begun. Around 10 September the rifles and uniforms once taken were returned to the Prussian post at Rypin and the captured hussars were released, together with Lieutenant Branky.
