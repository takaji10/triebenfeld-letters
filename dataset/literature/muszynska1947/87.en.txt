Thereupon Székely declared that he wished to speak with Dąbrowski and Mniewski himself, and when all had left he told them that his baggage was at Grudziądz and advised that the Poles should not advance further, because a corps was marching from Pomerania and Brandenburg that was to have joined him. As a token of friendship he gave Dąbrowski 6 riding horses, which the latter never claimed. The doctor who was called did not predict a long life for the wounded man, and indeed four days after the assault Székely died and was buried with military honours[^257].

The capture of Bydgoszcz made a great impression throughout Prussia. Berlin was ashamed of the defeat and at the same time anxious for the fate of the seized provinces[^258]. In Poznań people simply would not believe Schwarz, the eyewitness of the Bydgoszcz events[^259]. He himself relates that when, on 6 October at noon, the day after his arrival in Poznań, he stood before General Elsner, who was then in command in the city, the latter at first took the news of Székely's disaster and the capture of Bydgoszcz by the Poles for a fable, until towards evening a rider who had come from Dąbrowski's corps confirmed it. At the court of the Prussian monarch there now arose a fear for the security of West Prussia. It was thought, namely, that in that whole region there were too few troops to be able to oppose the Poles effectively. The garrison of Toruń under Colonel Hundt could be reinforced by only seven more companies, and the state of the city's entrenchments left much to be desired. Frederick William was therefore ready, in the last resort, to heed the advice of General Favrat and, to save Toruń, to fold up the front on the Narew and thus reinforce the threatened fortress with troops.
