In the Gniezno voivodeship, in the part that came under Prussian rule in 1793[^125], the most zealous propagators of the idea of insurrection were Józef Lipski, lord of Czerniejewo[^126], and the Rogoziński brothers. The nobility from the neighbourhood of Gniezno declared for the fight against the Prussians, and succeeded in winning over even the peasants of that area and part of the burghers of Gniezno. On 22 August the insurgents arrived at the assembly point at Gniezno and the next day attacked the Prussian garrison, numbering about 210 men, and disarmed it. At the same time the Prussian emblems were removed from the official buildings, after which the citizens assembled in the town hall swore the insurrection act. Seated on the commission chosen for civil and criminal matters were: the castellan Walenty Godzimirski, elected chairman against his will and in the end removed as suspect; Korytowski, who succeeded him in that office; Józef Jaraczewski; the chamberlain Piotr Kaliszowski; the canons Loga, Skiwski and Murzynowski (the last soon expelled); Colonel Twardowski; the Protestant clergymen Bothe, Schocken, Koch and Hanke; the burghers Gostomski and Kłosowski; and several peasants[^127]. The town hall became the seat of the commission, from which appeals went out over the whole district calling the population to arms. At this call peasants armed with scythes and pikes began to stream in from all sides. The monastery of Wągrowiec [Wongrowitz] alone supplied 100 peasants. Near the village of Winiary[^128] they set up a camp[^129], where the sense of discipline was instilled in the masses with great diligence. Unfortunately, however, right at the very start, the camp did not escape quarrels. Lipski, the commander of the armed force, having fallen out with the Rogozińskis, all but cut down one of the brothers.
