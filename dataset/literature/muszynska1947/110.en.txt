# Muszyńska-Zygmańska (1947), p. 110
# translation by Claude (machine translation, checked and corrected in a bilingual QA pass, 2026)

In order not to allow despair to take hold, the Supreme National Council issued appeals, encouraging the citizens to further strenuous action. On 16 October it addressed a separate proclamation to the Greater Poles, calling upon them not to bury "the hopes of the Fatherland through the loss of a man, albeit a most estimable one, whose fate cannot and ought not to determine the fate of the Fatherland". On the same day a letter also appeared, directed to the citizens of the Sieradz voivodeship and Wieluń Land, urging them to apply themselves to the rising and to support the work begun "most valiantly and most eagerly". The Supreme National Council appointed for them a new plenipotentiary in the person of citizen Okołów, because the plenipotentiary Józef Wybicki "is not in a position to fulfil the duties entrusted to him with regard to the Sieradz voivodeship and Wieluń Land"[^333].

These efforts to sustain the spirit of the nation were, however, in vain. Most Poles already regarded the defeat of 10 October as the end of the insurrection and all further exertion as pointless[^334]. For the catastrophe of Maciejowice had checked "the impetus of the revolution"[^335].

Dąbrowski, having learned on 14 October, on his return from the march on Toruń, of the defeat at Maciejowice, as well as of the severing of communication with Warsaw and of the planned destruction of his division by the Prussian forces, considered it necessary, without waiting for an order[^336], to leave Bydgoszcz and return to Warsaw. He began the preparations for the march back at once, and on the following day set out from Bydgoszcz with the main corps in the direction of Gniewkowo. He took with him more than 400 prisoners, among them also the arrested Prussian officials: the councillor Grabowski, Broskovius, the director of posts Tschepius and the mayor Radzibor[^337].
