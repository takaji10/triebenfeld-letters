For a long time, until as late as 7 December, the partisan Ksawery Łukomski harassed the Prussians. With a detachment that by some accounts numbered 5 or 8 men, by others 30, he ranged over the neighbourhood of Książ [Xions], Środa [Schroda] and Zaniemyśl. He was marked, moreover, by great recklessness. The Prussians also set a reward of 50 ducats on his head. On 7 December Łukomski was captured and handed over to the investigative commission in Poznań[^422].

The events described here, fragmentary as they are, give a picture of the last insurgent struggles in Greater Poland. After the fall of the uprising in the main theatre of the fighting, further efforts by the Greater Poles could not avail much; they were not able to save the national cause. It is true that when the Polish army was making its last march towards the Pilica, the Greater Poland insurgents still sustained themselves with the hope that the armed movement might revive once more, if the soldiers leaving Warsaw were to set foot on their soil[^423]. This hope was not groundless. Dąbrowski, as we know, long insisted on his plan of transferring the war to Greater Poland. But from the moment the Polish troops fell into disarray and the Greater Poles could expect rescue from nowhere, they finally had to regard the continuation of the struggle as pointless.
