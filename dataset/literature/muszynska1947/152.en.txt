For the administration of justice he had to maintain a justiciary (Justitiar), and he exercised police authority by means of the village head (sołtys; German Schulze); he appointed these officials after consultation with the Landrat. The justiciary and the village head sowed discord between the lord and his subjects[^507].

The royal towns lost the right to elect their officials. In the private towns in the territory of South Prussia the government did, it is true, assure the hereditary owners and the commoners, by a patent of 18 April 1795, the right to nominate or elect the town councils, but it reserved to itself the appointment of the mayor. It controlled the municipal revenues, devised ways of repaying debts, and so on[^508].

The Prussian government paid close attention to the Jews. In the annexed lands only those Jews could live who had settled there in the time of the Commonwealth; those who arrived later had to leave the country. A Jew entitled to reside in the country possessed a so-called letter of protection (Schutzbrief), which meant the same as a passport. The district offices imposed German surnames on the Jews, often ridiculous ones. Without the authorisation of the Chamber, Jews were forbidden to change their place of residence and their occupation[^509].

In organising social relations the Prussian government was guided not by the good of the inhabitants but by the aims of the state. It made officials of the priests. The nobility it now favoured, now extended police control over. In order to curry favour with the nobility, it undertook no improvement of peasant conditions and did not abolish labour dues, despite announcements to that effect[^510].
