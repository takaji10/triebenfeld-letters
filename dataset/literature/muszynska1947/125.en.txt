# Muszyńska-Zygmańska (1947), p. 125
# translation by Claude (machine translation, checked and corrected in a bilingual QA pass, 2026)

General Kleist. General Kleist[^390] asked "most humbly" on what terms Dąbrowski would be willing to "enter and join the army of His Prussian Majesty the King"[^391] together with his regular troops. Dąbrowski read the letter and translated it for the Commander in the presence of all the generals. There was much dispute over the reply, until at last it was decided to give an evasive answer, although Dąbrowski himself was of the opinion that matters should have been handled otherwise. He considered that in so grave a position, when there was no hope left, when Russian and Austrian troops surrounded the insurgents on all sides, and when the soldiers, "the infantry above all, for a thousand reasons neither wished nor was able to fight", Kleist ought to be answered with points: 1) a general amnesty in Greater Poland; 2) the placing of all officers in the Prussian army according to their commissions; 3) payment of 4 years' salary to officers unwilling to serve. The Greater Poles, Dąbrowski writes in his memoir on the end of the insurrection, liked the proposal, but Wawrzecki and Zakrzewski opposed it. Dąbrowski did not press the matter, yet "the greater part of the Greater Poles" begged him not to abandon the proposal. He therefore put it forward again, and, so that it should not be supposed that he was seeking "a decent place of maintenance" for himself, he assured them "on his honour" that he would take his dismissal. He hoped that the Greater Poles would not refuse him 6,000 Polish zlotys a year until such time as he might again serve the country. This time the declaration was received fairly well; nevertheless the Commander had weighty reasons for "not approving Dąbrowski's opinion"[^392]
