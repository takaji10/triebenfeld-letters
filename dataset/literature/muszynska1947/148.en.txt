We do not know how large a sum the insurgents of West Prussia had to pay by way of penalty. In any case it must have been small, since it did not suffice even for repairing the damage caused in that area by the insurrection. For that reason the authorities there made representations to the king that he should assign to West Prussia at least a part of the sum collected in South Prussia. The representations, however, foundered on the resistance of Minister Hoym. By virtue of a royal rescript of 17 October 1796 the injured parties of West Prussia could only claim compensation for damages from those who had caused them. In this way the matter was referred to private proceedings. Since, however, the guilt of the defendants often could not be proved, and many of them were abroad or were altogether poor, even by this route some compensation could be obtained only in a few cases[^496]. The small sum that came in from the insurgents in West Prussia is explained, in general, by the fact that the area affected by the revolt was small and was confined chiefly to certain parts of the Netze District. Of 138 accused, only 63 inhabitants were found guilty. Of these, 26 came from West Prussia proper, the rest from the Netze District. What fine was imposed on these 63 is not known, since no closer data are available[^497].

The punishment of the insurgents, which was intended to deter the Greater Poles from starting revolts in the future and to teach them obedience, evidently did not produce that effect, since the Prussian king in the patent of 9 August 1796 warned against a new uprising.
