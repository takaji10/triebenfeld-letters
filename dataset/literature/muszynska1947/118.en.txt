# Muszyńska-Zygmańska (1947), p. 118
# translation by Claude (machine translation, checked and corrected in a bilingual QA pass, 2026)

There was a period when, in view of the defeats on the other theatres of the insurrectionary struggle, favourable news of the movements of his corps was the one thing that cheered the hearts of the inhabitants of the capital and sustained faith in the future of Poland among the army and at those foreign courts that were well disposed towards the Polish nation. It was clear that he could not venture upon the destruction of an enemy who surpassed him in every respect, and that he was often compelled to avoid battle and confine himself rather to guerrilla warfare[^363]; yet he absorbed all the enemy's forces and, together with the Greater Poland insurgents, managed to bear almost the entire burden of the war west of the Vistula. When he could no longer expect further successes owing to the fatal turn of events on the main theatre of war, he set out on the return march and withdrew successfully with his whole corps to Warsaw, although the entire effort of the Prussian commander was directed against him, and the king repeatedly sent him orders to destroy the Greater Poland division by every means at his disposal[^364]. Dąbrowski's expedition also had considerable significance abroad. It undermined the position of the Prussian king in the partition negotiations, deprived him of part of the land in the Sandomierz and Kraków regions, discouraged him altogether from waging a simultaneous war in the west, showed the world that the famous army of Frederick the Great had incompetent commanders, and proved that "above the most seasoned routine there always prevails a spirit of civic self-sacrifice and zeal for a good cause[^365]".
