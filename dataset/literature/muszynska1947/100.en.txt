The insurgents, alarmed by the shot, at once rushed in pursuit of the fugitive, but at the sight of the remaining hussars turned back. Three days later this same band of insurgents seized the Prussian mail sent out from Piła[^302].

In the districts of Wieluń and Ostrzeszów the armed movement, begun successfully in the third decade of August, weakened considerably in the course of September, although there were no Prussian troops nearby. Michał Jerlicz, who was to form an armed force, returned home to the region of Działoszyn. Nor did the head of the rising, Colonel Stokowski, hold out, and he soon disbanded his detachment for want of any means of feeding it. It was he, writes Kierzkowski in his memoir[^303], who, "standing with 500 insurgent horse", at the moment when the Prussian king was travelling by post from Warsaw through Wieluń towards Silesia, "played at cards, and the insurgents slept well". When he later learned of the passage of so important a person, "it grieved him greatly that, when he could have seized the king, he had neglected the opportunity. He threw his cap on the ground, but that was of no further use". Of the other organisers of the rising in this district, Psarski, so zealous at the outset, returned to his estate of Bobrownik[^304]. After some time the rising gathered strength again. At Bobrownik deliberations were held on how to restore discipline in the loosening insurgent ranks, and the dignity of deputy commander was entrusted to an old officer of the Polish army, Korycki. Psarski went to Ostrzeszów and there kindled anew among the citizens zeal for the national cause, and ordered Stokowski to justify himself for his conduct.
