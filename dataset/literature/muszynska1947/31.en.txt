General Mokronowski became commandant of the city. On 19 April the inhabitants of the Duchy of Mazovia, together with the king, declared their accession to the uprising. The example of Warsaw was soon followed by Vilnius, which, on the night of 22 to 23 April, under the leadership of Jasiński, removed the Russian garrison from the city and created its own insurgent authorities[^78].

Only Greater Poland did not stir. In 1793, when the secret societies arose, this region too was embraced by the conspiracy. Among the agitators active there were Gliszczyński, and also Wybicki, who found fertile ground for his work among the citizenry of Poznań, Gniezno and Kalisz. After all, both the chief leader of the conspirators, Ignacy Działyński, and President Zakrzewski were natives of Greater Poland. Nevertheless the insurrection was to break out there later than elsewhere, although the inhabitants of that land declared their solidarity with the Act of Kraków and felt themselves bound to fight in defence of the fatherland. What held them back from active action was the scattering of Prussian troops about the country, and above all Kościuszko's policy towards his western neighbour[^79].

At first Kościuszko, as the future Commander, was undoubtedly resolved to act against the Prussians, when in mid-September 1793 he is said to have spoken these words to the conspirators: "I myself shall strike at Piotrków, at Möllendorff, with the troops that are between Warsaw and Kraków, and having cleared Greater Poland I shall turn towards Warsaw[^80]...".
