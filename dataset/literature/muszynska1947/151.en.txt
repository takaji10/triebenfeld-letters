# translation by Claude (machine translation, checked and corrected in a bilingual QA pass, 2026)

For Prussian legislation, the so-called Landrecht, was introduced[^503].

The Greater Poles were removed from participation in public affairs; the Prussian government extended over them the tutelage of the German bureaucracy. Special regulations concerning the clergy, the nobility, the peasants, the burghers and the Jews governed the manner of ruling the population by means of the administrative organs[^504]. The clergy was placed wholly under the authority of the state. Through its commissioners the Chamber directed the elections of bishops, prelates and the superiors of religious congregations; from priests it received the oath of allegiance as subjects. In civil and criminal matters the clergy were subject to the jurisdiction of the Regierung. Only matters of a purely ecclesiastical nature belonged to the competence of the ecclesiastical courts. In 1796 the ecclesiastical estates were seized for the benefit of the treasury. For the maintenance of the clergy the treasury set aside a part of the revenues from the church properties it had taken and the interest on the capital belonging to ecclesiastical corporations and lent out privately; it allowed the clergy to collect fees for religious services according to an official tariff[^506].

The nobility retained the privilege of owning landed estates, but with certain restrictions. Those among the nobility who had held no landed property at the time of the fall of the Commonwealth could now acquire it only after obtaining the incolat (indigenat, the right of a native noble to hold land). If anyone who had neither any right to landed property nor the incolat wished to acquire an estate, he had first to secure a special permission from the Prussian king. Inheritance, bequest by will and gift did not confer the right to hold landed estates without the incolat or royal permission.
