Only on 29 October, when Suvorov had already begun to bombard Praga, did an order come that the corps of Dąbrowski and Giedroyć should move towards Rawa in order to observe the Prussians. There they stood idle for several days[^380].

Meanwhile, on 4 November, Suvorov took the entrenchments of Praga after a brief attack; they had been thrown up without any plan and served only for a reflexive defence, and he inflicted a bloody slaughter on the inhabitants of Praga. The capital, fearing a similar fate, surrendered on 6 November, with the proviso that troops who did not lay down their arms might leave it. The Supreme National Council dissolved itself. On 9 November the victorious Suvorov already rode ceremonially into Warsaw[^381].

After the fall of the capital, complete disorder reigned in the army. The soldiers, abandoned by their commanders, threw down their arms in crowds, heedless of the fate of the country or of their comrades; they wished only to save themselves. Dąbrowski alone in this general confusion did not lose his head. With his corps, not yet touched by the ferment, he began a march to the south. His intention was "to hold out in the Sandomierz and Kraków regions, and from there to extend operations into the Sieradz region and farther into Greater Poland[^382]". He also spun plans that, given favourable circumstances, he might perhaps realise his cherished idea of breaking through to France by way of Silesia, Moravia, Bohemia, Bavaria and Swabia, "straight to Landau, or else to Switzerland towards the Rhine"[^383]. During the march, however, "the misfortune of Praga and Warsaw"[^384] made itself felt in his ranks as well. When he encamped at Gostomia, "generals, officers and soldiers from both places"[^385] began to arrive, bringing fear and dread with them.
