A conspirator who informed could expect a mitigation of his penalty, and one who took no part in it, a reward for denunciation. The patent assured informers of discretion and threatened with punishment those who knowingly made false accusations[^501].

Simultaneously with the punishment of the former insurgents the Prussian government set about improving anew the whole administrative and judicial apparatus introduced in Greater Poland before the uprising, introduced further innovations, and spared no effort to turn the seized lands into a Prussian province.

On 5 January 1795 the South Prussian courts resumed their interrupted activity[^502]. In the judiciary the German language came to reign supreme, and Prussian procedure was introduced. This caused great discontent among Polish citizens, and the bolder among them even addressed the king on the matter. As a result of their efforts a publicandum appeared, dated 16 November 1795, intended to adapt judicial proceedings "as far as possible to the character and way of thinking of the nation". German was not, however, removed from the judiciary; it was merely promised that extracts from the general Prussian law (das allgemeine Landrecht, the General Land Law for the Prussian States) and the procedure would be published in Polish. The Regierungen were instructed to translate judgments and summonses into Polish, in whole or in abridged form. In 1797 Polish laws lost their binding force.
