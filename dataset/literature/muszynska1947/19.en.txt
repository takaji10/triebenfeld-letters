Polish-Prussian alliance of 1790, and of several officers, arrived in Poznań. From there he went to Kościan [Kosten], then to Wschowa, Rawicz [Rawitsch], Kalisz, Płock, Łowicz and Piotrków. Everywhere he was warmly welcomed by Jews and some Poles, although few in number. The Primate, Michał Poniatowski, came from Warsaw to Łowicz to greet the king, and the Radziwiłł princely couple gave a magnificent ball in honour of the Prussian monarch at Nieborów. Having stayed on Polish lands until the end of October, Frederick William returned to Berlin on 10 November[^48]. The fruit of this journey was the conviction that the Greater Poles were not glad to have passed under Prussian rule, whatever Lucchesini vainly tried to make the king believe. For the generality of the inhabitants did not even come out to meet their new master[^49].

Nor is it surprising that this was so. In those few months of rule the Prussians had already made themselves felt by the Greater Poles. Slowly but systematically they uprooted everything that breathed of the Polish past and stamped the Prussian imprint on every sphere of life. Efforts were made to give the offices a Prussian character. Poles were removed from them. Only out of necessity, in order to communicate with a population that did not understand German, was the office of Landrat[^50] entrusted to Poles, together with the dignity of one of the chamber councillors. These officials, however, were forbidden to wear Polish dress, lest they recall the bygone order of things. The Prussian government extended its supervision over the Catholic clergy, the confraternities and the guilds, it checked the activities of the town councils, it sent in colonists, and it took the youth of Greater Poland as recruits. Deepening the distinctions of estate among the inhabitants, it permitted by the edict of 24 July 1793 only the nobility to own estates.
