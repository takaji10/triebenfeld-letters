Two days after Kościuszko's by now official move against the Prussians, on 12 June, there appeared an appeal of the Supreme National Council "to the citizens of the Greater Poland province" calling for a rising. Depicting emphatically the falsehood, the hypocrisy and the unparalleled greed of the Prussian king, it declared the Prussian annexations "unlawful" and the voivodeships and lands of the Greater Poland province "indivisible parts of the Commonwealth"; it called traitors to the fatherland, and resolved to punish, those "who would insinuate any subjection to the unlawful power of appropriators and invaders"[^97].

Yet it was not then that the "revolt" flared up in Greater Poland; garrisons of Prussian troops that were still fairly strong stood in its way. Only later, after this region had been stripped of Prussian troops, which were used partly for the siege of Warsaw and partly on the Rhine, did the appeals of the Supreme National Council turn into deed[^98].
