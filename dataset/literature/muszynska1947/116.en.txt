19 October, did not come about. On that day General Pollitz did reach Słupca by way of Łęczyca, Koło and Kazimierz, but waited there in vain for the arrival of Major-General Schwerin, who seemed to be acting contrary to orders. The latter was still in Poznań, where on 17 October news of the defeat at Maciejowice was brought to him also by Lt. von Heiligenstadt[^355], and only on 21 October, on the express order of his monarch, did he leave the city, to join General Pollitz at last, four days later[^356], at Słupca. The reason for his long stay in the capital of Greater Poland was fear of the might of an enemy standing nearby, which in reality was only Bielamowski's elusive raiding party[^357].

Since it was impossible to surround Dąbrowski's corps, there remained now only the possibility of fighting an open battle with the enemy, but to this way of extricating himself from an awkward situation, which only incompetent commanders resorted to, Lieutenant-General Schwerin would not consent. After all, he knew neither the enemy's manner of fighting nor his strength, supposing it to amount to about 10,000 regular soldiers. In such conditions he feared a defeat, which would draw after it a strengthening of the insurgent movement, and perhaps even the loss of Greater Poland. For that reason, when on 23 October, at 5 o'clock in the afternoon, General Klinkowström appeared at Kocierzów with the news of the attack on Sochaczew and of Dąbrowski's possible simultaneous crossing, Schwerin pretended not to believe it, or perhaps really did not believe it, "as one long refuses to believe in misfortune"[^358]. He did set out at last from Kocierzów towards Mistrzewice, and, having joined Klinkowström's corps at Sochaczew, arrived on the spot at 8 o'clock in the morning of the 24th.
