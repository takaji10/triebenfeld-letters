Kleist. Dąbrowski, however, no longer had any "inclination" to surrender his corps of regular troops to the Prussians; he wished only to treat on behalf of the "unfortunate citizens of Greater Poland". Colonel Gorzeński of the Greater Poland rising, who knew German, was therefore appointed to him, to be a witness at the talks with the Prussians; but the meeting did not take place. Meanwhile a courier arrived with word from King Stanisław August[^393] that General Suvorov was "bloodily" crushing those who would not comply with his demands, and had ordered a warning that the rest should not suffer a like misfortune. The last attempt at an understanding with Dąbrowski, which the Prussians made during the march of the Polish troops towards Radoszyce, likewise came to nothing, because "it had already been decided not to engage with the Prussians"[^394]. Thus the securing of the fate of the Greater Poles, who above all could be called "rebels" and who for that reason were threatened with repression by the Prussian government, was not accomplished[^395].

Faithful to the national cause to the last, the Greater Poland insurgents accompanying Dąbrowski did not desert their ranks. Towards the end, another 1,000 horsemen from the Sieradz and Wieluń risings joined, and these, together with the other Greater Poland risings, under the command of General Skórzewski, and with the brigades of Dąbrowski and Madaliński, represented, after the dispersal of all the regiments that had joined Dąbrowski on the way, the Polish army by themselves[^396]. They saw how Dąbrowski, after the unfavourable reply of Suvorov brought by the dispatched Gorzeński, in utmost despair, not knowing what to do next, wished to reach the vicinity of the Galician border, burn the weapons there, and disband Madaliński and his own brigade. They were witnesses when on 18 November General Denisov arrived at Radoszyce
