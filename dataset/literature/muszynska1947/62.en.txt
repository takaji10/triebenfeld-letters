After the capitulation of Vilnius [Wilna] on 11 August, the Lithuanian army resisted the Russian troops with difficulty and began to fall back toward Grodno and Warsaw[^163]. Only the events in Greater Poland were a brighter page of the uprising, although even there the situation was now becoming less easy.

The Prussian detachments under Col. Székely and Major-General Schwerin, which had been withdrawn from before Warsaw to Greater Poland while the siege was still in progress, were stamping out the insurgents there; and as soon as the siege was raised General Elsner was dispatched against the rebellious provinces and went to the left bank of the Warta, General Frankenberg toward the Bzura. The greater part of the Prussian forces, which until 9 September remained at Raszyn, then began a further retreat, drawing close to the borders of Greater Poland[^164].

There, of the Prussian commanders, the most dangerous was Székely.

He set out from the camp before Warsaw on 29 August at the head of Hinrichs's rifle battalion and the squadrons of Trenck, Czettritz and Wirtemberg, by the road through Sochaczew, in order to rout the insurgents gathering near Gniezno. He did not, however, go in the intended direction, but on hearing of the notorious events in Kuyavia changed his route and made for Włocławek. Having reached it, he met with considerable resistance from the insurgents, from whom he captured 4 guns, but he could accomplish no more. For 600 armed peasants and 200 horsemen were moving west to join the other Greater Poland risings. On 8 September Székely sent detachments of cavalry after them, while he himself remained with the rest of the corps in Włocławek.
