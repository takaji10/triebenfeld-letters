[^257]: Skałkowski, Mon. IV, 201, 223; ibid., 135, 136; Hübner, 89, 92, 94; Dworzaczek, 149—152. Jan Fryderyk Székely, Hungarian by birth and "a true spawn of the Huns", as Wybicki calls him in his memoir, had a reputation as an outstanding commander of light cavalry and at the same time as a brute, a cruel extortioner and a persecutor of the weak. He entered the Prussian army in 1758 during the Seven Years' War, later took part in the War of the Bavarian Succession, like Dąbrowski (1779), and in 1792, already with the rank of colonel, which he had earned 2 years before, fought on the Rhine. When the insurrection broke out in Poland, Székely was already over 50 years old. Sent by the king to crush the Greater Poland insurgents, he proceeded with the utmost severity. He was even a terror to the German population in the Netze District. After the defeat at Bydgoszcz attempts were made to blame Székely for the loss, whereas the commandant of Poznań, v. Diether, was also at fault, who should have attacked the rear of Dąbrowski's forces at the moment when he became convinced that Dąbrowski would not strike at Poznań but was heading for the Noteć. It is Székely's fault, on the other hand, that before the start of the battle that was to decide his fate he did not, despite the advice of the old Colonel Witte, occupy the hill dominating the town. In Toruń Székely set up a special commission under the chairmanship of the judicial councillor Kühze, which was to carry out systematically the confiscation of the insurgents' estates.

[^373]: Skałkowski, Mon. IV, 150, 151, 372; cf. Treskow, 323—325. Wilhelm, Fryderyk, Karol, Count Schwerin, was born on 11 December 1739 in Berlin. In 1753 he entered the Prussian army, serving in the regiment of his paternal uncle. At the outbreak of the Seven Years' War in 1756 he was adjutant to General Winterfeld, and after the latter's death (1757), adjutant to the king. In 1758 he was wounded at Zorndorf and taken prisoner by the Russians. There he managed to win over the Grand Duke Peter, and in 1760, exchanged for a Russian prisoner, he was sent back to Prussia. After the conclusion of peace at Hubertusburg in 1763 he returned to the army, later became a lieutenant-general, commander of an infantry regiment for the district of West Prussia, and governor of Toruń (Allgemeine deutsche Biographie (Leipzig 1891, vol. 33, p. 427)). Of the supreme command of the Prussian forces on the left bank of the Vistula, entrusted to him in mid-September 1794, he acquitted himself as badly as possible. According to the opinion of Prussian military men (Treskow, 282 ff.; Boyen, 79 ff.; Knoll, 156 ff.), Schwerin ought, immediately after taking over the command, to have concentrated his forces and attacked the opponent standing between him and Warsaw. The Polish troops equalled the Prussian neither in numbers nor in quality. During the attack mentioned above it was necessary for Generals Lattorff and Lichnowski to occupy Kraków and Sandomierz and to guard the regions of Piotrków and Łowicz against intrusion by the Greater Poland insurgents, while the corps of Elsner, of Schwerin the Younger and of Székely, joined together, were to strike at Dąbrowski's division. Later still, after the defeat at Maciejowice, when Suvorov was approaching Warsaw, Schwerin could have attacked the Polish troops stationed before his front and beaten them separately. Even if he had merely succeeded in pushing the opponent back towards Warsaw, he would already have gained freedom of action, and then he could have turned with the greater part of his army against Dąbrowski. But Schwerin remained indecisive even then, frittering away his forces from day to day, detaching more and more units against the insurgents; in the end he set out with 5 battalions to destroy the returning Greater Poland column, but as a result of false combinations he chose the wrong road. He allowed Dąbrowski's troops, together with a considerable baggage train, to cross the Bzura in peace, and afterwards took no step to support the Russians at Warsaw. He lacked, as indeed throughout the whole campaign, a clear judgment of events and decisiveness. He had set against him a very able opponent in the person of Dąbrowski, and by that measure he assessed the rest of the Polish army and its commanders (Skałkowski, Mon. IV, 151). For all the oversights and errors committed in his responsible post he answered with the loss of his command. The king took it from him under the pretext of weakened health and sent him back to his former governorship at Toruń. Schwerin, being certain that an injustice had befallen him, asked for an inquiry to be opened. His demand was granted. The inquiry was conducted by Lieutenant-General Brüneck at Königsberg. On 9 May 1795 a court martial assembled in Berlin under the presidency of General Rohdich and pronounced a sentence by virtue of which Schwerin was condemned to lose his regiment, to resign the governorship of Toruń, to one year's detention in a fortress, and to repay the costs of the investigations. He served the sentence at Grudziądz. After the death of Frederick William II he made efforts to return to the Prussian army, but the new king, Frederick William III, opposed this; he was permitted only to enter foreign service. On 17 August 1802, during a journey to Hamburg, Schwerin died. In his own justification he wrote "Wahre u. mit Aktenstücke belegte Darstellung der Veranlassung auf welche ich nach 43 Dienstjahren aus dem Königlichen Preuss. Militärdienste entlassen worden bin" (Leipzig 1798). The title of the second edition (Leipzig 1799) read: "Muster für Stabsoffiziere Rapporte zu machen". In the above treatise Schwerin accused Lieutenant-General Klinkowström as the one who in October 1794 had allowed Dąbrowski's division to slip away across the Bzura (Allgemeine Biographie. vol. 33, 428). Klinkowström rejected the charges made against him by Schwerin in the work: "Berichtigungen einiger Angaben, welche in dem vom General-Lieutenant Grafen v. Schwerin herausgegebenen Buche... enthalten sind" (Berlin 1799). The blame that he attributed to Schwerin during the campaign of 1794 he set out in points: 1) the unnecessary drawing in of General Lattorff's corps from the Sandomierz region and the consequent loss of that province; 2) the pointless formation of the corps of Pollitz and Ledivary and their unsuitable use for holding back the opponent in West Prussia; 3) remaining idle in the camp at Chrzonowice, on the strength of false assumptions, until 20 October 1794; 4) the undertaking on 22 October of a march from Łowicz to Kiernozia and Osmalin that was pointless in the highest degree, as a result of which the enemy was able to cross the Bzura with impunity; 5) the failure to undertake, jointly with the Russian troops, an offensive against Warsaw, contrary to the royal orders and to the very nature of things, and instead remaining idle at Łowicz from 26 October until 10 November (according to Klinkowström, Berichtigungen.... 11, 12).
