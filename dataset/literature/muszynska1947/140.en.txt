1795, and Prussia gained a free hand to settle the matter of its acquisitions in Poland[^446]. Its resistance was, however, soon broken by the Empress. Ostermann declared to Count Tauentzien that the Polish question must be brought to an end, and that if Prussia intended to attack Austria, the Empress was determined to support the latter with all her strength, while the emperor would make peace with France and himself take the field against Prussia[^447]. The threat had its effect. On 3 September 1795 Tauentzien held the first joint conference with the representatives of Russia and Austria, Markov and Cobenzl, and on 24 October the partition treaty between Prussia and Russia, with the participation of Austria, was concluded, in which Prussia agreed to the provisions of the convention of 3 January 1795[^448].

The Prusso-Russian partition convention was supplemented by an additional one of 26 January 1797, accepted by Austria. A separate and secret article of this convention erased from memory for all time the name of the Polish Commonwealth (Rzeczpospolita Polska)[^449].

Of the lands which Prussia received in the last partition, the area on the left bank of the Vistula with Warsaw was incorporated into South Prussia. The area on the right bank of the Vistula and north of the Bug formed, together with the Płock voivodeship and the small piece of the Warsaw voivodeship which Prussia had gained in 1793, New East Prussia [Neuostpreußen]; and part of the new lands in the south, namely the Duchy of Siewierz and a piece of the Kraków voivodeship, formed New Silesia [Neuschlesien]. South Prussia was in time divided into the departments of Poznań, Kalisz and Warsaw; New East Prussia into those of Płock and Białystok[^450].
