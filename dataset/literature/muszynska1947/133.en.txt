Deprived now of guiding aims, the insurgents, insofar as they had not laid down their arms, acted on their own account. The Prussian offices in small towns without garrisons continued to be exposed to danger from them, and communications were uncertain. Yet even these most persistent men were unable to resist the victorious Prussians for long. The year had not yet run out, and the insurrection in Greater Poland already belonged to the past. The advancing Prussian troops met with no resistance from the inhabitants, and "only neighbourly jealousies, mutual accusations and anxious politeness towards the officers"[^426] recalled the times so lately past.

The activity of the Greater Poles during those few months of the insurrection in this region may be characterised as follows[^427]: those to whose credit the kindling of the revolt belongs were the nobility and the clergy; the Prussian government, too, regarded them as its greatest enemies. It was above all the petty nobility, who had little to lose, that took up arms willingly. Owners of larger fortunes, fearing damage to their property, often kept away from the uprising and frequently had to be forced into it. There were even some who, stripped of patriotic feeling, fled from the insurgent ranks to the protection of the Prussian government. The burghers, too, made common cause with the national movement, but only in small towns where the garrisons were weak or absent altogether; and always at the instigation of the nobility and clergy. The principal towns, such as Poznań, Łęczyca and Piotrków, where Germans formed a large percentage of the population, remained quiet, although cases of anti-Prussian agitation occurred there as well.
