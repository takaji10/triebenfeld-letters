Embitterment among the local population was caused by certain Prussian ordinances touching everyday life, such as, for example, the raising of the price of salt by nearly 50 per cent, and the order to pay a tax on the so-called "Musikzettli" (music permits), which authorised the holding of entertainments accompanied by music, and so on[^52].

Although the Greater Poles received the Prussian innovations with reluctance, they nevertheless did not rise to any more serious resistance against the invader. At the beginning of the Prussian invasion, when Prussian troops were entering Poznań and a Polish infantry regiment was withdrawing to Kórnik [Kurnik][^53], the Poznań burghers were not moved by this to any great extent. This passivity must puzzle us all the more since it was precisely they who had played a great role in the movement that led to the Constitution of 3 May[^54], and at one time maintained lively relations with the plenipotentiary of the towns of the Poznań district at the Four-Year Sejm, Józef[^55] Wybicki. A few months later, in the year 1792, when danger began to threaten the new constitution, the Poznań burghers were ready to stand in defence of the endangered order. This is stated plainly in the letter of the then syndic and town councillor Sobolewski of 25 May, in which he wrote to Wybicki[^56] that among the burghers "zeal is growing for putting up lanterns for the opponents of the constitution"; Sobolewski himself gave his assurance "that if he heard anyone speaking against the constitution", he would be "the first to put the noose" on him. And so the calm behaviour of the inhabitants of Poznań at the moment when Prussian troops entered the city exceeded even the expectation of Möllendorff himself. Möllendorff
