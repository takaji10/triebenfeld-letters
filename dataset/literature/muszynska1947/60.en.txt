The news of the Greater Poland uprising also found a lively echo in the chambers of the last of the Polish kings. On 4 September Stanisław August wrote to Naruszewicz: "Today God has given an event that revives our hopes: from the Wieluń Land as far as Kuyavia all the Greater Poland voivodeships have risen, joining themselves to the rising of the Commander," and at the end, as if by way of warning, he added: "whether we shall know how to make good use of it"[^155]?

Meanwhile the reverses of the last few days had shaken the Prussian army's faith in certain victory, and in the staff quarters they made a depressing impression. For 2 September the Prussian king was preparing a new assault, but on the evening of 1 September he withdrew the orders already issued and resolved to break off the siege[^156].

The reasons for this decision were also weighty.

Lieutenant-General Schwerin, ever cautious, who had returned to the army in mid-July[^157], drew the king's attention to the shortage of provisions and ammunition, to the great number of sick soldiers, and to the possibility that even after the capture of Warsaw the weakened Prussian army would meet in the Polish capital the same fate that had once befallen Igelström's garrison; finally he put forward the strongest argument, namely he pointed to the ever more violent spread of the insurrection in Greater Poland and advised that peace be restored there first. In addition, relations with the commander of the Russian forces, General Fersen[^158], which had long been strained, had worsened, and from Vienna came word that Austria, which at the beginning of July had itself brought its troops into the territory of the Commonwealth[^159], would not support the besiegers with troops.
