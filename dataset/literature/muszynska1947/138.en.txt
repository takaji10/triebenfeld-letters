The machinations concerning the final partition of Poland had begun as early as the middle of 1794. On 4 June the Austrian minister Thugut received a dispatch from Austria's representative in St Petersburg, Count Cobenzl, that Catherine regarded a new partition of Poland as unavoidable and promised to support Austria's aspirations in this respect[^438]. Thugut, who had dreamed of a third partition of Poland even earlier, succeeded in winning the emperor over completely to his plans of partition. Francis II therefore laid the duty of wrestling with the revolution on the shoulders of his generals and on 19 June returned to Vienna to watch, first and foremost, over his interest in Poland[^439].

In August the final partition negotiations began, to the immense joy of the Prussian king, who had for months been awaiting that moment with impatience[^440]. The new Prussian envoy in St Petersburg, Count Tauentzien, then demanded, in accordance with the instructions he had received, so extensive a share for Prussia that he provoked the decisive protest of his Austrian colleague Cobenzl, likewise supported by Russia. For he demanded not only the areas on the Narew and the Niemen and the lands west of the Pilica and the Vistula with Warsaw, but also the Kraków and Sandomierz voivodeships. In order to avoid bordering on Russia, he proposed carving out of Mazovia and Samogitia two separate duchies for Platon Zubov and Prince Nassau-Siegen. The Empress had no thought of agreeing to such far-reaching claims by Prussia. She assigned the Kraków and Sandomierz regions to Austria and Samogitia to herself.
