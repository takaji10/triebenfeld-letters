# Muszyńska-Zygmańska (1947), p. 84
# translation by Claude (machine translation, checked and corrected in a bilingual QA pass, 2026)

In his report to Kościuszko[^241] Dąbrowski stressed with full satisfaction the orderly conduct of his own soldiers, who in the first moment after the victory, being masters of the city, "did little to violate this natural power". They neither plundered nor wronged the inhabitants, and there was no need to restrain them from excesses. There were even cases in which the old soldiers forbade the scythemen entry into houses, lest it be thought that they wished to rob[^242].

This great discipline was Dąbrowski's merit. For it was he who, brought up on the military principles of Frederick the Great[^243], sought to instil in the soldiers a sense of discipline, punishing even the slightest offence against the service[^244]. The second man who saw to it that the army did not commit abuses in captured Bydgoszcz was Wybicki[^245]. He did not allow the private property of the inhabitants to be touched, and of what was found in the public stores he ordered, as he himself writes in his memoir[^246], an inventory to be drawn up and the goods to be turned to the needs of the soldiers[^247]. In a proclamation to the people that he issued[^248] he declared that the Poles had come "to free their brothers from foreign rule and to return them to the bosom of the common mother". Using the authority entrusted to him by the Commander, he abolished the Prussian court of justice and the Prussian Administrative Chamber, and in their place he restored "to the city, which had been Polish from time immemorial, the national offices[^249]". As the highest administrative authority he set up a Commission of Order under his own direction, which occupied itself chiefly with intelligence work and with the use of the richly stocked Prussian magazines[^250]. Its members were: Zakrzewski as president, and as members Twardowski, Nadratowski, Karski, Bulewski, Rudowski, Modliński, the Protestant preacher Stellmann, and the merchants Oppermann and Ziegler.
