In a note drawn up three months later under the eye of the king himself in the village of Merle near Luxembourg, and soon communicated to the Viennese court, Prussia made its further participation in the war against France dependent on Austria's consent to its taking possession of Polish provinces. How large an area Prussia meant to take for itself the Austrian statesman Spielmann soon discovered, when he travelled with the Prussian minister Haugwitz from Merle to Luxembourg. Haugwitz spread before him a map of Poland on which the king had drawn with his own hand the boundary of the Polish lands that were to fall to him as his share in return for continuing to support his ally. The country that Prussia demanded was almost twice as large as that demanded at the Mainz meeting. "If," said Haugwitz, "obstacles were placed in Prussia's way in the seizure of this territory, the King of Prussia would limit his aid in the new campaign against France to only those 20,000 men to which he was bound by the February alliance, but in any case he would seize a smaller part of Poland for the losses suffered so far"[^4].

Emperor Francis II long delayed expressing his consent to such exorbitant pretensions on the part of his ally. The prospects of realising his own plans concerning Bavaria became very slim from the moment when General Dumouriez, after the victory won in November over the Austrians at Jemappes, began to overrun Belgium. Two possibilities now arose for Austria: to conclude peace with France, as England, which was opposed to the partitions of Poland, desired, or to take the field against the victorious French with redoubled energy; and in that case Prussian help was indispensable.
