Nor did the starostas Dąmbski of[^147] Kaczków and Mieczkowski of Żelechlin[^148] prove any better; they lodged a complaint against the insurgents with the King of Prussia.

Meanwhile the Germans in the towns, unable to wait for the Prussian army for which they had petitioned, began to arm themselves. At Barcin and Łabiszyn [Labischin] they tried to repulse the insurgents, and in the latter place they captured 15 and sent them to Bydgoszcz. The insurgents, however, quickly dealt with such slight resistance, and still at the end of August the whole southern part of the Netze District was in their hands. Krupecki, having obtained from the Gniezno confederation the dignity of commandant, continued to bustle about the Kcynia area. The receipts he issued in return for the requisitions made in the name of the insurrection of the Gniezno voivodeship bore the inscription: integrity, liberty, independence[^149].

The spread of the insurrection throughout Greater Poland was particularly favoured by local conditions there. For the whole country was covered with forests, the largest belts of which stretched along the Noteć [Netze] and south of the sources of that river, and farther along the Warta [Warthe] and the Prosna. So great a wealth of forests was of first-rate importance for guerrilla warfare. The individual detachments of Greater Poland partisans, who in the open field would not have been able to match the Prussian army, nevertheless found a safe hiding place for themselves once they had taken refuge in the forests. There the Prussian soldier, not yet familiar with the terrain, could not track them down, whereas they could undertake unexpected forays into the countryside, cut communications, paralyse the Prussian authorities, take money from the Prussian treasuries, and escape pursuit safely.
