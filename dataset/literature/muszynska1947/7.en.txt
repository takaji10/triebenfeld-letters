In 1792, when the Targowica Confederation held sway in Poland, two German powers in the west of Europe, Austria and Prussia, were waging a bloody struggle against revolutionary France. The campaign, which had begun successfully in the spring, took an altogether unfortunate turn in the autumn. The stout resistance offered by General Dumouriez at Valmy on 20 September, and the diseases raging among the soldiers[^1], induced both allied monarchs to retreat, burying the hopes of a swift suppression of the revolution.

Frederick William II, in a letter of 17 October, informed Catherine of the retreat of his troops from the field of battle, and at the same time reminded her of the compensation due to him for the losses and war costs he had sustained[^2]. The question of compensation for taking part in the struggle against the revolution had, indeed, been the principal concern of the Prussian king throughout the whole campaign. As early as the second half of July the rulers of Prussia and Austria had met at Mainz to consider this important matter. There they deliberated on the exchange of Belgium for Bavaria to the benefit of Austria, and on the compensation of Prussia with Polish lands.
