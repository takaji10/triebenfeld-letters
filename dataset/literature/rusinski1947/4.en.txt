In their husbandry the dominant role was played by the keeping of cattle and of sheep. The tradition of the Dutchmen as stock-breeders has been preserved in Mecklenburg down to our own times.[^4-1]

After the exhaustion of the first wave of settlement, a new, intensified period of emigration from the Netherlands begins in the era of the religious Reformation. The reforming movements found in the Netherlands an especially receptive soil. Luther as well as the other reformers gained numerous adherents here. Sects arose, such as, for example, that of the Anabaptists 1 of the Mennonites. On the other hand, both Charles V and Philip II of Spain, fervent Catholics, were determined to root out heresy with fire and sword in the Netherlands subject to them. Conditions thus arose in which not only the Mennonites but also a considerable part of the remaining population was compelled to seek refuge beyond the borders of the country. This time Prussia — Ducal as well as Royal — stood open to the emigrants. After the secularisation of Prussia, Albert Hohenzollern gladly welcomed to his lands fellow-confessionals persecuted in their own country.[^4-2] In favour of receiving them there spoke, no doubt, also the experience which the Germans had had with Netherlands colonists in the XII and XIII centuries. The first Dutch settlement in Ducal Prussia arose in 1527 in the vicinity of the present town of Pasłęk (Preussisch Holland),[^4-3] and soon after that a series of further ones were founded. Somewhat later Dutch colonies began to arise in Royal Prussia. Danzig initiated the colonising activity. That city had received as early as 1454, from King Kazimierz Jagiellończyk as a gift, the Żuławy Gdańskie, and after unsuccessful attempts to settle them with the local population, from the middle of the XVI century, on the initiative of the burgomaster Ferber,[^4-4] Dutchmen began to be settled there.[^4-5] Dutch colonisation in Royal Prussia now began to draw ever wider circles.
