There were also cases in which Olęders were settled in villages still partly inhabited by a serf population. Mixed settlements arose in this way. The Olęders were then usually given a separate self-government 1 treated as an independent commune. Their separation from the total number of the inhabitants presented as a rule no difficulty; it was harder, however, to divide between them and the serf peasants the other groups of the rural population (craftsmen, lodgers, etc.), for it is not known how many of these inhabitants lived from work for the Olęders and how many from work for the peasants. In every such case, therefore, one had to have recourse to a more or less probable estimate 5.

As a special case there must still be discussed the settlement of Jędrzejewo. It was located by Piotr Czarnkowski in 1625 6, and in the charter he calls the settlers “holendrami”. The charter, however, lacks data specifying the legal position of the sołtys, and likewise lacks provisions on communal self-government. From the later information it appears that the settlement possessed an organisation proper not to the Olęder settlements but to the settlements founded at that time under German law (the so-called sołtys villages). Namely, the sołtys was hereditary, privileged as regards the manorial obligations, and sat under a separate law.
