Summing up the considerations so far, it must be stated that although as a rule the lord, and at times also the community, set certain conditions at the sale of Olęder farms, these did not — apart from the rarely occurring obligation to obtain the lord's permission for the sale — seriously restrict the principle of the free disposal of the land.

The sale as a rule comprised both the land itself and everything that was on that land (timber, grass, at times sowings and buildings). At times the lord reserved to himself the right to the oaks standing on the Olęder lands.[^48-3] Similarly the barcie (bee-trees) which the lord's beekeepers, or the inhabitants of the surrounding villages, had on the ground assigned to the Olęders, usually remained their property so long as bees were there.[^48-4] Ores (minerals) that might possibly occur on the ground assigned to the Olęders remained the property of the lord.[^48-5] The Olęders themselves built the buildings on the lands assigned to them; these were their indivisible and unrestricted property. It was the same with the live and dead stock. The community, too, appeared as owner. At times it possessed its own common pasture.[^48-6] Its common property also comprised the public buildings, such as schools and houses of prayer. The community likewise had at its disposal the field forming the endowment of the teacher of the settlement.

On the lands of the Olęders there could exist certain servitudes in favour of the lord or of third persons. To these must be reckoned the sporadic right of the manor to pasture its own cattle on the Olęder lands.[^48-7] The neighbours of an Olęder sometimes had the right of digging ditches through his land for the discharge of water,[^48-8] at times there belonged to them the right of driving their herds across Olęder land.[^48-9] On the whole, however, the burdens enumerated occurred
