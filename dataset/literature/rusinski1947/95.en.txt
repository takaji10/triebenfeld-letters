b. The milling compulsion. We meet it in the Olęder settlements only exceptionally[^95-1]. On the whole the Olęders might grind wherever it pleased them.

Other burdens were not laid upon the Olęders; in one settlement only[^95-2] every Olęder was obliged, in the period of Lent, to buy half a kopa of herrings from the lord's tavern. It is not visible, however, whether or to what extent the lord[^95-3] drew profit from this sale.

c. Manorial servitudes on the Olęders' land. In a few cases the lord had the right of pasturing his cattle on the Olęders' land 3. There thus belonged to him, in relation to the Olęders, a certain kind[^95-4] of pasture servitude. It is an understandable thing that, in view of the meagre extent of the Olęders' land, this privilege played no great part in the lord's husbandry.

5. The evolution of the manorial obligations. From the foregoing considerations it follows that the manorial obligations of the Olęders changed in the course of the colonisation. These changes might manifest themselves in this way, that either at the location of later settlements arrangements were made for services different from those in the earlier settlements, or else in one and the same settlement the lords altered the hitherto existing obligations in the course of time. The question of the evolution of the Olęders' obligations has already repeatedly been the subject of consideration in German literature. For the most part the matter has been treated in a simplified 1 one-sided way. Certain extreme cases were cited, and were then generalised. Among the first (in 1798) to concern himself with this matter was Stenger, a Prussian judge at Kargowa and later at Wolsztyn 4. He was struck by the considerable number
