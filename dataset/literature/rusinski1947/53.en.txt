# Rusiński (1947), p. 53
# translation by Claude

The communal judicature comprised above all cases of offences against the provisions of the wilkierz and the administrative orders of the communal organs, further all other cases (quarrels, brawls, thefts, boundary disputes) apart from matters of greater importance reserved to the lord. To the competence of the village court belonged also cases of offences against the provisions of the location charter.1 It is possible that the village court also performed the functions of an arbitration court. According to the charter for Borui the village judges "will look most diligently to order, security[^53-1] and tranquillity, indeed to success, order and justice, and will apply all diligence and earnestness[^53-2] of their own to it". The punishment applied by the court was the fine, and in exceptional cases arrest 3. From the judgments of the village court the condemned always had an appeal[^53-3] to the manor. So that the means of appeal should not be abused, the wilkierze provided that "whoever appeals out of spite and needlessly, groundlessly troubling the lord, shall be punished double".[^53-4] While in the sphere of administration the sołtys usually had wider competences than the remaining communal organs, in the panel of judges every member had equal rights.[^53-5] An accused member of the court was judged by the remaining sworn members in the same way as any other inhabitant of the settlement. The wilkierze defined the judicial procedure in detail. In Góździn the sittings of the court were to take place every 2 weeks, in the Jastrzębnickie Olęders every 4 weeks, on a Sunday. The members of the community were bound to appear at every summons of the court, to give testimony and to respect unconditionally all the other judgments and orders issued by the court. Special police provisions were designed to secure the safety and dignity of the court during the hearings. Women as a rule had no procedural capacity, nor could they be present at the place where the court sat. Exceptionally they could appear in court when their own affairs were being decided and their personal appearance was necessary. Also by way of exception they could substitute for their husbands in urgent matters, in the event of their absence or grave illness. But if they presumed to hurl insults before the court, a severe punishment awaited them.
