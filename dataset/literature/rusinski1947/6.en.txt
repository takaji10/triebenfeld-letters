# Rusiński (1947), p. 6
# translation by Claude

Chełmno law !, and it very much recalled the legal foundations on which[^6-1] colonists from the Netherlands were settled in the Middle Ages.[^6-2] Comparing the legal position of the Dutchman settler with that of the medieval peasant located under German law (by which was understood also the Chełmno law), we find the following most important differences:

I. The Dutchmen settled on the basis of contracts which the lord concluded not with a locator appearing in his own name as the future superior factor in the communal hierarchy, but with the whole commune, or with a deputy acting in its name;

2. the whole village answered solidarily for the obligations towards the lord. This solidarity was either expressly stated in the location document, or presumed 3,[^6-3] 3. the indispensable consequence of the equality of all the members of the commune vis-à-vis the lord was their equal standing in the internal relations of the commune, and a strong sense of community within the commune.[^6-4] Hence the locator, if there was one, received no lasting advantages in the form of exemption from rents, and the sołtys was elective;[^6-5]

4. all the manorial obligations of the Dutchmen amounted to money rents, whereas in the villages under German law — quite apart from the labour dues, minimal in any case — rents in kind played an important role. It must here, however, be noted that if we have in view[^6-6]

Ducal Prussia, certain small labour dues were imposed there on the Dutchmen too, it is true,[^6-7] which became a precedent at the founding of the later settlements on the Polish lands;[^6-8]
