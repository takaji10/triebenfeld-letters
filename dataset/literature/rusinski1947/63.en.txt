Among the peasants these are the strata designated in the Tol. as “journeymen”, “the unattached at work” and “beggars”. A factor indirectly causing the social differentiation of the peasant stratum was the legal position of its individual members. A peasant personally free had far greater chances of attaining economic independence, if only for the reason that, being independent of the lord upon whose land he wished to settle, he could in the contract put forward his own demands and in this way reach agreement upon terms more favourable to himself. This applies particularly to the population of the Olęder settlements. This population belonged as a whole to the estate of the peasants,[1][^63-1] and was also, in its entirety at least — so far as the Olęder farmers proper were concerned — free of personal subjection. For lack of more precise bases for reconstructing the whole social hierarchy of this population, it was necessary to limit oneself to a simplified division into the “economically independent”, the “intermediate” and the “economically dependent”. To depict the social structure of the Olęder population within these broadest limits there will serve us the population and hearth tables of 1789 and 1790 already employed in the discussion of the occupational division. To the independent must be assigned the farmers, innkeepers and millers enumerated there. Their independence was decided by the fact of their possessing sufficiently large farms. It is harder to determine the social position of the craftsmen. Some of them were endowed with land, which placed them on a level with the independent stratum. The isolation of the craftsmen is, however, a very difficult matter. In general, although they worked in their own workshops, their material situation arranged itself variously, according to the local demand for skilled work or for finished products. Under these conditions it will be most appropriate to assign them to the “intermediate” social stratum.
