It happened exceptionally that in a settlement there was neither a tavern nor the farm belonging to it, and the settlers kept the tap in turn, receiving for it only the świętojanka, or a money remuneration in the same proportion.[^77-1] Sales of liquors of this kind might even be for them a burdensome duty. The innkeeper was bound to haul the liquors himself from the manorial[^77-2] brewery,[3] and, under penalty, to have them always in sufficient quantity in[^77-3][^77-4] store.[^77-5] The tap embraced beer as well as vodka. “The sołtys[^77-6] and the ławniks are to see to it that (the innkeeper) does not make the liquors out of water, that people do not drink bad liquors for good money”.[5] The innkeeper was also bound “to see to music for the merry folk in the time of leisure”, presumably in order to increase the consumption of liquors. For the liquors sold the innkeeper had to render an account, presumably whenever new supplies were drawn. Isolated cases occurred in which the offices of sołtys[^77-7] and innkeeper were combined in one person. Thus, for example, in the Ol. Radoszkowskie the sołtys of the day (holding for life) was at the same time the innkeeper.[^77-8] In accordance with the lord's directives the tavern was to present itself as “a seemly house, fit for the tap as well as for the comfort of travellers”,[8] and beside it there was to stand a spacious stable.[^77-9] Incidentally we shall touch here upon the question of the extent of the consumption of liquors, concerning which we possess only fragmentary statistical data. In the taverns of the Stare and Nowe Borujskie Olęders and in the Ol. Szarki, according to the official assessment of the manorial incomes drawn up in 1797, the annual tap amounted to 229 barrels[^77-10] of garnce of beer and 12 barrels 121½ garnce of vodka, in Kościan measure.[19] In 1789 (Tol.) the population of these communities amounted to 1152 persons.
