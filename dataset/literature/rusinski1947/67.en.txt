The lands, doubtless at the settlers' request, were almost always measured out to them in a single block. This property was characteristic, moreover, of the other types of modern settlement in the area here discussed. Exceptionally an Olęder farm possessed lands in two blocks, or even in three, usually when the settlers had bought up deserted peasant farms. For the most part, as the old maps show, the lands were divided into two fields: winter and spring. Probably part of each field lay fallow for a time, so that the system of land cultivation most frequently practised by the Olęders was the three-course system.[^67-1] It is also possible that upon the better lands the improved three-course system was employed at least in part. In many settlements of particularly poor soil the fallow exceeded ⅓ of the total area of the lands; the land had to lie fallow for several years before it could again be taken under cultivation.

b. Endowment with land. The share of the individual settlers in the social income was decided above all by their endowment with land. This was usually reckoned in Chełmno włókas (hubas). The włóka was divided into[^67-2] [—] morgs; the morg contained 300 square rods (30×10). The rod, called also the laska or the różga, was of unequal length; it ranged from 7½ ells (the rod of the new or Warsaw measure)[5] to 9 ells[^67-3] (the margravial rod).[6] In an isolated instance we meet with a mention of lands measured with the Magdeburg rod,[^67-4] but there are no data as to how many ells it contained. Most often the lands were measured[^67-5] with the rod of 8 and of 9 ells, most rarely with that of 7½ ells. The matter[^67-6] was further complicated by the fact that 1 ell itself was of unequal length.
