Colonists who did not possess sufficient means were looked upon unfavourably. The Prussian king, seeing this attitude of the local authorities towards the colonists, drew minister Voss's attention to the fact that in no case should a situation be allowed in which colonists returned to their own country. For this might discourage the rest of the population from moving to the new lands. In such cases even certain sacrifices would have to be made, so long as a steady inflow of settlers from the Reich to the province was assured[^33]. At the same time the king promised to grant certain funds to cover the costs of settling the colonists[^34]. For at that time, in his opinion, was the most suitable moment to win a larger number of settlers for the colonising action, because of the threat to the southern lands of Germany from the French armies.

A great hindrance in carrying on the whole action was the lack of any guidelines on the principles of admitting colonists and the manner of carrying out settlement. Frederick William III in a letter to minister Voss ordered that in proceedings on colonisation the older models from the time of Frederick II be followed[^35]. As we shall see further, however, they were not fully applied during this action in South Prussia. Given the meagreness of the funds set aside for colonisation, Voss recommended the chambers to be economical with the funds intended for this purpose[^36]. In a letter of March 1799 minister Voss, informing the chambers of the arrival in their administrative districts of 19 families of colonists, sent at the same time, for the first time, more precise instructions on the manner of settling them[^37]. The area of land assigned to colonists, together with meadow, could not exceed 3 łany. The grant of land and its area depended on the settler's degree of wealth. Colonists were to be granted years free of obligations to the state. The instruction did not fix precisely the number of free years, stating rather generally that this would depend on the time needed to set up the farm on the new land. The rent paid by the colonists was to rise in proportion to the size of the area taken under cultivation. The chambers were to try to have suitable pastures granted to the colonists, or the right to graze cattle in the forests for a certain fee. Colonists were released for two years from the obligation of supplying forage. They were to be supplied with timber for building their dwelling houses and farm buildings. The colonists and their sons, and the persons who came with them, were exempted from the duty of military service. The settlers were personally free; they received the land in perpetual lease. The amount of the canon had not yet been fixed. It was decided to postpone this matter until all the colonists had been settled on the land set aside for this purpose.
