The question was settled in such a way that until separate regulations for the guilds were issued on this matter, they would have to grant the right of mastership to those of the newcomers who had held it before. If the applicant did not hold such a right, then all the costs connected with it were to be covered by the chamber from the general fund set aside for colonisation. From the numerous enquiries addressed by the chambers to the General Directory we may conclude that among the newcomers there were some who had left this province still in the time of the nobles' Commonwealth and now wished to return to it. In such cases all the concessions provided for colonists were due to them. If, however, they had left these lands after the partition and now wished to return again, they lost all right to the benefit[^94].

A greater intensity of urban colonisation came during the reign of Frederick William III. More and more often the conditions and concessions for colonists provided by the General Directory's order of January 1794 were made public. In the Gazeta Południowo-Pruska the full text issued by the chamber in Kalisz was published. It would follow from this that the aim now was to attract colonists to the towns of that department. For earlier Poznań and Piotrków had been the object of the central authorities' special care. This did not exclude, however, the fact that colonists settled in other towns, as for example the weavers in Babimost[^95].

Urban colonisation did not make very great progress, the more so since under minister Voss's administration, in accordance with his recommendations, an attempt was made to administer the fund set aside for colonisation very economically. For it was thought that an artisan would find good earnings in his new place. For this reason the time during which the chambers were to pay for the colonist's lodging was shortened[^96]. The principle was strictly observed that in the South Prussian towns only those artisans should be settled, and in such numbers, as were indispensably needed by the inhabitants.

When a larger number of artisans than needed wished to settle in them, the help due to them was naturally reduced[^97].

In August 1799 Voss informed the South Prussian chambers that many artisans from the Prussian provinces would try to settle in the new province to improve their condition of life. This did not agree with the real settlement intentions of the General Directory, the more so since these artisans did not possess sufficient means to set up a workshop. The minister therefore considered that the number of this category of colonists should be limited, since the partial financial help granted them by the state would in any case not give the expected results.
