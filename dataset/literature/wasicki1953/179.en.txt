In urban colonisation, then, the same property factors played the decisive part as in rural colonisation. Evidently the chambers did not keep very strictly to the minister's instructions and directions, since in reply to the chambers' numerous enquiries about settling colonists he ordered them to keep strictly to the orders issued earlier[^99]. The chambers, however, were concerned mainly with the newcomers from Württemberg, a relatively large number of whom had come to South Prussia, and for them too they tried to obtain increased financial help from the state. To these efforts, however, they received a negative answer from the General Directory, which instructed the chambers that the purpose of the fund set aside for colonisation was help for all the colonists settling, without making particular distinctions[^100]. In the particular case of the colonist joiner Baltazar Schiedt, settled in Krotoszyn, the General Directory listed the benefits due to him under the order of January 1794 on this matter.

Those colonists, however, deserved special support who possessed some means, thereby giving a guarantee that after settling they would be able to maintain the workshop they founded by themselves without the state's help. At first the amount of means a colonist should possess to receive the state's financial help was not exactly fixed. Only in 1802 was the amount of means a colonist must possess to obtain permission to settle fixed at 200 thalers. Only this category of persons, then, was entitled to the state's financial help needed for setting up a workshop. All the other colonists were entitled to the benefits listed in the order of 1794, but without financial help. They received a free licence and the right of mastership only after passing the appropriate examination.

As follows from the facts cited above, then, the Prussian authorities did not attach great importance to urban colonisation. This was undoubtedly bound up with the whole policy of the Prussian authorities towards the towns, industry and crafts in the period of South Prussia. The strong development of manufacturing industry in the other Prussian provinces, and especially in Silesia, did not allow the Prussian authorities to settle specialists of this branch in South Prussia, so as not to create dangerous competition for the Prussian provinces. For these reasons, too, urban colonisation was confined to only a small number of building craftsmen. Only the increased inflow of settlers from the lands on the left bank of the Rhine brought about the settlement here of a certain number of weavers, chiefly in Dąbie on the Ner[^101]. Unfortunately we cannot give the exact total number of town colonists who arrived and settled in South Prussia. In Voss's reports, tables and lists there was not the slightest mention of it. It must therefore be thought that their number was negligible, since the main stress was laid on rural colonisation.
