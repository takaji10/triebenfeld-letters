After this time he could sell the land, but only after presenting the new buyer to the chamber, which established his qualifications. The colonist could name his successor in case of death, also with the chamber's consent. If he left several successors, the choice of one of them belonged to the chamber.

The colonist was obliged to pay money dues to the state, the amount of which depended on the quantity and quality of the land he held. He paid, then, a rent and a hearth tax (podymne) calculated from the average price of grain. Part of the rent, as a rule ⅛, the colonist delivered in grain to the royal magazines. In the contract he was obliged to keep the dwelling houses and farm buildings in usable condition, but he could not demand free timber for their repair. He was exempt from performing labour services for the domains, but within the colony the settlers were obliged to keep the roads and bridges in order. Besides the hearth tax paid to the state, the colonist was also obliged, after the expiry of the free years, to supply in case of war a horse with a cart, and in peacetime to supply the required carting and forage and to work on fortifications[^80].

The colonies founded formed an independent administrative unit in which authority was exercised by the sołtys (village headman). The authority above the sołtys was the "Amtmann".

It is difficult to give general precise figures for the number of colonists settled in South Prussia. Some light is thrown here only by minister Voss's report, laid before the king. According to it, the number of colonists who arrived up to 1805 was in all 10,295 persons; it is not known, however, whether this covered only settlers in the villages or also in the towns, nor does it appear whether these figures include colonists settled on private estates. The colonists were settled on 62,190 Magdeburg morgen. This figure is given also by other bourgeois historians, German and Polish.

Relying on the list drawn up by the Poznań chamber in 1804, giving both the number of colonies founded and of settlers who arrived, we can state that their number for the Poznań department was 2324 persons in all. It should be supposed that the colonising action in the years 1805—1806 did not increase, but rather weakened, because of the lack of funds and the difficulties the chambers' officials met in settling colonists. Besides, Voss's report sent in 1804 shows many "établissements" not yet occupied but already prepared to receive settlers. Moreover, his report does not show the number of those colonists who, settled in the Poznań chamber, left the land allotted to them and either returned to Germany or moved elsewhere. This was in any case understandable, since Voss was showing the overall expenditure connected with the colonising action and therefore gave figures for all the colonists who arrived and were settled, without mentioning what then became of them.
