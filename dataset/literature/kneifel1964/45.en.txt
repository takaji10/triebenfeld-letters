# Kneifel (1964), p. 45
# translation by Claude

1731 it said: „There is permitted to them for all eternity to build and maintain a school, and for this school there is given to them two Morgen of land, so that from it they can maintain a schoolmaster". Further there arose schools: 1765 in Witoszyn-Lengden, 1770 in Bögpomöz and 1780 in Rybitwy.

In the Dobrzyner Land German schools organized themselves: 1710 in Michalki near Rypin, 1730 in Klosno, 1745 in Tomaszewo, 1747 in the Wo Ischen Buden; then 1765 in Gaj, Iwane, Kierz, Kotowo, Kleszczyn, Klein-Kretki, Seedorf and Zduny. To these still 1769 in Bocheniec, 1774 in Ryszewek, Bialowiezyn and 1776 in Witkowo.

In Kujawien there were about 25 German schools until the taking over of the land by Prussia, while in the Warthebruch their number was ten. In the Kalischer Land German schools formed: 1740 in Wladyslawow-Rosterschütz, then in Prazuchy, Zbiersk, Danowiec, Zamenty, Zakrzyn, Przespolew, Czachulec, Grodziec, Mycielin, Obory, Bialobloty, Gadowskie Hol., Orlina, Wierzby, Lazinsk. In all one counted in the Kalischer Land 16 and in the Gostyniner district about ten schools. Further there constituted themselves in the Lodz area schools among others: in Dombrowa Hol., Mileszki, Groß- Bruzyca, Klein-Bruzyca, Radogoszcz, Kaly, Slowik, Swendow, Glogowiec, Domaradzyn, Paprotnia Hol. With the schools in the cities there existed here until 1793/95, i.e. until the incorporation of Mittelpolen into the Prussian state, approximately 125 German-Lutheran schools.

With this basic stock was laid, in a manner of speaking, the foundation for our school system. While the Polish-Catholic peasant at that time stood in a relation of serfdom to his lord of the manor and culturally on a low level, the German-Lutheran colonist was a free man and, thanks to his schooling and his more progressive manner of farming, far his superior. Only in the cities, though by no means sufficiently, did the Polish population have schools.

In the German village schools, generally called cantorate schools (religion schools), the teachers taught — almost down into the first decades of the 19th century — only in the winter half of the year, i.e. from late autumn to spring. But even then instruction as well as school attendance left much to be desired. The subjects of instruction — religion, German (writing and reading) and arithmetic — exhausted themselves in imparting the most necessary things and for the most part did not go beyond the rudiments of very modest knowledge. Naturally there were among the teachers also such as filled the gaps of their knowledge despite their low degree of education and honestly strove to raise their schools in every respect and to give the children not only knowledge but also to form and strengthen them in character. In instruction the greatest stress was laid precisely on religion (Bible, catechism, hymnal). Naturally every school session began and closed with song and prayer.

Besides their service as teachers the „school-holders" were also cantors, i.e. readers in the service of worship and rural representatives of the pastors. On Sundays and feast days they held a reading service, performed churchly acts (baptisms and burials) and represented the pastor also otherwise, for example in confirmation instruction. To the cantor was subject the religious care often of several localities, which were joined together into the so-called cantorate. According to their size a rural church congregation was made up of a number of cantorates. They lay from the parish place on the average five to twenty kilometers and even more distant away. In consequence of the great distances, especially with the bad conditions of the roads, the pastors visited them only two to three times a year.
