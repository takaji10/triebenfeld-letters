Marriages of an Olęder with a Polish peasant woman, or the other way round, did not occur in the eighteenth century, or at least he had not heard of them, and on the Polish side the lack of relations and the separateness of the two groups were stressed. The Polish peasants were pious and fond of the outward rites of religion, but on the other hand enormously superstitious, mistrustful of the manor and of people from the town, of officials, but also humble towards them, not valuing education, but loving their land and judging a man by his work, but only manual work. Among their faults were a lack of respect for other people's property, especially forest property, and perhaps a somewhat loose life of the women. The Protestant Olęders were not inferior to the Poles in piety, and in superstition they far surpassed them, using, especially in raising cattle, a host of sorcerer's practices, which their Polish neighbours borrowed from them. At the same time, however, they showed a greater knowledge of farming. In their dealings with more educated people the Olęders were less humble than the peasants, showing more worldly manners. They did not attach much importance to learning either; many of them could not write, though probably most could read. Although they lived in Poland, not all of them could speak Polish. There were among them individuals who, having been in the army, had learnt to speak Russian fairly well. The Polish peasants, on the other hand, tried to say at least a few words in their dialect, which was rather hard to understand, even for persons who knew German reasonably well. The Olęders were also distinguished by their industry, but did not show such attachment to the land as the Polish peasants. Among the Olęders one did not meet very poor people, frequent among the Polish villagers, which also followed from their having farms of larger area and from their ingenuity and good husbandry. A fair income came to them, for instance, from home weaving, practised by the Olęder women and neglected by the Polish peasant women. From the sheep the Olęders raised, almost all the Olęder women made very fine and durable woollen skirts, thicker and thinner, and aprons, giving the yarn to the town only for dyeing. The Polish peasants, on the other hand, made only coarse linen, which they sometimes had dyed blue in the little town and from which they sewed trousers and *sukmany* for the men, or else they bought factory goods. The prosperity and higher culture of daily life of the Olęders compared with the Polish peasant showed itself also in their love of a tidy dwelling. The [Polish] peasants' huts looked worse beside the neat Olęder ones, but as a worker, a so-called "manual" one, the Polish peasant was more sought after.

As for the spiritual culture of the people of the Olęder settlements that arose in the eighteenth century in the Pyzdry Forest, the sources allow us to grasp, directly or indirectly, their confessional relations, schooling and religious worship, and the culture of daily life (chiefly material).
