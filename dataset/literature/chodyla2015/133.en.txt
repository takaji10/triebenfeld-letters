Buildings were then put up of wood, in post-and-plank, log and half-timbered (frame) construction. Clay and bog iron ore were also commonly used building materials. Buildings of wood and of bog iron ore were insulated inside with a daub of clay. The architecture of the dwelling houses of the Pyzdry Forest area, as architectural research of the years 2009 – 2011 has shown[^332], has recurring features common to the whole area of the forest. They are: symmetrical pitched roofs at an angle of 45 degrees; gables boarded vertically; rafters resting on decorated ceiling beams projecting beyond the face of the wall; box windows, "standing rectangles", of one or two casements; divided windows, with panes of the same size or larger below and smaller above; outer doors of ledged or frame-and-panel construction, mostly decorated, most often double. The features of the farm buildings are close to those of the dwellings. The visible differences concern the following features: rectangular or square windows; additional small window openings without glass, in the shape of an equilateral triangle; doors of boards set vertically.

The houses usually had wide chimneys, joined at the bottom to bread ovens. The latter, which also served for drying flax, were built by the Olęders of the Pyzdry Forest, as a precaution against fire, in the yard or the garden. The house ovens were inspected every year by the sołtys and the assessors, who collected fines for the manor for the forbidden drying of flax in the cottages[^333].

Because the surviving houses have been rebuilt many times, the original layout of their interiors cannot be established exactly. According to the ethnographic literature, however, it may be supposed that the most popular type was the two-room cottage, with the two rooms on either side of a hall in the middle.

The furnishing of the interiors of the Olęder cottages consisted of beds, standing most often against the walls, chests for keeping clothes, a table under the window, and benches for sitting and for mangling linen. Not every room had a stove. Shelves hung on the walls, and there was also (probably above all in the houses of Catholics) the so-called "*holy corner*", where a crucifix and other devotional objects were placed[^334].

In the interiors of the Olęders' houses there were also a fair number of the utensils needed in the household, most probably like those used in other, non-Olęder settlements and known from the ethnographic literature.
