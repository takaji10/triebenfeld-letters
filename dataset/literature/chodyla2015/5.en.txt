# The Near Unknown

The Pyzdry Forest (Puszcza Pyzdrska) is a large forest complex that owes its name to the fact that nearly one sixth of its area belonged to the town of Pyzdry from 1387 to 1793. The natural boundaries of this geographical region are formed on the west, north and east by the rivers Prosna, Warta and Powa. The southern boundary of the Pyzdry Forest is set by the extent of the continuous woods stretching north of Brudzew, Stawiszyn and Ceków Kolonia. Centuries of agricultural colonisation, which consisted in cutting down the forest, reduced its area considerably. A continuous forest complex of 16,500 ha has nevertheless survived to our day, together with three other complexes close by of 1,100 to 1,400 ha. In their neighbourhood there are, besides, more than 300 smaller woods.

We owe the breaking up of the forest chiefly to the Olęder settlers, who in the eighteenth, but also in the nineteenth century cleared about 50% of its area, creating in the years 1746-1793 alone, that is in less than half a century, 58 settlements with their own churches, schools and cemeteries. These were not colonists settled in the time of the Prussian partition by the Prussian occupying authorities. The Olęder settlers came at the invitation of Polish landowners at the end of the First Commonwealth, migrating to Poland for their bread. Settlers of the Protestant confessions (chiefly the Lutheran), of German and sometimes of Polish nationality, made up the greater part of this movement, but Catholics and Poles took part in it too, in more than a third. Besides settlements whose people were of one confession and one nation there were also settlements mixed in both respects.

After the Second World War, when the descendants of the Protestant German Olęders were expelled, many of these villages were emptied. On land that until recently had been worked intensively, cleared and drained for farming, natural succession set in, and the abandoned meadows turned into marsh. Locally the forest began to be called "the Bieszczady of Greater Poland", a name that sums up the character of the region.
