# Chodyła (2015), p. 94
# translation by Claude

The form of these villages was also affected by the layout of the fields (arable land, meadows, pastures) and the system of tilling them. As to the latter, the three-field system in the Olęder villages was, to a far smaller degree than in the labour-service villages, a communal three-field system, that is one tied to the so-called compulsion of the field (*przymus polny* or *niwowy*) for the whole village, under which each peasant had in a given year to sow, on one of the fields of his farm, lying in one line with and next to the like fields of other farms, particular crops (winter or spring crops) or to leave that field fallow, according to the decision of the whole village community. In the compact Olęder settlements, in the regular and irregular row villages, but also in those of various other forms, often, and in the scattered, loose ones generally, the individual three-field system was used. This followed from the predominance in them of the *łan* (strip) layout. With such a layout of the land each settler had his field in one piece (block), usually on a long strip of land (a rectangle), and tilled it as he saw fit, independently of his neighbours. These strips lay parallel to each other and ran from the middle of the village to its boundary. A characteristic feature of the *łan* layout, which distinguished the villages in which it was found from those with the *niwa* (open-field) layout, was also that each farm had its own field road running the length of its whole strip of land. The *łan* layout of many Olęder villages came about because they were founded on forest clearings, where the land lay in just such a way and the soils varied, or on so-called *pustkowia* (isolated holdings), where the land was brought into use gradually. In the latter case settlement most often began with one, two or a few scattered farms, distant from each other, which of course had no conditions for the communal three-field system[^190].

The layout of the Olęder villages, especially their internal layout, was affected by the types of their homesteads, their farmsteads. Of the four types of farmstead – sets of buildings (distinguished by the placing of the masses of the dwelling and farm buildings, and by their position in relation to the roads, the watercourses and each other) – proper to the traditions of the country and place the settlers came from, the most widespread in Greater Poland, including the settlements founded in the eighteenth century in the Pyzdry Forest, was the fourth type: the scattered farmstead, most often with an arcaded cottage (in time this type of building disappeared and is not found today), set with its gable or its wall to the road, and with free-standing farm buildings, put up of wooden logs, in post-and-plank or, more rarely, log construction, and also, in large numbers, of clay and bog iron ore. In general people built with the material that was most readily available and cheapest. All the buildings (house, cowshed-pigsty, stable, barn, granary) were put up separately, scattered, most often on a quadrangular plan; barns were sometimes placed apart, beyond the road. The commonest pattern was one of two or three buildings: the cottage, the barn, and a livestock building (made up of pigsty, stable, cowshed and cart shed). At the same time, though more rarely, there were free-standing buildings such as sheepfolds, sheds and cart sheds[^191].
