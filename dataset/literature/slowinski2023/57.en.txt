"I have no opinion of bare realism, but the opposite is equally dangerous. You yourself think that you are not a dreamer, but I think you are not very far from being one, and you must watch yourself so as not to become one. Forgive me if this causes you pain, but I hope that you understand me well and will not take it amiss. I cannot and do not want to believe that you could turn away from me because I do not accept everything intensely. Yet it is true, what I feel. I think it is very unwise to discuss this in letters, because it will only lead to misunderstandings. Remember one thing: that you need not worry on my account."

Two letters to his brother Gustav have survived from January. He writes on the twentieth, heavily burdened by illness, about how he spent New Year's Eve. "At 10 p.m. I was able to disappear behind one of the snow-covered hills and, praying, receive the year 1943 from the hand of God." Bruno is troubled by the state of his brother's soul – he is carrying some great burden, and it stands between him and God. Since that burden could not be sunk along with the old year, let him listen to a brotherly piece of advice and go to a pastor. "He will summon the wondrous, divine power (which Christ passes on to him together with his office) and will name the sin by its name. And then it will turn out that this sin, however great and filthy it may be, will have to give way." An honest conversation is necessary, and the beginning is always the hardest.

As in the earlier letter to his sister, so here too we see the great zeal of a candidate for a future pastor in the religious, spiritual formatting of his siblings. It is hard to say what to call Gustav's sin (if – as Bruno writes – it is a sin at all), but perhaps these torments of the brother may be the aftermath of the ban on administering baptisms, funerals or direct work at the services. "But there is an answer to this, which you will find in the Letter to the Romans 8:35." Yet he does not quote it.
