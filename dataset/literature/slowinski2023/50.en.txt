He also talks about two courses he attended: anti-aircraft defence and the conducting of ceremonies. With bitterness he writes about the issued ban on lay preachers and cantors performing baptisms, funerals and other church rites. The previously quoted Pastor Gross wrote about this. "Our neighbouring parishes have been experiencing tensions since the summer, because no cantor or, as it is called in the decree of the Reichsstatthalter, lay assistant may any longer celebrate services; only pastors may celebrate them. Thereby depriving the cantors' councils of the possibility of acting and of experiencing the readings during services every Sunday." The cantors, who were most often also teachers administratively, are, as teachers, being separated from the Church. One may interpret this decision as an effect of Nazi policy, whose aim was to push the Church out of the teaching and upbringing of the young. This ban pained Gustav deeply. He greatly enjoyed active participation in the mass and had always done so gladly. Christmas, New Year's Eve, the New Year – these therefore put him in a sad mood. Apart from that he has nothing special to report. "Yesterday [he writes on 19 December 1942] we had a pig slaughter."

Erich Kunitzer, director of the Teachers' Seminary in Łódź at the time when Bruno Mielke was studying there, is delighted with the letter from his former pupil. He asks at which sector of the front he has found himself, and boasts of his son Friedrich, who as a commander in a special unit is fighting somewhere in the Kalmyk steppe to the south-east of Rostov. Many former seminarians are at the front; of the 120 teachers from Łódź, 50 have been called up to the army. "May God protect you all – Kunitzer writes – so that we may yet see one another. The struggle is hard, but we must carry it through to the end. I, too, would rather die at the front than fall under the rule of the Jews, who have sworn a terrible vengeance upon us."
