In the five-month battle, one and a half million German soldiers and their allies perished. Of the 91,000 prisoners taken in the Stalingrad cauldron, only about six thousand returned. But what news could this soldier have read in the "delayed newspapers"? Unaware of the westward turn of the wheel of history, he plans to establish numerous pen-pal contacts with potential candidates for a wife, so that after a life at the front full of unrest and renunciation he might look about for a cosy home. "It will be beautiful when I return home after the victory and, with a sense of peace, can lead a quiet life."

In December Bruno Mielke wrote a great deal. The approaching Christmas brought back memories of the family home, of his sisters, his brother, his father, and of his mother who was no longer alive (she died in 1933). "I want to write so that you especially, during the Christmas season, receive more mail from me." His first joy was the three-kilogram parcel received from home, and in it a fir wreath with a little angel's head and tinsel. "I immediately fastened it to my little cupboard, made from an empty grenade crate with two boards fitted in. Several comrades who 'happened' to find themselves nearby and were gazing with longing eyes at the holiday cake being taken out were given a bite to taste. They delivered hymns of praise concerning the delicacy's exquisite flavour."

The letter of 14 December also contains the information that soldiers who have no families should send stamps to the local party leaders, and these will pass them on to the Women's Leagues, which will prepare holiday parcels for the men at the front. The Wehrmacht, too, saw to it that every soldier received a richly stocked parcel, in which, alongside snuff, cigarettes, sweets, chocolate and darning wool, there were also envelopes for letters, a comb, and a bottle of vodka and of wine.
