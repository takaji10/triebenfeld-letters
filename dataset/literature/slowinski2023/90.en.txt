For this is noticeable – even in this relatively modest group of letters – that regardless of whether he is marching to the East to build the promised paradise, or retreating under the growing pressure of the enemy, he never gives a sign that he is undergoing a crisis of faith. Still his chief dream is to become a pastor, to celebrate masses and other rites and to proclaim the word of God. "It is not out of the question that God will have to lead us all through very difficult roads." He did not abandon his God for the new "filthy" faith. But – and this observation is worth raising – the image of the Christian: the tiny, humble "servant of God," mocked by Nazi propaganda, does not quite agree with what we perceive in the stance of this soldier. He fights on the front line, kills enemies and lice, is tough and does not want to be worse or weaker than his comrades. He differs, perhaps, from some of them in that he sees, or only senses, the coming days of defeat. In this sense one may venture the remark that he did not walk those bad roads in darkness. Another matter is whether the light that shone for him was bright to the very end.

Reading Bruno Mielke's letters, one has the impression that they were written in two languages. Personal experiences, concern for the family, confessional declarations – this is vocabulary drawn from the Bible, religious writings and sentimental ballads about love. Here even the descriptions of the side he is fighting against do not "reek" of hatred. These are Russians, opponents, in the sharpest version the enemy. And the pre-war times are an idyllic picture of "blessed hours" and the happy inhabitants of Łazińsk. Quite different phrases flow when he declares fidelity and obedience to the Führer, readiness for every sacrifice for the great Germany that is setting the world to rights. This is a set of ready-made clichés from the language of the Third Reich (LTI – Lingua Tertii Imperii).
