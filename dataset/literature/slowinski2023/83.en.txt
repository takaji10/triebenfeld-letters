# Słowiński (2023), p. 83
# translation by the editor's files

 the situation is more tense, twice a week! He asks his brother not to burden himself with writing to him every day. A week earlier they were withdrawn to the rear – 70 km from the front. "Here it is nicer and more comfortable than in the previous place. The barracks are spacious and airy. For a whole week now we have been wallpapering and working on beautifying them. The cold and the darkness no longer weary us so much. A little heating suffices, and at 11 o'clock at night it is still light. Soon there will be no night. Outside there is a thaw, and in some places last year's moss is showing". In the next paragraph he shares with his brother joyful news. Namely, on 20 April, on the Führer's birthday, he was promoted to the rank of corporal (*Obergefreiter*). True, after two years of service it is nothing great, but dear to the heart all the same. He also treats it as a testimony of the grace of God, which blessed his service and above all preserved him from all obstacles. As a corporal he also held his first service in the melting snow. A clump of moss served him as an altar. "This hour in solitude allows one to see one's own weaknesses". He is also saddened by the sight of his chums drinking and carousing all night long. He is almost ashamed that he sits here in the north and listens to the murmur of the pines, while his *Kameraden* (comrades) must fight heavy battles on the southern front. "Compared with them – we are on furlough here", and he adds at once – "may it not get worse". He is also lucky with his superiors. The commander of his company is Oberleutnant Hengl. He is the son of the lieutenant general Ritter von Hengl, commanding the 2nd Mountain Rifle Division[^21].



[^21]: General of Mountain Troops Georg Ritter von Hengl was a distinguished professional officer. During the First World War he fought, among other things, at Verdun and in Russia. Later, as an aviator, he shot down 10 English aircraft. For his war merits as an aviator, in his native kingdom of Bavaria he was granted the title of lifelong nobility, which was connected with adding the appellation Ritter von to his surname. During the Second World War he took part in the campaign on the Arctic front in Russia. In 1943 he commanded the 2nd Mountain Division, and in the year 1944 – during the period from which Bruno Mielke's letters have been preserved – he commanded the XIX Mountain Corps.
