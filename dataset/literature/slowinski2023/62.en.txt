She judges such conduct of the hierarchy as acting together with the enemy states against the Third Reich. Once victory has been achieved, she believes, the affairs with the Church will finally be put in order, for it cannot be that the Church acts against the state. Then, too, she will take decisive steps. It will be either – or. And although she will not try to convince her parents, she at least hopes that they will show understanding for her stance. Of course she believes that "…among the Christians faithful to their Church there are few who stand up in defence of Germany and the Führer, but these are exceptions – among them you are. You have a sincere faith, but you also, after all, have reason, which allows you to discern what the future holds. Earlier you were rooted to that village of yours, cut off from the events of the world. Yet now you cannot close your eyes and ears to all this, which does not correspond to your unyielding views. Do you not see at all what the struggle of nations is about?". She knows that Bruno will want to rebut all this. And she concludes: "Believe me, if I did not like you, your views would be indifferent to me. You are such a dear man, so natural, that I have grown fond of you from the heart." There are no other letters or cards that would attest that this acquaintance was continued.

What happened at Stalingrad was a great shock for the Germans, a source of sharp tensions and discussions in the political and military leadership of the Third Reich. For not everyone agreed with the führer, who called this spectacular defeat (of which the world was already speaking) a "heroic spectacle of German history". Generals Karl Strecker and Helmuth Groscurth did not end their report on the capitulation with the sacramental *Heil Hitler*, but with the traditional call: "Long live Germany"...
