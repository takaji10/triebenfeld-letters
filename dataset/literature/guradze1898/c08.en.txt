# Guradze (1898), Chapter VI.
# translation by Claude

# **Chapter VI.** {#chapter-vi.}

The Time of the Grand Duchy of Warsaw 1807–1815.

When Napoleon created the Duchy of Warsaw through the constitution of July 22, 1807,[^171] with the King of Saxony as Grand Duke at the helm, to which the entire present-day province of Poznań belonged, except for 52 localities of the Netze District that remained part of West Prussia in 1807, he declared in Article 4, Part I of the constitutional document: 'All slavery (esclavage) is abolished, all citizens are equal before the law, the status of individuals is under the protection of the tribunals.

The term “esclavage” here did not only mean serfdom, which had already been abolished in Prussian times, but also hereditary subjugation. One may hold the view that the abolition of Prussian laws during the war formally reversed the changes to the subjugation system that had been made in favour of the peasants. However, from now on (1807), not only was serfdom abolished, but also hereditary subjugation was completely done away with. This is evident from the decree of King Friedrich August of Saxony on December 24, 1807,[^172] “Regarding the Implementation of Article 4, Part One of the Constitution,” which will be discussed shortly. The Constitution not only further assured the peasants of the legal hearing, jurisdiction, and protection of their rights already granted to them during Prussian times, but the personal status of the hitherto subjugated peasants was extraordinarily improved by the abolition of the subjugation ties.

Since it was necessary to more precisely define the relationship that existed between the landowners and the peasants after the abolition of subjugation, and particularly to establish the new relationship of those peasants who held their positions for an indefinite period without contracts, the aforementioned decree appeared on December 21, 1807, which due to its importance should be reproduced here in its wording:

Article 1\. Every farmer and day labourer, who does not possess property rights in perpetuity or for a certain number of years due to a previous, voluntarily made agreement,[^173] can freely leave his current place of residence and settle wherever he wants within the Duchy of Warsaw.

Article 2\. However, he is obliged to first report to the landowner, who does not have the right to hold him back, and to the authorities appointed for this purpose by the district administrative bodies, which must report to the higher authorities about anyone arriving or departing in their district.

Article 3\. Those countrymen and day labourers who wish to remain in their current place of residence are granted the freedom to live there for another year, on the condition that they fulfil the same obligations to which they have been subjected so far. The landowner cannot force them to move out during this period, nor can he increase the services previously provided, regardless of what they may be called.

Article 4\. Upon any request, authorised judicial persons will take on all voluntarily made contracts, whether they concern hereditary or temporarily secured possession, according to the mutual conditions entered into between the landowner and the countrymen, either with their previous or with another landowner. The judicial person is to act as a witness on behalf of the government and is personally responsible for ensuring 1\. whether the contracting parties are capable of entering into such voluntary contracts; 2\. whether the contract is truly being concluded freely and not under fear, coercion, or deceit. Every point of the negotiation must be precisely defined or described in the recorded protocol.

Article 5\. Finally, any countryman who wishes to leave his current place of residence is obliged to hand over the property belonging to him to the landowner,[^174] which consists of the farming equipment and the sown crops. He cannot be exempted from this obligation unless he proves that he has lost this property after October 1, 1807, whether through livestock disease or other misfortunes.

All other demands that the landowner could make against the farmer, and which the latter disputes, can be asserted through a lawsuit in court. However, under no circumstances can the landowner retain the farmer against his will, but is only entitled to seek his disputed property through legal means.

As a supplement to this ordinance, the royal decree of January 18, 1810,[^175] established that the departing peasant persons should seek certificates from the authorities of the place, district, or department they were leaving, confirming that they had complied with the provisions of the decree of July 22, 1807, and that they would not be accepted at the place to which they were going without such certificates.

The legislation of the Grand Duchy of Warsaw even granted, and this is to my knowledge the first case of its kind in Eastern Europe, some members of the peasant class, namely the property owners, political rights. The constitution was known to be a monarchical-constitutional one, and laws were to be decided in the Diet by the country's representatives. For this purpose, not only was the nobility elected, but also in community meetings. According to Article 58 of the Constitutional Document,[^176] all non-noble, property-owning citizens could participate in these elections, thus also farmers if they were property owners.

On July 23, 1809, the formation of municipalities and municipal self-administrations was ordered. However, the actual changes brought about by these measures were small, as everywhere the landowner remained the judicial lord and chairman of the municipality. Plichta[^177] does report that in the Płock Department, where the matter was pursued with the necessary seriousness, an excellent influence was exerted on the farmers appointed as 'village councillors.' “They felt elevated by their new dignity, began to pay attention to their behaviour, and also to work on the order in the village.” But the turmoil of war also put an end to any flourishing development here again.

The change in applicable laws, which occurred with the introduction of the Code Civil as statutory law, could have no positive influence on peasant conditions. The French laws, assuming the equality of all inhabitants of the state, contained only general provisions, none that specifically related to the manorial-peasant relationships. Therefore, the conditions of the peasants during that epoch must solely be judged from the standpoint of Polish law and the new Warsaw laws.

The ducal government had by no means considered the legislation in this regard as complete. Surveys were continually being carried out, and inquiries were being made to find a good basis for changes for the benefit of the peasants. However, as they had set themselves the insoluble task of improving the situation of the peasants without diminishing the landowners' rights, who were already financially weakened significantly by Napoleon's war and other financial demands, naturally no result was achieved. The only positive proposal that had almost come to fruition[^178] was to levy an annual tax of several thousand thalers on the landowners and to give it as a premium to the best peasant farmers in the Duchy to encourage them to purchase their farms as property. But before this certainly good plan could be executed, the Warsaw regime had come to an end again.

From this brief epoch, the ordinance of December 21, 1807, seems to be of immense importance for shaping peasant conditions. It grants the corvée farmers, who hold their positions for an indefinite period (what was legally referred to in Prussia as “for cultivation and use against services and levies”), the right to leave them and to establish an existence elsewhere at their discretion. A simple deregistration with the landowner and the authorities sufficed; then the peasant, who had previously been tied to the land for his entire life, could go wherever he pleased, where he found lighter work or a fairer master. There was no longer any talk of ransom money, forced labour service, leaving behind adult children, etc., upon departure; only the inventory, which belonged to the landowner, had to be left behind. On the other hand, the landowner had to keep the peasant on his property for a year after the announcement of the ordinance, that is, until December 21, 1808, if he fulfilled the previous services; after that, he too could make arbitrary changes in the occupation of the positions.

It is inexplicable how one could infer a mutual one-year termination right from the wording of Article 3\. Nonetheless, later Prussian legislation did just that. The Cabinet Order of May 3, 1815, stated that the mutual one-year termination right introduced during the Warsaw period should remain in place. However, the Warsaw laws do not reveal any such thing; they merely stipulated that the peasants in question had to be left in their positions with their previous rights for one more year after the publication of the above ordinance. However, it does seem that specific moving dates were indeed observed. For example, the records indicate usual moving dates such as St. John's Day (Johanni), St. George's Day (Georgi), Feast of the Immaculate Conception (Mariä Empfängnis), the feast day of St. Adalbert, and others. If a one-year notice was really common after 1808, it was in no way legally justified by the provisions of the ordinance of December 21, 1807\.

This law has now given the peasants the opportunity to escape any oppression from their masters by simply leaving them, thereby exerting pressure on the landowners who could not do without their peasants, to reduce the burdens and make the treatment more lenient. On the other hand, the landowner could rid himself of peasants who seemed superfluous or useless to him without being forced to reoccupy the positions.

Even though these two factors could have had a profound impact on the peasant situation, at least in the Poznań Government Department, everything remained the same. In the Netze District, the law seems to have had some effect, which will be discussed later. On the other hand, the consequences in inner Poland are said to have been extraordinary. A then Duchy of Warsaw official, the later Prussian Government Councilor Plichta, describes his personal observations as follows:[^179] “As soon as freedom was granted to the rural population, it began forcefully to tear itself away from the soil to which it had been inhumanly and unnaturally chained for centuries, particularly in the Płock Department. It gave notice en masse to their landlords regarding the land they possessed, thereby causing the landlords, given their inability to manage their farms other than through corvée labour, considerable alarm and making them inclined almost throughout to reduce the peasants' services and thus alleviate their lot. And, in order not to find themselves in the same predicament again, caused by such terminations, as long as the current state of culture and population continues in deeper Poland, most of them have endeavoured to enter into contracts with the peasants for as long a time as possible.”

However, these contracts still bore the stamp of one-sided superiority, even though they were voluntarily entered into, as the government, despite the relevant regulations, did not support the peasants—who were still mostly intellectually quite backward and dependent—in concluding the contracts to the extent that they would have been fully equal to the landlords.

In fact, freedom of movement has resulted in a reduction of the peasants' burdens in those areas. Naturally, negative consequences could not be avoided for those peasants who gave up their property without having a new place to stay in sight, and who then unexpectedly did not find one. However, it is unlikely that this happened too frequently, because on many estates there were vacant farmsteads whose landlords were glad to find new tenants. One only has to read the descriptions provided by Beheim-Schwarzbach to readily admit that it was indeed in the interest of most landlords to attract as many new peasants as possible. Why should the effect of freedom of movement not be favourable back then when no landlord even considered the possibility that the peasants' land would ever actually be owned by the current tenants, when only a few considered the possibility of abolishing forced labour, and therefore almost everyone had an interest in having a large number of peasants? Despite this, the prevailing opinion in the literature is that the ordinance of December 21, 1808, did nothing but harm the peasant class, as freedom of movement was introduced without the landlords being obligated to reoccupy the positions.[^180] This view is a result of the widespread misconception that landlords generally had an interest in taking over vacant farmsteads, which may occasionally have been the case, but was by no means the rule, as already elaborated above. Thus, the statement of Minister Baden[^181] was partly correct when he said, “the Warsaw law may have freed the peasants from their chains but also took away their boots,” but these words must at least be supplemented as follows: “however, the peasant could easily and without money buy new boots everywhere.”

"In the Poznań Department," says Plichta, "it is true that peasants here and there have changed their landlords, but the obligations have apparently remained unchanged. The reason for this is to be found in the fact that there were very extensive estates here, whose few owners could assert all the advantages against the peasants that are available to monopolists. The fact that many of the local landlords were already familiar with the advantages of the large-scale farming common in other countries, without corvée labour, and therefore no longer considered the retention of the peasants as the only tools of agriculture to be absolutely necessary. Hence, they were not unsettled by the migrations of the peasants that also took place here, and that the peasants did not yet dare to migrate to distant regions, but usually restricted such migrations, either due to a lack of means or due to the shyness still attached to them from the time of serfdom, to the neighbourhood."

However, these reasons do not seem to be entirely exhaustive, and the first one is incorrect, because a size difference between the estates of that time in Poznań compared to those in inner Poland is unlikely to be demonstrable. Moreover, it seems strange that the peasants in Poznań should have been too timid to make use of a freedom available to them to the same extent as the peasants in the eastern departments, who were certainly not at a higher level of culture. The number of landlords who would have liked to have dispensed with managing their estates through corvée labour is also unlikely to have been as large as Plichta assumes.

Much more plausible is what Zerboni di Sposetti, the later Chief President of the Province of Poznań, states in an opinion.[^182] He says, “The peasants in Poznań partly had no knowledge of the freedom they had gained,” which already sounds credible for the reason that the authorities who should have informed them of this law were precisely the landlords in their capacity as village chiefs, who had an interest in keeping their peasants without reducing their obligations; “also, the peasant, who wanted to leave his previous conditions and enter into more favourable ones on another estate, was taught that no changes of ownership were permissible during the war.” It is understandable that these statements usually sufficed to persuade the peasant, accustomed to blind obedience to his master, to stay.

“One would have found the peasants in the Grand Duchy of Poznań,” Zerboni continues, “more enlightened about their situation, in a more favourable position, had the Royal Saxon decree fully taken effect. Human labour is an extremely sought-after commodity in Poland, for which every estate owner competes against the other. This decree could only bring about beneficial consequences for the peasants.”

In the hope that landlords and peasants would conclude contracts for the properties, which had previously been made available for an indefinite period, officials had been sent to the various districts of the Grand Duchy according to Article IV, Section 2 of the ordinance, to record such contracts; however, this task made no progress in Poznań, and neither landlords nor peasants seemed inclined to put their mutual obligations into writing. It was in the interest of the landlords to keep as much leeway for their arbitrariness as possible; the Polish peasants, on the other hand, who generally could neither write nor read, had great respect for the written word and were very reluctant to sign contracts, behind which they always suspected deceit.

In the Netze District, as already mentioned, the law seems to have actually led to a reduction in the burdens on the peasants. In the deliberations of the Bromberg Law Commission (1818), the member from the class of estate owners, Mr. von Loga, states that it is the general wish of the estate owners that in the event of upcoming redemptions, the services and burdens of the peasant holdings should be assessed according to the status quo of 1806, as after this time 'due to the burdens of war,' the peasant obligations have been almost universally reduced. One is probably not mistaken to see this reduction in obligations as a result of the ordinance of December 21, 1807, and not as a consequence of the war burdens, a view also expressed by the Poznań Law Commission, since the war burdens would have only caused extraordinary, momentary, not permanent remissions. But even if the war burdens would have caused this reduction in peasant obligations, which is hardly to be assumed, one would still see the beneficial effect of the Grand Ducal Warsaw ordinance in that the estate owners had not been able to raise the obligations again later, as this would have risked the departure of the peasants and thus of their necessary labour force.

The reinstatement of the peasant protection, exercised during part of the time of the first Prussian rule, took place automatically through the repeal of the Prussian laws. The official reports of later times contain no information that would suggest that this repeal had a particularly detrimental effect on the existence of the peasant holdings, as Klebs claims.

Incidentally, there were also efforts at that time to make it obligatory for the estate owners to maintain the peasant holdings. Thus, the Bromberg government[^183] says in a report of May 3, 1816: “We have found in the files of the dissolved prefectural authorities general decrees by the same, which prohibit the withdrawal of rustic holdings for manor land, but there is no legal provision presented to justify these decrees,” etc.

The content of the Warsaw legislation can thus be briefly summarised as follows: all peasants were made personally free, the subject associations and all obligations arising from them were abolished, village communities were created but without a good community constitution, and full freedom of movement for service peasants was introduced for an indefinite period.

The effect of these changes in the Poznań Department was virtually nil or at least very low, whereas in the Netze District, as a result, there has been an almost universal reduction in peasant obligations.

It is permissible to point out here the inaccuracies contained in Klebs' depiction of this legislative era.

Klebs says that the Warsaw legislation led to all the formerly subject peasants, who under Prussian rule could only be dispossessed of their holdings by judgement and law, now being able to be dispossessed at the landlord's discretion after one year's notice, and that the abolition of peasant protection greatly disadvantaged them.

The peasant-friendly provisions of the land law had indeed, as already mentioned, fallen out of effect due to the repeal of all Prussian laws. The loss of peasant protection was therefore a mere coincidence, not an intentional abandonment of peasant lands. This abolition hardly had any adverse influence during the Warsaw era, as Klebs claims; rather, the trend towards dispossession of peasants only gained momentum after 1815\. “Withdrawals of peasant holdings have occurred here and there under the protection of laws under the previous (Warsaw) government,” says a well-informed contemporary,[^184] so certainly not on a large scale.

The provisions on permissible termination do not concern, as Klebs suggests (he also incorrectly speaks of a one-year termination right created by the law), "all former subjects," but only those who held their positions for an indefinite period. This does not include the inheritable tenants and also not the contract farmers (time lessees). Klebs seems to have come to his understanding because the article of the law speaks of "property rights forever or for a certain time," when it should have more accurately said "rights to the position forever or for a definite time;" because it refers to all farmers except those who are sitting "at the mere favour of the lord" for an indefinite period. The law is characterised by such faulty expressions. For example, it always uses the term "hereditary lord" and "new hereditary lord," although the law specifically abolishes hereditary subjugation; it mentions "land ownership, which consists of the courtyard, the buildings, and the sowing," from which one could conclude that the fields and meadows, etc., were not included, and so on. However, it emerges from the entire content of the law that its provisions only apply to the one class of rural inhabitants set for an indefinite period, and that disadvantages for the other classes, which had no property, cannot be derived from it, as Klebs portrays this to us in the most horrifying images.

All officials who lived through that time either assume this as self-evident or explicitly point it out. For example, Appellate Court Counselor Fischer says:[^185] "The ordinance refers, as far as it speaks of farmers and their possessions, only to the peasant owners who had manorial farms for cultivation and use for services to be performed for an indefinite period."

Klebs concludes as a result of his, as shown above, incorrect understanding: "Even those residents who held their positions with a hereditary right of use and often had their own buildings and inventory, could be removed after the expiry of one year. In several cases, the landlords exercised their rights, but even more often, the same was used to increase the peasants' burdens, which was previously forbidden."

I have previously explained that hereditary corvée farmers, at least in South Prussia, were almost only present in the crown lands. If there were any in noble estates, they usually bought the inventory and the courtyard, and the land was then given to them hereditarily. None of the contemporaries of that time say anything about their rights or those of the time lessees being curtailed during the Grand Duchy of Warsaw, even though numerous essays by officials and private individuals are included in the files I used. Nor is there anywhere talk of an increase in burdens during the Warsaw era, but rather often of a reduction of the same.

Klebs provides this flawed depiction of the impact of the Warsaw legislation, whose good side and intentions, which I have highlighted above, he does not recognize.

Knapp[^186] has given a judgement on the Warsaw legislation, which he knows from Dönniges' brief description;[^187] wherein he characteristically "points out" and gives the following sentences: "From December 21, 1808, the former subjects, now completely free people, were no longer protected if they did not hold their possessions hereditarily, but only for an indefinite period; the former hereditary lord could terminate their tenure, and it might have been poor consolation that they could also terminate his. They were free and at the same time uprooted."

"Through this, the Polish landlords had achieved exactly what those in the province of Prussia were also expecting at that time, but could not obtain so unconditionally: 'Freedom for the peasants, but the land for us.'"

"Not as if they had immediately taken possession of all peasant land; they did it only as needed."

I have previously explained that there was by no means the level of interest among the landlords in taking over peasant lands, as Knapp assumes. The peasants were no more or less "uprooted" after December 21, 1808, than they had always been until the introduction of peasant protection in South Prussian times (1797). It was also not such a poor consolation for them to have the right to terminate the landlords, because from my previous explanations it follows that in certain regions it was precisely the peasants who made the most extensive use of this right, while the landlords, precisely because they usually had an interest in keeping their peasants, often found themselves compelled to reduce their services. On the other hand, there is nowhere to be seen that the landlords had already frequently exercised their right to terminate and had taken over the properties.

"The majority of the residents have been in undisturbed possession of their farms for a long period of time, despite the termination rights granted to the landlords by law; most of them do not even know how very insecure their ownership rights are," it says at one point in the files.[^188]

The claim that the landlords in the province of Prussia had the tendency at that time to give “freedom to the peasants, the land for us,” also seems to me to be an erroneous exaggeration; for from Knapp's source[^189] excerpts it only becomes apparent that the nobility's aspiration was to gain freer control over the peasant lands.

The wishes aimed in that direction appear, when considering the conditions after the devastating war, to be partly justified for that time and have also been appropriately considered in the edict of October 9, 1807, and the accompanying regulations. However, to the extent that they go beyond the bounds of what is justified, they still do not, in my opinion, allow for the exaggerated conclusion that Knapp draws, namely that the landlords generally aspired to take the peasants' land for themselves in return for the freedom of the peasants—i.e., to completely eliminate the peasant class from the world. A sharp, albeit not total, conflict of interest between peasants and landlords emerged, in my view, only when the project of the proprietary allocation of lands to the corvée farmers was raised by the state. At that time, the landlords feared that their previously undisputed property rights to the “for cultivation and use” leased-out peasant farms would be taken away from them. Not only the fact of the impending state intervention in the property rights of the landlords but also the circumstance that they feared they would not be sufficiently compensated, and also considered most of the service peasants incapable of managing independently and properly fulfilling their new rental payments; moreover, that they faced a shortage of labour and a complete, costly, forced overhaul of their farms, make the existential struggle at that time seem as understandable as it is incomprehensible why the landlords should have previously sought to acquire all the peasants' lands as compensation for their personal freedom.

In 1813, the Grand Duchy of Warsaw was occupied and organised by Russia. Russian authorities also dealt with the peasant conditions, although no significant reforms occurred in the short period until the Treaty of Paris.
