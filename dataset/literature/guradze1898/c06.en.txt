# Guradze (1898), Chapter IV.
# translation by Claude

# **Chapter IV.** {#chapter-iv.}

The Peasant Legislation for South Prussia from 1793 to 1806\.

The province of South Prussia included the areas of the current Posen region, as well as parts of the current Bromberg region, including the old Wongrowitz and Gnesen districts with Powidz. Additionally, a significant portion of what is now the Kingdom of Poland was part of South Prussia, but it will not be considered in our discussions.

The takeover of South Prussia was carried out through the patent of April 25, 1793.[^121] In South Prussia, too, the peasants benefited significantly from the change in regime. The transition from serfdom[^122] to mere subjection and the provision of legal protection for all residents of the land were the first significant steps towards improvement.

Even through the 'Notificationspatent of May 8, 1793, regarding the organisation of ecclesiastical and secular justice,'[^123] an improvement in the peasant conditions was achieved, as according to the provisions of Section 8, the landowners entitled to exercise jurisdiction could only do so in the future through certified experts and individuals sworn in for justice.

The intention expressed in the "Huldigungspatent" of March 25, 1793, that all residents, without exception, should be protected in their possessions and rights, seems to have given many peasants the impression that they were now liberated from all manorial authority. This led them to become disobedient, refusing their duties and services, and appropriating rights to pastures, and so on, without further ado. This behaviour resulted in a patent issued on July 12, 1793,[^124] strictly prohibiting such actions. At the same time, it was announced that the king intended to thoroughly examine and establish the contributions and rights of all subjects through special commissions. Until this laborious task was completed, everyone was required to continue providing the same contributions as in 1792 and not unlawfully claim additional benefits. If a lordship imposed more services on the subjects, the government would assess them against it.

There is a threat of severe corporal punishment and even life-long hard labour against the "Winkelkonsulenten,"[^125] and a warning to the people about them.

The implementation of the general determination of peasant services in "Urbarien" (land registers) was, like many other planned reforms, abandoned due to the uprising of 1794\. However, the principle was maintained for the legal system that unilateral increases in peasant services by the landlords during Prussian rule would be considered unacceptable.

The government's intention to improve the peasants' situation in every respect is also evident from the way they began to prepare tax assessments[^126] before the outbreak of the uprising. They deducted one grain from the gross yield of grain for the peasants for sowing and ⅔ of the remainder as operating expenses, unlike, for example, in Silesia, where only half was deducted. Furthermore, they calculated lower selling prices for the remaining grain for sale than for the landlords and deducted the value of the lord's dues and the church tithe without taking into account the lord's obligations. It is clear that according to these principles, a net profit was rarely obtained, so a usable and fair basis for taxation could hardly be found. Instead, it only stirred up resentment among the nobility, who, in turn, should pay significantly more taxes than during the time of the Polish kingdom, namely about 2½ times as much.

It appears that the Urbarienkommission, which was responsible for determining the duties and obligations of landowners and subjects,[^127] did not resume its activities after the uprising of 1794\. At least, official records of the rural contributions were not established until 1806.[^128]

Despite the "Allgemeine Landrecht" (General Land Law) for South Prussia only being introduced in 1794 on a subsidiary basis and later established as statutory law in 1797, the legislation was still focused on fulfilling the king's wish to provide all possible benefits to the peasants without abolishing their subordination.[^129]

The edict of March 26, 1794[^130] stipulated in Section 1 that the existing Polish laws would remain in force until the issuance of a provincial code. However, Section 2 listed exceptions to this, including "1. everything concerning the judicial system, 2\. anything contrary to a Prussian prohibition law, 3\. contrary to the form of government in general, and 4\. contrary to principles that require equal treatment for all."

In Section 3, the preparation of a provincial code was mandated, and in Sections 4-6, it was determined that the Trębicki Code should be legally applied, with the "General Land Law" serving as subsidiary legislation for cases after July 1, 1794\.

The provisions of Article 2 were particularly important for the legal status of the peasants. Equal rights were to be granted to everyone, which meant that even the serf, who had no legal standing, could now file a lawsuit against the landlord. Furthermore, a Prussian prohibition law stated:[^131] "The former serfdom, as a form of personal slavery, does not apply to the subject inhabitants of the flat country," etc. Since according to Clause 3 of Article 2 of the Edict of March 26, 1794, no institution that contradicted a Prussian prohibition law was to remain in effect, the serfdom of the noble estate peasants came to a complete end from that day on. Instead, only the obligation to perform labour services on the land, as established by the ordinance of November 8, 1773, remained, just as it did in the Netze District. Another prohibition law can be found in Part II, Title 7, Section 14, and following of the "General Land Law," which introduced "peasant protection." It was indeed enforced by the War and Domain Chambers, ensuring that no additional estates were seized, left fallow, merged, or that capable estates were converted into corvée labour[^132] services, as confirmed by several reports in the records.

The 'Patent on the Ownership and Management of Starosties in South Prussia' dated February 26, 1794,[^133] had already thoroughly addressed the situation of peasants in the royal starosties. It stated:

* Section 10: We are particularly concerned about the subjects and residents of the starosties and their well-being; therefore, we want them not only to be preserved by the starosty owners but also to promote their prosperity to the best of their ability and pay close attention to this.  
* Section 27: The starosty owners are obligated to keep all subject farms properly maintained and occupied by competent tenants and not let them go to waste.  
* Section 28: Without the knowledge and consent of the chamber, no subject may be evicted or relocated, under penalty of severe punishment.  
* Section 29: The starosty owners should ensure that the subjects properly maintain the buildings, livestock, and fields.  
* Section 30: However, they must treat the subjects with consideration both in this oversight and in general, as fellow human beings. Chamber commissioners shall compile precise prestation tables. No additional services beyond those listed therein may be demanded from the subjects. Jurisdiction remained with the starosts but should only be exercised by qualified jurists, and that in a 'prompt and impartial' manner.

The "Declaration of the Edict of March 28, 1794," dated April 30, 1797,[^134] stated that since it was a fundamental principle of the Prussian state to grant legal hearings to everyone without distinction of status, a significant portion of the Polish constitutions was inapplicable. Therefore, the "General Land Law" would serve as the basis for all future legal actions in South Prussia. Only for some matters were the Polish rights retained. This included, according to Section 9, "the laws and customs concerning the hereditary subjects," but it was expressly mentioned that this retention naturally excluded those who were already listed in the Patent of March 26, 1794, Section 2 (as mentioned above).

In the same paragraph, it was also announced that general provisions, extending to the entire relationship, would be issued regarding the constitution of hereditary subjects. Additionally, Government Councillor von Greveniz (later Privy Chief Justice and author of the book "Der Bauer in Polen") was tasked with collecting the material for the Provincial Code and drafting it.[^135] However, during the South Prussian period, neither the Provincial Code was completed nor did the promised regulation, which was supposed to elucidate the entire relationship of hereditary subjects, appear.

On the other hand, special regulations were issued that concerned specific aspects of peasant legal relationships and are of great interest. I will list them here to the extent that I was able to obtain them.

By a decree of the Department of Justice[^136] to the government in Petrikau on September 21, 1797,[^137] it was ordered that while owners of noble estates were still allowed to reserve one or another subject when selling them, it should only be under the following conditions:

1. The subject had to give their free consent;  
2. They must not have yet inherited a position;  
3. They must either leave the old subject association and become a personally free servant, or receive a permit to work elsewhere, thus remaining within the old subject association, or voluntarily enter the subject association of another estate of their lord's, "for a state in which one person is solely dependent on another exhausts the proper concept of slavery, which is no longer tolerated in any relationship and cannot be assumed even by consent or contract."  
4. The subject must not be taken out of the country.

The amount of redemption money for the subjects of South Prussia was fixed according to the customary rates in Silesia by a Most High Rescript dated March 3, 1800.[^138] Therefore, from that point on and until the issuance of the provincial law code, the following payments were to be made: One man 6 Thalers, one woman or a boy under 14 years 3 Thalers, and a girl under 12 years 1 Thaler and 8 Groschen.

This Most High Regulation was prompted by the South Prussian government, which suggested that, "in the absence of specific Polish laws determining the amount of redemption money for subjects of the land, the rates introduced in neighbouring Silesia should be adopted until the issuance of the Provincial Land Law." It had happened that a General N. had demanded 30 Thalers in ransom from a subject who was engaged to a shoemaker. However, when the matter was brought before the authorities in Poznań, the demand was reduced to 15 Thalers. The local authorities had subsequently inquired with the King and made the above proposals. By accepting them, the redemption money was reduced to 3 Thalers, which is one-tenth of the initial demand. These rates were indeed exceptionally low; in West Prussia, more than three times as much was paid, specifically 20 Thalers for a man and 10 Thalers for a woman.[^139]

The processes initiated by the Hauländer communities against their landlords due to the imposition of increased services and dues, which contradicted the content of their privileges during Polish times, were numerous. A document from Justice Commissioner Stenger in Unruhstadt[^140] in 1798 describes the circumstances as follows: The Hauländer privileges, which were often very obscure in expression, only contained obligations related to rent payments. These obligations were so minimal that it would be astonishing how the Polish landowners could give up so much for so little, if one did not consider that their main goal was to obtain cash income, and that at that time, they managed their estates with the services of the subordinate peasants. When there was a desire for expansion and improvement of their estates, and the services of the subjects were no longer sufficient, the Hauländers were initially approached "at their request" for help, "in the end, it indeed became a right, and the Hauländers had nothing left but to be thankful if not too much was demanded from them." As soon as it was "publicised from the pulpits and everywhere in Prussia that everyone should be protected in their rights and privileges," the Hauländers were so quickly agitated that they not only sought legal assistance based on their privileges but also immediately became defiant, abruptly stopped serving, and preferred military execution for two to three weeks rather than agreeing to a settlement. The author then states that "the question of whether the Hauländer privileges can and must be the sole norm for deciding Hauländer cases" is superfluous to discuss since the statute of limitations had already been accepted in practice in this context as well.

Regarding these statements, it should be noted first that the assertion that there were no service obligations in the privileges may only apply to the limited number of privileges known to the author from his judicial activities. It cannot be unilaterally assumed that the petitionary services of the Hauländers always originated from originally voluntary support and became customary law. This is contradicted by the fact that, as already mentioned in the Holländerprivilegien of the Netze District cited by Beheim-Schwarzbach, services were often stipulated from the beginning, which were referred to as petitionary services, even though they were by no means voluntary. Since the Hauländer estates in South Prussia mostly originated at the end of the seventeenth and beginning of the eighteenth centuries, around the same time as those in the Netze District, it is hardly conceivable that such service obligations would not have been found in some privileges in South Prussia as well.[^141]

Furthermore, there was naturally also the possibility that along with increased services and dues, increased obligations from the lordship, such as granting more land, providing greater grazing or forest rights, etc., had been assumed. In the third line, there were then the petitionary or forced services imposed by the landlord[^142] through unilateral acts of power. Now, based on Stenger's explanations, it seems that in practice, until 1798 when his essay was published, the statute of limitations was assumed in favour of the landlords everywhere, and Meisner also adopted this view, as if the claims for reduced services had been "definitively rejected"[^143] altogether. However, this was by no means the case; on the contrary, the higher authority, the government in Posen, based on its experience with the relationships of the manors not only with their serf subjects but also with the German[^144] Holländers during Polish times, adopted the following principles:[^145]

1. That the voluntary assumption of multiple services and dues contrary to the explicit content of a Hauländer community's privilege would not be presumed, but rather such agreements, in which no coercion or threat had occurred, must be expressly proven by the landlord;  
2. That such an agreement would be presumed if the lordship, at the time of increasing the dues and services, had granted more land or other benefits to the Hauländer;  
3. That if the lordship did not provide the evidence mentioned in points 1 and 2, it could not bona fide claim possession of multiple services and dues against the privilege;  
4. That the Hauländers could not sue their lordship during Polish times, and;  
5. Therefore, no statute of limitations applied against them.

The principles communicated in a report by the government of Poznan on April 15, 1799, were approved by the Law Commission in an opinion dated July 2, 1800, and established as a legal norm through a decree from the Ministry of Justice on July 14, 1800, until the promulgation of the South Prussian provincial law.

This was of the utmost practical importance because, according to these principles,[^146] the Hauländer (German colonists), settlers, and other property owners were able to regain the rights and freedoms granted to them through privileges and contracts through court decisions. Oberappellationsgerichtsrath Fischer in Poznan wrote about this in 1819, stating that the records of the former Poznan government bore witness to it. However, the matter was so widely known that it was unnecessary to specify individual cases and provide the rulings.

Another court decree dated December 12, 1800,[^147] concerning the interpretation of Section 2 of the Edict of March 28, 1794, stated the following: "It must be considered as established 1\. that it is a principle entirely incompatible with the current constitution if the landed estates were to wantonly dismiss the subjects of their noble estates in South Prussia as per the former Polish custom; that, therefore, this custom, according to the provision of Section 2 of the Edict of March 28, 1794, deserves no consideration in terms of the laws and rights that should be applied in South Prussia, and that the dismissal, in case of refusal on the part of the subjects, can only be done through judgement and legal process and for reasons deemed sufficient under the General Land Law."

"From this, it follows 2\. that the subjects, when they claim that they cannot continue to perform the services and obligations imposed on them without being ruined and deprived of their means of subsistence, must be heard with an impossibility claim according to the rules of the Court Regulations Part I, Title 41, Section 66 et seq. and the General Land Law Part II, Title 7, Section 444 et seq. and Section 459 et seq."

"However, 3\. all services and obligations that the manorial estates have previously held, until the local provincial law determines otherwise, must continue to be fulfilled. Neither the brevity of time since their increase nor the provision of fewer services and obligations in neighbouring places can be a reason for complaint. Only impossibility can justify a claim."

With the 'brevity of time' mentioned in the last sentence, only the Polish period before 1793 can be meant, as the principle was indeed adopted in the legal system that a unilateral increase of services \[i.e., duties or obligations\] after the subsidiary introduction of the General Land Law would have been impermissible.

In the above-mentioned essay by Oberappellationsgerichtsrath Fischer, an example is cited of a subject \[farmer or peasant\] lawsuit in which arbitrary increases in services were reversed.[^148] The justification for the verdict stated: “The defendant (landowner) has not proven that the services that have been performed from time immemorial were changed in a lawful manner, and is therefore not authorised to arbitrarily demand more from the plaintiff.”

In another lawsuit cited there, a position given out for an indefinite period for cultivation was considered hereditary based on Section 634, Part I, Title 21 of the General Land Law. This verdict was confirmed in the appellate review; a settlement was reached in the appellate instance.

A court decree dated February 8, 1803,[^149] stipulated that to convert an individually owned or emphyteutic peasant's position into a burdened peasant's position, as well as for the exchange of already cultivated land for new and cleared land, with the reservation of the right of sale, the approval of the competent government is required.

Through this, the method previously often practised of reclaiming cultivated fields from socage farmers and assigning them new land in return was significantly restricted. Completely prohibiting the relocation was, of course, not feasible, as such a ban would have made any separation impossible.

A service that the Prussian state had to impose on the farmers of South Prussia was the "Vorspann" (harness duty). The frequent travels, which especially in the time of the organisation of the province had to be undertaken by civil and military servants (officials) in the interest of the state, made this arrangement absolutely necessary. At that time, when there were still hardly any public transport facilities to many places, the state was forced to make use of the subjects' horses for travels necessary in the general interest. To acquire and station state horses in sufficient numbers for this purpose in all places would have been associated with enormous costs, and the province would have had to bear the costs for it. Therefore, it was the cheapest and most practical, as in all other Prussian lands, to also establish a harness duty system here. However, to prevent any misuse from the outset, a "Harness Duty Regulation for South Prussia"[^150] was already issued on October 1, 1793, which contains extraordinarily considerate and precise provisions, compared to the regulations applicable in other provinces. All farmers and most cities were subject to harness duty. The royal, noble, and ecclesiastical estates were exempt from this service because the estates only kept the horses most essential to the economy, which, when claimed, were usually also the property of the socage farmers. Nevertheless, one could speak of a preference for larger estates, which was due to the corresponding provisions of the other Prussian provinces and was by no means significant in South Prussia, as the harness duty was not only sufficiently paid for by the state to the farmers but was also deducted as a public burden in the tax assessments. The distances to be covered from one relay to the next were only 2, at most 3 miles; only in very urgent cases could the traveller, if the horses were not yet present at the next relay, demand to continue travelling to at most the second station against advance payment of the usual rates for extra posts. Since the speed was set at at least 1½ hours per mile (7.5 km), and one didn't even have to drive "that fast" on bad roads, and the harness duty regulation even pointed out the payment of tips, the harness duty may often not have been a nuisance, but a very pleasant change for the farmers. The harness duty could only be demanded on the basis of passes, which had to be issued by the king himself or the General Directorate,[^151] and of which the chambers always kept some for accounting in stock. The horses should not be on-site earlier or later than one hour before the time they were needed and had to wait up to a maximum of 24 hours, as delays of travellers were often inevitable due to the poor roads at that time. No harness duty recipient was allowed to hit or have hit the harness drivers or their horses, drive themselves or have someone else drive, and everyone was liable for damage resulting from "exertion" of horses. No locality should be prevented from introducing oxen instead of horses for harnessing in their economies; but also, in the state's interest, no horses could be abolished anywhere without the permission of the chambers.

It goes without saying that especially in the initial period after the introduction of the harness duty, abuses and improprieties occurred. For example, overly zealous harness duty expeditions once ordered horses for Minister von Voß a full 24 hours too early,[^152] which resulted in such behaviour being sharply criticised by the Minister. In general, the arrangement was urgently needed in the interest of the community, a necessary evil, and the farmers and other harness duty providers were adequately compensated for it, which was not the case in the old provinces.

In the years 1803–1806, a regulation was prepared[^153] which was to make the provisions contained in the General Land Law regarding the conditions of the peasant class, with some modifications, explicitly the guideline for South Prussia until the promulgation of provincial law. However, the appearance of this regulation was thwarted by the outbreak of the war in 1806 and the resulting end of Prussian rule.

In the report submitted on this matter by the authorities, it was stated that they did not yet consider it advisable to grant the subjects hereditary rights to their holdings in general, since they were far behind in cultivation, lived in the greatest indolence, and could not make beneficial use of property if it were granted to them.

As for the domain peasants, as mentioned above, provisions for their preservation and improvement had been made by the patent of February 26, 1794\. The war and domain chambers supervised this. According to the declaration of July 28, 1796, the royal estates initially left to the starosts were confiscated, and the owners were compensated. At the same time, most of the ecclesiastical estates were also confiscated, and the previous mode—that they had to pay 50% of their estimated net income as tax to the state—was reversed, so that from then on, the clergy received said 50%, which was naturally the smaller half of the actual income, as salary from the state. Most of these estates were turned into domains, but some were given away to state officials or sold well below their value. To these domains were added the estates confiscated from some Polish nobles who had been in rebellion in 1794 and the manor of Krotoschin belonging to the maritime trade.

The conditions of the subjects in the domains[^154] varied according to the different origins of these estates. Steps to organise and improve them were soon taken by the authorities. Above all, the immediate preparation of performance tables and registers (Urbarien) was undertaken, using any existing contracts and Polish lustrations as a basis. The services were thereby reduced to a moderate amount, even if they remained significantly higher than in the domains of West Prussia.

At that time, the question was also seriously considered whether or not to grant domain peasants, who did not own their holdings, property rights to them. However, the opinions solicited from the authorities mostly indicated that the majority of peasants of Polish nationality were at such a low level of cultural development that they would hardly be able to maintain and supplement buildings and inventory in the long term, which of course would be a natural consequence of granting ownership. Therefore, for the time being, general ownership grants were avoided and initially limited to the hereditary, but mostly not proprietary, granting of those holdings where the best farmers resided.

Minister von Voß, referring to a report from the Posen Domain Chamber, instructed in a directive dated May 25, 1799, to consider hereditary and proprietary granting of peasant farms in the domains. Where such had not yet been initiated, subject peasants who bore the burdens (Lastbauern) in the domains were to be treated according to the declaration of March 27, 1790\. The chambers should primarily put competent farmers into a condition, through repairs to the inventory and reduction of the levies where they were "excessive," so that they could maintain their farms themselves. Later on, proprietary granting should be proceeded with. The necessary financial support would be approved by the ministry. In the letters of acceptance to be issued,[^155] it should be stipulated that the estates must not be indebted, and that the farm must be bequeathed to an heir, chosen by the domain intendant, undivided, along with the inventory.

The proprietary granting should proceed according to the regulations of Intendant von Haugwitz and be done in such a way that, with every change of ownership and with every new building, a deed of purchase would be issued to the farmer, which bequeathed the holding to him hereditarily. In these deeds of sale, all burdens should be noted, and an appropriate laudemium (a fee paid to the lord upon the transfer of property) agreed upon for the case of sale, as well as obligating the farmers to maintain the buildings themselves, practise good husbandry, and accept changes to the registers (Urbarien) if such were agreed upon with the entire community.[^156]

Moreover, it was emphasised everywhere that the royal treasury should not suffer any permanent losses due to the proprietary granting.

All these reforms carried out to improve the rural conditions in South Prussia did not quite reach the level of those in the Netze District.

The levy conditions of the noble socage farmers of the Netze District had been fixed and regulated everywhere by written, mutually binding contracts, and in part even by state-set registers (Urbarien).

In South Prussia, on the other hand, the levy conditions that were customary during Polish times continued to exist. That is, there was general uncertainty everywhere, the same lack of written contracts as before, unless a regulation and determination had been made voluntarily or judicially in the case of a pre-emption lawsuit or a lawsuit for arbitrary increase in levies. Moreover, the material well-being of the domain farmers had not been provided for to the same extent as in the Netze District. Nevertheless, the reforms of the South Prussian era achieved a still quite significant extent.

With the noble socage farmers, Prussia had especially abolished serfdom, brought about legal capability, protection against increased levies and against overburdening, as well as against arbitrary dismissal. Also, the excesses of the estate owners against the privileges of personally free owners during Polish times had been redressed. The services of the domain farmers were fixed and reduced, and the property rights of many had been improved.

Immediately after the outbreak of the war of 1806, the Prussian authorities of the province were put out of operation. Until the Treaty of Tilsit, thus during the time of the activity of the "Warsaw governing commission," no noteworthy regulations concerning the peasants were issued, except that a regulation of the same on January 6, 1807, stipulated that the exclusive jurisdiction of all peasants should be with the patrimonial courts.
