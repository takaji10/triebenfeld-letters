Most families were not so protected. The constant division of property to provide children with their share of the family holdings meant that little attention was given to the consolidation of blocks of land, and contiguous estates – even parts of the same village – acquired at different times often continued to be administered separately.[^78] Partible inheritance made a great deal of sense: no landowner who held more than one or two villages could hope to administer his lands as a rational block; even in the entails much of the land was not administered directly. Division between sons and daughters of wealthy landowners therefore did not necessarily affect the viability of individual estates or groups of estates: it may even have enhanced it. It did, however, make it difficult for children to sustain the standard of living achieved by their parents.

Even the wealthiest faced enormous problems. Much is often made of the maintenance of 'private armies' by magnates, especially in the light of the State's lack of a large standing army: the existence of these forces is taken to demonstrate the weakness of central power and the increasingly dominant position of the great magnates; after 1945, Polish historians were quick to claim that the large private forces of magnates were part of the structure of feudal oppression.[^79] The term 'private army' is, however, at best unhelpful and at worst downright misleading. While it is true that one or two of the wealthiest Lithuanian and Ukrainian magnates did maintain small garrisons organised and equipped on western lines – such as the Radziwiłł garrison at Słuck – the forces that could be raised by what was always the tiniest of minorities even among the aristocracy were far from being professional, modern armies, and they were certainly not standing forces on any scale. Historians have been too quick to accept impressionistic estimates of numbers and the hearsay evidence of diaries, in the absence of detailed muster rolls; where these do exist, the numbers of such private forces come tumbling down. It is true that, on particular occasions, such as royal elections, a handful of magnates could and did show up with impressive retinues. It is claimed that there were 60,000 private troops at the 1632 election of Władysław IV: 10,000 with Krzysztof II Radziwiłł, and 6000 each with Stanisław Konopczynski and one of the Leszczyńskis. Similar numbers are claimed for the 1669 election of Michael Korybut Wiśniowiecki, with Bogusław and Michał Kazimierz Radziwiłł bringing nearly 8000 men, as did Krzysztof and Michał Kazimierz Pac, while Jan Sobieski and Dymitr Wiśniowiecki brought 4000 each.[^80] Yet such figures are barely credible, and no magnate, however wealthy, was capable of maintaining forces of anything like this size.
