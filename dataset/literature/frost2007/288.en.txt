If the magnates did seize control of the ‘helm of state' to rule in their own interests, why did this powerful oligarchy allow the collapse of central government? Second, it proved remarkably difficult to agree on the attributes of a magnate. It is impossible to define any élite group on the basis purely of landholding, since the value of property varied greatly from one part of the Commonwealth to another: Dworzaczek calculated that a nobleman from Little Poland would have to own twenty villages to enjoy the same income as his counterpart from Great Poland who merely owned ten.[^63] Senate membership was important, but the Senate was small – only 150-strong at its peak in the seventeenth century, of which 52 were minor castellans of lesser prestige – and the practice of life-tenure of office meant that turnover was slow. Many members of wealthy and powerful families were therefore not senators or dignitaries; indeed many senators, particularly the minor castellans, were of comparatively humble backgrounds. Other attributes were therefore suggested as vital to the categorization of this élite, including birth, connections, lifestyle, education and standing amongst the local szlachta, but are too subjective and imprecise to be particularly helpful.[^64]

The problem with attempts to delineate a magnate élite is that they assume a stability at the top level of noble society which did not exist. Although social life and politics were dominated in particular periods by a tiny élite of families and individuals, this élite was subject to constant change, with only a handful of families maintaining their position unchallenged.[^65] There is a natural tendency within any aristocracy for families to rise, decline or die out, but certain peculiar features of the Commonwealth's political and social structure ensured that the instability within its élite was greater than elsewhere in Europe and does much to explain why noble society in the Commonwealth failed to settle down into the stability and relative calm experienced in other noble-dominated societies between the mid-seventeenth century and the French Revolution.

No family could take its position for granted. Office may have performed a roughly similar function to titles in other nobilities with regard to the delineation of status, but its possession was more significant than that: it helped maintain influence at local level, while the importance to the monarchy of Senate support ensured that senators increasingly monopolized leases on royal land: the percentage of starosties in the hands of Polish senators and central dignitaries between 1697 and 1763 varied from palatinate to palatinate, but nowhere was it lower than 56 per cent (palatinate of Sieradz) and the highest figure was in the Rawa palatinate, where it reached 99 per cent. Out of 22 palatinates in the kingdom of Poland, in nine the figure was between 90 and 99 per cent; in five it was between 80 and 89 per cent, in six it was between 70 and 79 per cent; only in the palatinates of Sieradz and Podlasie (62 per cent) was it under 70 per cent. And 79 per cent of government ministers, 43 per cent of palatines and 30 per cent of central dignitaries held starosties valued at over 10,000 zloties in 1771.[^66]
