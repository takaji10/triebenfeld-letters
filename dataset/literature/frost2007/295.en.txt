Some families were thus able to avoid the worst consequences of subdivision, and individual starosties could become all but hereditary in certain families, at least for a period.[^89] In the eighteenth century the rich starosty of Hrubieszów in the palatinate of Ruthenia was held successively by Feliks Potocki, his son and grandson. Feliks's nephew Józef held three great starosties in the same palatinate (Leżajsk, Halicz and Śniatyrń). All three were passed on to his son, Stanisław, palatine of Poznań, who had to divide them since he had four sons, none of whom became a senator.[^90]

Nevertheless, if a tiny group of families undoubtedly grabbed more than its share of starosties, competition was so intense that the king had a great deal of room for manoeuvre. Despite the widespread practice of cession in favour, each transaction had to be confirmed by the royal chancery. Approval was usually forthcoming, even if payment in one form or another might be exacted. Nobody, however, could rely on passing on a starosty to their children, and starosties often passed out of the hands of families that had held them for a generation or more: the starosty of Bila Tserkva, the richest in the Commonwealth, was held successively by Jan Stanisław Jabłonowski, Polish Grand Hetman under John III Sobieski and Augustus II, and his son, Stanisław Wincenty, but on the latter's death in 1754 it was conferred on Jerzy Mniszech, despite the fact that Stanisław had a son, Antoni, who was allowed to keep two of his father's starosties, but lost Bila Tserkva, probably because of his father's long opposition to Augustus III.

Thus, for all the apparent advantages of the wealthiest groups in society, they never constituted a stable economic élite. Despite a dearth of studies, there is some evidence – not least the number of new men rising to senatorial rank throughout the period – to suggest that the middling nobility, while facing enormous problems between 1648 and 1721, was remarkably resilient. Contrary to the impression which is often given, the middle and lesser nobility held most of the cultivated land in private ownership: about 80 per cent in Great Poland at the end of the sixteenth century.[^91] One study of the district of Kalisz – which unfortunately only goes up to 1655 – suggests that the middle nobility devised strategies to avoid the worst effects of partible inheritance. Despite Kula's view that land was not a commodity, the land market was extremely lively, and although there was considerable activity by richer nobles, most transactions involved the middle nobility. A large number of sales took place within the family, as older siblings took advantage of their position to buy out the portions assigned to their younger brothers and ensure that divisions of the family property were limited or transient.[^92] The lack of studies of the economic activities of lesser landholders – particularly of those who leased or rented their lands – makes it difficult to reach firm conclusions, but it is clear that they were highly active economically: leases were often short, litigation was frequent and great effort had to be devoted to maintaining one's position.[^93]
