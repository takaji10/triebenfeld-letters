Between the late sixteenth and the early eighteenth century several factors increased the gap between a small number of rich nobles and the ordinary szlachta. Economic developments played a part in this process. Richer nobles profited most from the favourable market conditions of the sixteenth century, while the impact of the long series of wars fought within the Commonwealth's borders after 1648 merely intensified this trend: poorer nobles lost a significant part of their land to wealthier landowners, who were better able through their economic and political power to avoid or absorb the worst effects of the wars and the downturn in demand for Polish grain. On the whole, those owning several villages increased their holdings, those owning one or two managed to hold their own, while it was the poor nobility which did worst. One study of the Kalisz district in Great Poland shows that between 1580 and 1655 the number of villages held by those owning only part of a village dropped dramatically, from 160 to 92, which was from 51 per cent of the villages in the district to 29 per cent. Those holding more than two villages increased their holdings from 50 villages (16 per cent) to 96 (30 per cent).[^42]

Access to royal land often played a vital part in this process. Royal estates comprised some 15–20 per cent of the Commonwealth's land, but the king was in no position to administer the royal domain directly and individual estates – known as starosties (starostwa) – were leased out, in most cases for life.[^43] Since few officials enjoyed a salary, the grant of a starosty was in theory a reward for public service; in practice, the proceeds far outweighed any service rendered. This was despite the triumph in the 1560s of the anti-aristocratic ‘Movement for the Execution of the Laws', which sought to prevent the alienation of the royal domain and put royal finances on a firm footing. Henceforth, in theory, the kwarta – one-quarter of net, one-fifth of gross revenue from royal lands – was assigned to the upkeep of the small army which protected the south-eastern frontier. The noble leaseholder was to keep a further fifth, with the rest returning to the royal – from 1590 the state – treasury. The monarchy's inability to carry out regular audits of its estates or to enforce the law, however, soon ensured that although payment of the kwarta was fairly regular from the early seventeenth century, in practice the leaseholder kept the rest of the proceeds. Ironically, the triumph of the Execution Movement had the opposite effect to the one intended; moreover, the limited reforms that were introduced did not apply to Lithuania.

Starosties varied widely in profitability, but they contributed significantly to the economic well-being of their holders. Some, particularly in the east, were highly lucrative. The starosty of Bila Tserkva near Kiev, the richest in the whole Commonwealth, was valued for the kwarta in 1771 at 63,506 zloties, an enormous sum; altogether there were six starosties in the Kiev palatinate alone valued at over 10,000 zloties.[^44] The select few who acquired several starosties could reap substantial profits.
