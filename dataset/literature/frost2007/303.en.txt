These changes were definitively embodied in the Constitution of 3 May 1791. Nobility might be eternal – though even that was now in question – but it no longer guaranteed the citizenship which had been its most important attribute; that was now determined by property, however subject to life's vagaries that might be. The way was now open into the nobility for commoners – over 400 of whom were ennobled in three weeks in 1790 – although it was unclear whether those who did not choose to enter it would win a greater place in the political system. The constitution, however, was never put to the test, since the spectacular collapse of noble solidarity helped bring about the destruction of the Commonwealth: there was no shortage of conservative magnates prepared to appeal to both the noble dispossessed and the Commonwealth's neighbours, who immediately condemned the new order. Within four years, the szlachta, citizens and non-citizens alike were subjects of three alien powers with very different traditions of nobility. It was easy enough for middling and wealthy landowners to be accommodated within systems in which landed nobilities were so dominant; nevertheless, the spell of the old Commonwealth remained strong, and many among the szlachta, from magnates to humble smallholders or noble leaseholders whose status remained unrecognized, refused to be reconciled to the new order and engaged enthusiastically in movements which sought to overturn it, from Kościuszko's rising in 1794 against the Second Partition, to the January Rising of 1863, often losing life, liberty or lands in the process. For noble consciousness could not easily be eradicated, either by the pen of Kołłątaj or by the decrees of the partitioning powers: in Mazovia and Podlasie even after 1945, villages of nobles economically indistinguishable from their peasant neighbours still protested their noble identity and refusal to be absorbed into peasant society under the rather different egalitarianism of the Polish Peoples Republic.[^111]

[^3]: As such, although Connor and other contemporary anglophone observers used both 'nobility' and 'gentry' almost interchangeably as translations for 'szlachta', the former is to be preferred: the concept of gentility was an important part of Polish definitions of nobility, but the szlachta was a legally privileged order, which the English gentry was not. A Polish noble was meant to be a gentleman, but an English gentleman was a commoner at law, which a Polish nobleman was not.
