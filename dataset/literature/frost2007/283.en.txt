Almost half of the grain produced on the estates of Aleksander Michał Lubomirski in 1660 came from lands leased from the Crown.[^45] Janusz Ostrogski, holder of the vast Ostrogski entail, held at his death in 1620 over 1127 villages, of which over half belonged to the Crown.[^46] The bloated private latifundia of the greatest magnates were thus built up at the expense of the royal and the State treasury.

These great complexes of land, frequently scattered across a wide area, gave their owners the important advantage of flexibility. Concentration on the European economy has tended to exaggerate the importance of the Baltic grain market and play down the significance of the internal market: only some 2.5 per cent of the grain produced in the Commonwealth was exported, even during the boom years of the sixteenth century, and sales on the internal market were worth four to five times more.[^47] Far from being dependent merely upon the Baltic trade, magnates were well placed to exploit all kinds of markets, both internal and external, and to sell not just grain, but timber, livestock, and a wide variety of raw materials; indeed the Ukrainian lands, where many of the largest latifundia were based, being far from the river network which served the Baltic, concentrated more on the lucrative cattle trade.

The agrarian economy was more varied than is often appreciated. While serfdom was certainly important, it was by no means the uniform system as which it is often portrayed, however convenient that view might be for general theories about the development of the European economy. The extent of labour-service (robot) obligations varied widely; in some areas, such as Royal Prussia, estates were almost entirely worked by hired labour. Even where serfdom was common, there was extensive use of hired labour: most large estates could not function without it, particularly at harvest-time. In Great Poland 10–15 per cent of the peasantry was free, working as hired labour for noble estates on contracts; 91 per cent of 337 estates studied in Little Poland between 1512 and 1642 used hired labour.[^48] Even on manorial estates based on robot, the demesne constituted only about 25 per cent of the land; the remaining 75 per cent was made up of peasant plots, which were far from subsistence farms, but were often heavily involved in the local market; 50 per cent of peasant farms in Little Poland in 1590 were themselves employers of hired labour.[^49]

Large estates based on serf labour undoubtedly enjoyed certain advantages. Those with access to the great river network could undercut smaller producers because their transport costs were lower since they eliminated middlemen by using their own flotillas of barges. When the relative profitability of the Danzig trade declined from about the mid-seventeenth century, large landowners were well placed to find alternative sources of income.
