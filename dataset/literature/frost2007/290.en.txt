Families could fall as well as rise. John Casimir may have nominated representatives of families with a Senate tradition dating back to the fifteenth or sixteenth century like the Myszkowskis, Opalińskis, Firlejs or Koniecpolskis, but few of them were appointed to particularly prestigious posts; in general, families which had been prominent in the sixteenth century such as the Firlejs, Ostrorógs, Tarnowskis and Zebrzydowskis died out or were eclipsed by relative newcomers like the Lubomirskis, Potockis and Zamoyskis. It was similar in the eighteenth century: some old families including the Leszczyńskis, Koniecpolskis and Sieniawskis died out, to be replaced by new families or families such as the Czartoryskis, which had experienced a hiatus in their office-holding career.[^70] Even the Radziwiłłs suffered something of a decline: with one or two exceptions, its members had to content themselves with less prestigious posts than hitherto.[^71]

By its very nature, the system caused frequent and bitter frustration and was liable to intense periods of volatility. Since appointment was for life, a monarch had to accept the senators and even ministers bequeathed to him by his predecessor. From the king's point of view, this was highly unsatisfactory; a shortage of convenient deaths could leave him unable to utilize his main political weapon: in 1711 Augustus II only had the opportunity to make one appointment.[^72] Coveted offices and starosties were often tied up for years in the hands of individuals: Albrycht Stanisław Radziwiłł, who was appointed Lithuanian Grand Chancellor in 1623 by Sigismund III, died in 1656, having outlived Sigismund's eldest son, Władysław IV. These bottlenecks caused intense frustration: individuals might languish in a relatively unprestigious post for years waiting for advancement. Nevertheless, problems also resulted from the occasional periods of intense upheaval. The early years of John Casimir's reign, which were stormy enough following the outbreak of the 1648 Cossack revolt, saw a period of dramatic change as 26 of the 79 most important Polish seats in the Senate fell vacant in 1649–50; in all, between 1649 and 1652, 47 per cent of Polish offices in the Senate changed hands.[^73] John Casimir therefore had an unprecedented opportunity to use his patronage powers, but the results were highly controversial. Krzysztof Opaliński was created palatine of Poznań, one of the most prestigious lay senators, but he had wanted to be Chancellor, and withdrew to his estates in the highest of dudgeon to foment opposition. John Casimir left the office of Polish Grand Hetman vacant for 3 years after 1651, and tried to avoid appointing his enemy, Janusz Radziwiłł, to the grand hetmanship of Lithuania in 1654. Both these cases drew bitter criticism, not only from the disappointed contenders, as did the practice of selling important offices to the highest bidder.[^74]

The most volatile periods came in the early eighteenth century, however, during the brief reigns of Stanisław Leszczyński (1704–10 and 1733–36).
