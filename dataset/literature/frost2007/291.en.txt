Leszczyński was elected by supporters of Charles XII of Sweden in 1704 during the Great Northern War; following the treaty of Altranstädt (1706), in which Charles forced Augustus II to abdicate, Leszczyński's supporters planned a clean sweep of the major offices of state. Although such decisions were more dependent upon Charles than Leszczyński and although – thanks to the backing of Peter the Great – Augustus's fortunes revived after the Battle of Poltava in 1709, the implications were clear: if Leszczyński won, his opponents faced being stripped of office and royal lands, as John Casimir had done to Polish Vice Chancellor Hieronim Radziejowski in 1652 and Polish Grand Marshal Jerzy Sebastian Lubomirski in 1664 by having them impeached before the Sejm. Similar fears were aroused during the War of the Polish Succession (1733–35) when Leszczyński was the French-backed candidate against Augustus III (1733–64) in a contested election, but he failed once more to consolidate the throne; his supporters understandably did not prosper under Augustus.

Instability also had economic roots. A great deal of study has been devoted to the dazzling fortunes of great nobles, and the ways in which one was accumulated; far less to the problems of preventing its dissipation. Yet partible inheritance was not just a problem for Mazovian smallholders; it also affected the greatest families. In 1586 the sons of Mikołaj Radziwiłł the Black established three separate entails from his vast estates, an example that was followed by the Zamoyskis (1589), Myszkowskis (1601), Ostrogskis (1609) and Sułkowskis (1783). Other families, however, showed no interest in following suit. There was some opposition on the grounds that entails violated the principle of noble equality, but in each of the above cases, Sejm approval was forthcoming in return for the acceptance of certain obligations on the part of the holder. The main reason why only seven were ever established seems to have been lack of interest rather than szlachta opposition. The holder of an entail was not as free to dispose of his property as other landowners: if he had acquired no great allodial holdings outside the entail it was difficult to provide for his younger children, and he could not sell land to pay his creditors, a dilemma that spectacularly confronted Janusz Sanguszko, holder of the ill-fated Ostrogski entail, in 1753.[^75]

This lack of flexibility meant that entails were not well suited to the condition of landholding in sparsely populated Eastern Europe, where land was plentiful but of little use without labour to work it; therefore, despite the frequent attempts to compute the area of land held by individual nobles, it is a meaningless exercise: acreage was less important than the number of villages controlled.
