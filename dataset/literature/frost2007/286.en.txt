The great economic and political power of this small élite thus undermined in practice the theoretical equality of the szlachta. Although attempts to introduce a hierarchy of titles had long been resisted and royal attempts to introduce orders of chivalry, such as Władysław IV's ill-fated Order of the Immaculate Conception, were condemned as assaults on the principle of noble equality, this did not prevent a number of families using them. Lithuanian and Ruthenian families descended from the houses of Rurik or Gedymin were legally allowed to use the title of prince (kniaz in Ruthenian, książę in Polish). Foreign nobles continued to use their titles if they obtained Polish nobility; native nobles were all too keen to seek honours abroad: the Radziwiłłs, Lubomirskis and Ossolińskis were dukes of the Holy Roman Empire and the Myszkowskis were created marquises of the Empire by Pope Clement VIII. Several families became counts or barons of the Empire, including the Tarnowskis, the Leszczyńskis, the Chodkiewicz and the Tęczyńskis; Paprocki printed their patents of nobility in his armorial. Although there was a great uproar at the temerity of Urban VIII, who created a duchy in Poland for Jerzy Ossoliński in 1633, many families linked their imperial titles with their Polish lands, using titles such as comes in Tarnow or comes in Wisnicz.[^60] Although it was occasionally prudent not to flaunt titles in public, in private they were invariably used.

Titles were, however, of relatively minor significance. Differences in status within the nobility, which elsewhere were denoted by a hierarchy of titles, were in the Commonwealth provided by the hierarchy of office. At its head was the Senate, ranked according to a strict order of precedence led by the two Catholic archbishops (of Gniezno and Lwów) and fifteen Catholic bishops. The leading lay senator was the castellan of Cracow, ahead of the palatines, among whom were counted the castellans of Wilno and Troki and the starosta general of Samogitia. Next came the major castellans, who were governors of the royal castle in the leading city of each palatinate, then the minor castellans, governors of royal castles in lesser cities, of whom there were none in Lithuania. Technically ranked at the bottom of the Senate, though actually possessing the enormous prestige of power, were ten government ministers: the grand marshals, chancellors, vice-chancellors, treasurers and marshals of the Court, of whom there was one each for Poland and Lithuania.

Certain non-senatorial offices were also highly prized, particularly the posts of grand or field hetman (commanders of the Polish and Lithuanian armies), which conferred a great deal of power, and of court treasurer, which was lucrative, as well as a number of honorific posts, such as chamberlain, steward, or carver, whose duties, if they existed, were largely ceremonial; these dignities were, nevertheless, much coveted: they were frequently the preserve of young members of prominent families.
