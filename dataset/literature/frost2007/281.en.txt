Connor's instinct was right: the practice of mésalliance had continued unchecked throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Many nobles, particularly poor nobles with daughters, were only too willing to marry their children off to burghers or even peasants, without any feeling that they were compromising their noble status.[^39] No doubt Trepka was spinning in his grave.

Commercial activity was another means of trying to guarantee the sort of income necessary to keep up noble appearances. The richer nobles were extensively involved in trade, commerce, manufacturing and money-lending (albeit usually through the medium of factors and managers), and lesser nobles were quite prepared to move to towns or cities in pursuit of a livelihood. Many urban nobles were happy to engage in commercial activities, and the phenomenon of the noble artisan was by no means unknown. The town of Bochnia in the Cracow palatinate, which had grown rich on the proceeds from its salt-mines, provides a good example both of the way in which rich burghers were able to pass into the ranks of the nobility, and of the extent to which nobles migrated to towns to engage in activities which theoretically threatened them with derogation, while still maintaining their noble status. Between 1531 and 1630, about 30 per cent of patrician families in Bochnia used the title nobilis. Between 1535 and 1642, 40 nobles acquired citizenship in Bochnia, which was also held by a further six.[^40] Some of these were already wealthy, such as Mikołaj Stadnicki, the inheritor of one town and 25 villages, who settled in Bochnia at the end of the sixteenth century; others were quite content to grow rich on the proceeds of the salt trade, or even the artisanal activities which fed upon it, without feeling it compromised their noble status. Involvement in such supposedly derogating activities certainly did not prevent these urban nobles from participating in the general political life of the palatinate or the Commonwealth: several had close links with the royal Court, and one family – the Wielopolskis – rose out of the Bochnia patriciate in the seventeenth century to a place among the Commonwealth's magnate élite.[^41]
