Throughout Poniatowski's reign, despite dogged opposition from conservative diehards, these trends gathered force. The tone was set immediately, when the tempo of ennoblements by the Sejm increased dramatically: 60 new nobles were created at the Coronation Sejm of 1764, the largest number at one Sejm since 1676, with further waves in 1768 and 1775. Some concern with maintaining the essential nature of nobility remained: the 1775 Sejm required new nobles to buy land worth the considerable sum of 50,000 zloties, beyond the means of the majority; nevertheless, this stipulation was not rigorously enforced, and the same body allowed several wealthy Warsaw burghers to buy land officially without being ennobled. Attitudes had changed: whereas seventeenth-century sejmik proceedings bristle with demands for the banning of commoners from obtaining land, those of the eighteenth century show steadily decreasing concern; in 1775, indeed, a measure was passed allowing burghers and foreigners to acquire allodial land in Lithuania to encourage settlement. Furthermore, after a series of petitions from impoverished nobles who had been forced into plebeian pursuits, the 1775 Sejm finally swept away the derogating force attached to ‘all forms of trade'.[^107] The distinctions between burgher and noble functions were dissolving in law as they had long ago dissolved in practice.

There was, of course, much opposition to this blurring of the borders between Estates; nevertheless, there was also growing common ground between the views of middling noble reformers like Hugo Kołłątaj (1750–1812), and burghers like Stanisław Staszic (1755–1826). During the Four Year Sejm the various strands of the reformist movement finally merged to sweep away the foundations of the old order. From the outset, debate centred around the political rights of the szlachta. In defining nobility, Kołłątaj sounded at first little different from Starowolski or Kromer:

> In a Commonwealth, rewards are necessary for those who are distinguished by their virtues, from which advantage falls on the whole of society. People of such virtues, as the benefactors of society, should enjoy a reward whose honour should even touch their descendants. This honour is called nobility.

Nevertheless, the use of ‘even' when discussing the descendants of a noble displays a note of hesitancy about the hereditary principle which is amplified in his insistence that mere noble descent was insufficient:
