No other family came close to the Radziwiłłs, who provided twelve palatines of Wilno between 1491 and 1795, seven Lithuanian grand chancellors, one vice-chancellor, four grand marshals, six grand hetmans, two field hetmans, three castellans of Wilno, and a cardinal, as well as filling a large number of more minor offices.[^67] The Radziwiłłs, however, were unique: the fact that they held three of the six entails designated by the Sejm in the late sixteenth century meant that they had an economic base whose security was matched by no other family in the Commonwealth.

The king, who appointed senators and central dignitaries, remained the most important factor in the scramble for office. All monarchs were, in practice, able to promote a substantial number of new figures to important offices, and were capable of blocking members of well-established dynasties. Between 1648 and 1696, 81 of the most coveted posts in Poland (the twenty leading central dignitaries and the Senate, excluding bishops and minor castellans) were filled by 330 men from 186 families. And 59 families had at least two representatives, which means that nearly two-thirds of families only produced one senator in this period. The Potockis had eleven, the Leszczyńskis nine, the Denhoffs, Lubomirskis and Opalińskis eight each, the Koniecpolskis had seven, the Działłyńskis, Grudzińskis and Zamoyskis had six; eleven more families had between three and five. Of 135 senators and dignitaries named by John Casimir from 98 families, 39 were the first members of their families so honoured, which represented 28.9 per cent of the men, and 39.8 per cent of the families appointed. Wiśniowiecki's reign was too short to be of great significance, but seven of his fourteen nominations were new men. John III Sobieski (1674–96) appointed 107 from 82 families; 33 were new men (30.8 per cent of the appointments; 40.2 per cent of the families).[^68]

Appointment to high office did not in itself establish an individual's position (still less that of his family) among the élite. With almost a third on average of such appointments being new men, it is difficult to argue that a magnate oligarchy was closing its ranks in the second half of the seventeenth century. Zielińska's study of these offices in the Saxon period (minus three palatines and three castellans of lands lost by 1697) shows that this trend continued: only 30 of 190 families had more than a couple of representatives.
