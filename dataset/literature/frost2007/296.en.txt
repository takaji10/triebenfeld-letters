The buoyancy of the economy in these areas was demonstrated by the scale of settlement from abroad: between 1721 and 1793, some 775 new settlements with about 12,700 new households were established in Great Poland.[^94]

Despite the apparent dominance of the political system by wealthy nobles, the views of lesser nobles could not be ignored, since the Commonwealth's constitutional structure after 1569 ensured that szlachta of the meaner sort enjoyed political influence substantially beyond their economic power. Interest in politics remained high: it is estimated that nearly half the adult male nobility of Royal Prussia took an active part in political life in the mid-eighteenth century.[^95] Mazovia, where magnate estates were virtually unknown, had a large number of sejmiki to match its numerous nobility; it therefore sent proportionally more envoys to the Sejm than other, wealthier provinces: until 1764, almost one-sixth (30 out of 182) of the Chamber of Envoys, excluding those from Royal Prussia, were from the palatinates of Mazovia, Rawa and Plock. If the poor nobility were often unable to leave their plots of land to travel to the general Mazovian Sejmik, they enthusiastically attended district sejmiki, and smallholders were by no means excluded from serving as envoys or deputies.[^96] Moreover, Warsaw, where royal elections took place and where the vast majority of Sejm sessions were held after 1569, was the capital of Mazovia, and Mazovians therefore did not have far to travel to attend. Traditionally, Mazovians were presented by Polish historians as rather primitive and limited in their political outlook, but more recent work has stressed the full engagement of Mazovians with the political ideals of the Commonwealth, stressing their intrinsic legalism. While Mazovian envoys to the Sejm could be unruly, and their attachment to the ideals of the Commonwealth meant that they were intrinsically hostile to any royal plans which seemed to threaten the introduction of absolutum dominium, they were broadly regalist in outlook and could often be relied on to oppose the pretensions of magnate factions: thus although the Mazovians opposed John Casimir's plans to introduce an election vivente rege in 1661–62, they overwhelmingly supported him against Jerzy Sebastian Lubomirski in the crisis which ended in Lubomirski's rokosz (rebellion) of 1665–66.[^97] In royal elections, from 1573, when Mazovians played a central role in the election of Henry duke of Anjou, their influence was important.
