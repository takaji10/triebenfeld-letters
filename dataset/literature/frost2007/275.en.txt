The Cossack revolt had tested the solidarity of the property-owning nobility in the Ukrainian lands with those elements, so numerous elsewhere in the Commonwealth, who supported themselves by fighting – itself a perfectly respectable noble occupation – but whose religion and way of life could not be accommodated within the political system. The Commonwealth's ethos was in many ways still far more attractive than that of the Muscovite tsar to whom the Cossacks had looked for protection in 1654, and it continued to influence the development of the Cossack hetmanate after 1659, but the noble nation had established its first frontier.

## IV

Nevertheless, the szlachta remained extremely diverse. This was inevitable, since it was extremely large. Noble status was conferred upon all children of a noble, both male and female; the szlachta, already large in 1569, therefore had a distinct tendency to grow very quickly. Quite how large it grew is unclear. Connor estimated it to constitute nearly the tenth part of the population at the end of the seventeenth century; from the nineteenth century, the figure of 8–10 per cent was widely accepted. As scholars began to investigate the sources more systematically, however, rather than rely on impressionistic estimates, a more realistic figure emerged. Of particular importance are the registers for the hearth tax raised at various points after 1629, the poll tax levied on four occasions between 1662 and 1676, and the censuses taken at the end of the eighteenth century; in 1790–92 by the Poles, and by the partitioning powers between 1772 and 1827.

Even making allowance for a degree of inaccuracy in the tax registers, which sought to list all members of noble households over the age of ten, but which were administered by a nobility not renowned for its cheerful acceptance of taxation, local studies have consistently revised downwards the percentage of noble inhabitants: the figures of 4.8 per cent for the palatinates of Poznań and Kalisz and 1.66 per cent for the Cracow palatinate in the mid-seventeenth century are significantly lower than previous claims.[^16] Emanuel Rostworowski's widely respected estimate for the late eighteenth century suggests a figure of 6–6.5 per cent of a population of approximately 14 million for the whole Commonwealth before the First Partition in 1772, and 7–7.5 per cent of approximately 10 million thereafter. A more accurate general picture will not emerge until more local studies are complete, but it seems that a figure of 6–7.5 per cent of the population is more likely than 10 per cent.[^17] Even this reduced figure, however, means that the Commonwealth's nobility constituted a higher percentage of the total population than anywhere else in Europe, with only Spain and Hungary coming close.
