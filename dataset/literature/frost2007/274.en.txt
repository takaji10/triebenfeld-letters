Yet integration had its limits. Local identities remained strong, and sejmiki provided an important focus for local political action. The truncated Grand Duchy of Lithuania which finally accepted the Union retained its own army, administration and law, as well as a powerful sense of autonomy. For all the influence of the middling Lithuanian nobility on the events of 1569, the power of the magnates across the vast lands of the old Grand Duchy in its pre-1569 borders remained much more substantial than in Poland.

In the heartlands of the old Grand Duchy, the transition did not cause substantial social disruption; it was, however, to be a different matter in the Ukrainian lands, where the limits of convergence soon became apparent. Many leading Ruthenian families, including the Vyshnevetsky (Wiśniowiecki), Chartorysky (Czartoryski) and Sangushko (Sanguszko), enthusiastically embraced the ethos of the szlachta in the century after 1569. Their conversion to Catholicism was an important part of a process in which the Commonwealth's noble élites moved away from the religious divisions which had followed the Reformation, when large numbers had turned Protestant, towards an increasing identification of Catholicism as a defining feature of the Commonwealth's ruling élite, a process cemented by the wars of the mid-seventeenth century with Sweden and Muscovy. This left large groups in the Ukrainian lands, who by their lifestyle were little different to the middling and petty nobility of the rest of the Commonwealth, poised awkwardly between its undoubtedly attractive socio-political model and its increasingly unwelcoming cultural and religious exclusivism. The emergence of Cossack society in the second half of the sixteenth century provided an alternative, in which some petty and middling Ruthenian nobles sought the military employment which was such a traditional part of the concept of noble functions. Yet the creation of a large Cossack host, useful to the Commonwealth though it was in time of war, was felt by many to be a potential threat to noble liberties, since the Vasa monarchs Sigismund III (1587–1632), Władysław IV (1632–48) and John Casimir (1648–68) all saw the Cossacks as a potential source of military support for their ambitious political plans to strengthen the monarchy.

For over half a century, the Commonwealth and the Cossacks coexisted in an ill-defined and ambiguous relationship which finally shattered apart in the great Cossack rising of 1648. The 1659 Treaty of Hadiach sought to integrate the Cossack élites into the noble nation by creating a Grand Duchy of Ruthenia and guaranteeing a place in the noble nation to Orthodoxy, but opposition from nobles in the rest of the Commonwealth to the mass ennoblement of Cossacks and the extension of privileges to the Orthodox Church caused its failure.
