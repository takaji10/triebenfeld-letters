Thus in practice, it could be relatively easy for a wealthy urban patrician to pass by one route or another into the nobility if he so desired, as did 26 (23.6 per cent) of 110 members of Cracow city council in the seventeenth century.[^25] For burghers of the great cities, the line between noble and commoner was already somewhat blurred. Cracow possessed noble status under a 1493 charter, confirmed in 1513, which permitted it as a corporate entity to own land; similar rights were extended to Wilno in 1568 and Lwów in 1658. Poznań, in Great Poland, and the Prussian cities of Danzig, Thorn and Elbing also possessed this privilege. Cracow, Danzig, Elbing, Lublin, Lwów, Poznań, Thorn and Wilno had the right to participate in royal elections; in 1632, the representatives of Danzig and Thorn made a point by claiming, albeit unsuccessfully, the right to vote separate from, and before, the Royal Prussian nobility.[^26] In these circumstances, it was relatively easy for burghers to acquire land: in Danzig in the fifteenth century, only 11 out of 132 members of the council (8.5 per cent) owned land, but by the seventeenth century, exactly half (31 out of 62) did so.[^27] From owning land to joining the nobility could be but a short step.

Thus Trepka's complaints were ineffectual: the Commonwealth's nobility remained open for many who wished to join it without the formality of an act of the Sejm. The succession of statutes attempting to seal off the nobility and define it more closely bears eloquent testimony to the ease with which they were circumvented. The ineffectiveness of the 1496 ban on commoners buying or leasing land is demonstrated by its renewal in 1538 and 1611, to similarly little effect, while an act of 1717 reflected continuing concern by attempting to ban the foundation of new towns or villages by those of uncertain status. An act of 1633 sought to deprive nobles resident in towns of their status if they took municipal office: the result was that, initially at least, burghers entering the nobility resigned from their town councils. Later in the century, the law was often ignored: Dominik Meglioruci, elected to the Cracow council in June 1697, was ennobled by the Sejm in September of the same year, yet served on the council until at least 1722.[^28] Frustration at the complete inability of the law to prevent the usurpation of noble status brought a revival of the medieval concept of scartabelat: a cadet nobility which did not enjoy full noble privileges. In the election charter of Michael Korybut Wiśniowiecki (1669–73), this idea was enshrined in a clause which banned anyone from holding public office until their family had enjoyed noble status for three generations. Challenges to those suspected of usurping noble status remained common into the eighteenth century, but in practice this law, like so many others, was frequently ignored.
