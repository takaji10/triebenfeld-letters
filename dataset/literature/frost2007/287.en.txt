Of these few had any real responsibilities; nevertheless, to be swordbearer of Cracow, cupbearer of Sandomierz or ensign of Mścisław was of great local significance. Individuals were invariably known by the offices that they held, and there was as definite a hierarchy of prestige with regard to local offices as in the Senate.

Thus for all its egalitarianism the szlachta was as status-conscious as any of its European counterparts. If nobles of whatever rank were addressed as pan brat (literally 'sir brother') and commoners as 'przyjaciół' (friend), senators were referred to as 'elder brethren', and the Latin term 'procer' was freely used to denote individuals at the summit of the social pyramid. In personal correspondence there were strict rules: wielmożny (roughly the Polish equivalent of procer) denoted a senator, urodzony (high-born) was used to other office-holders, and szlachetny (noble) to non office-holders. By the late seventeenth century, titular inflation had set in: wielmożny was used liberally for any office-holder, while urodzony frequently denoted a noble property-owner.[^61] Concern with status led to a veritable mania for titles, as sons of a palatine (wojewoda) would call themselves wojewodzic, sons of a castellan would be addressed as kasztelanic and the children of lesser office-holders claimed a series of bizarre appellations. By the early eighteenth century, many of the old taboos were increasingly ignored: if the Order of the Immaculate Conception collapsed in 1638 amid bitter protest from the middle and petty nobility, Augustus II (1697–1733) freely distributed his Order of the White Eagle, instituted in 1705, despite the continuing legal ban on such baubles.

## VIII

It might seem, therefore, that a small group of wealthy families had subverted the egalitarianism which still formally survived, and stealthily overturned the victories won by the mass of the nobility in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Historians in the Polish Peoples Republic after 1945, required to write within a broadly Marxist framework, devoted much attention to documenting and explaining this phenomenon. From the 1950s, the standard interpretation of the Commonwealth's development was that the sixteenth-century ‘szlachta democracy' was overturned by a ‘magnate oligarchy' during the seventeenth century. As the power of these magnates grew, they seized control of the helm of state in their own ‘reactionary' interests, while pretending to keep up the façade of the ‘progressive' szlachta democracy which they consistently undermined.[^62]

This theory was thought to provide a model of Polish development which might explain its divergence from the general European pattern. There are, however, serious objections to it.
