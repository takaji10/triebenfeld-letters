Many of the supposedly 'private' troops at the royal elections of 1632 and 1669 were in all probability part of the regular army: in 1632 Krzysztof II Radziwiłł and Stanisław Koniecpolski were respectively grand hetmans of Lithuania and Poland, while in 1669 Michał Kazimierz Radziwiłł was Lithuanian grand hetman, Mikołaj Kazimierz Pac was Lithuanian field hetman, Jan Sobieski was Polish grand hetman and Dymitr Wiśniowiecki was Polish field hetman. In 1631, a year before the election to which he is supposed to have brought 10,000 men, Krzysztof II Radziwiłł's infantry numbered a princely 59 men.[^82] It is claimed that the size of these 'private armies' grew rapidly into the eighteenth century,[^83] but wild estimates do not accord with reality. Hieronim Florian Radziwiłł, who decided to establish a private army on his return from his Grand Tour in 1735, was estimated to have maintained a force of 6–10,000 men; the documents reveal that at its height, he managed to raise 1854 men, more than half of them irregular cossacks; after his death in 1760, it was reduced to 545 men by his successor, Michał Kazimierz Radziwiłł.[^84] For all their fabulous wealth, even the greatest Polish-Lithuanian magnates could not really compete with the State in military terms, even one so weakened by the eighteenth century as the Commonwealth; at best, they were more akin to clan chiefs in the Scottish Highlands who could raise a couple of thousand retainers in time of need, than German princes with properly-organised modern forces. Those that tried too hard were likely merely to compound their economic difficulties: rental rolls, especially in a system so dominated by serfdom, simply could not compete with taxation income, however large the estates.

Indeed, by the eighteenth century, many of the greater landholders had leased out or mortgaged large parts of their estates. Mortgaging was a useful way of raising loans, but the Commonwealth's high interest rates submerged many wealthy nobles in a deepening slough of debt. One study of landholding patterns in what is now central and western Belarus on the basis of hearth-tax registers reveals that although Karol Radziwiłł nominally owned 35,800 hearths in 1790 (14 per cent of the total for this area), less than a third (10,700) were in his direct control. Of 32 properties he inherited in the district of Nowogród, twenty had already been mortgaged in the early eighteenth century; by 1776, only one was left unmortgaged.[^85] Radziwiłł blamed the sequestration of his lands in 1764–67 and 1769–76 for his problems; the rest of the family blamed his prodigality.
