The extent of the problem is demonstrated by the notorious Liber generationis plebeanorum, universally known as Liber chamorum, drawn up in manuscript by Walerian Trepka between 1626 and 1639 and only published in full in 1963.[^22] A poor noble himself, Trepka was an inveterate gossip. Much of the material he collected was based on hearsay, but he ruthlessly pointed the finger at those he accused of usurping noble status; subsequent research has revealed the accuracy of many of his findings. He made his intentions quite clear in his preface, condemning the increasing frequency with which commoners were styling themselves noble and attempting to enter the nobility. He listed the means by which this was achieved and named names extensively, avidly recounting scandal and salacious detail:

> Borowski: . . . the son of a peasant, an idiot who could neither read nor write; a yokel and a boor; had a peasant wife as well. Bought land.
>
> Fresser, Urban: Cracow burgher. These Fressers only began to pass themselves off as noble in 1626, but we do not want them, because ‘Fresser' (in German) means glutton, and the Polish nobility prefers drinking to eating.
>
> Jasiński, Matyusz: who was deputy starosta of Sieradz for several years . . . He had a nephew, Turski, who frequently said to his face: ‘A peasant and a bastard, who dares boast of his nobility to me!' For he knew he was a peasant. This Turski finally killed him in the street in 1628. It was said that [Turski] slept with [Jasiński's] wife, his own aunt . . . He married his daughter by his first wife to the burgrave of Kalisz.
>
> Jugowicz, . . . was a burgher from Grodzka street in Cracow; his family was from the country, from Myślenice . . . This Oleksy Jugowicz was terribly humpbacked and could not turn his neck. As black as a gypsy, and he pretended he was a nobleman. He traded in salt . . .[^23]

Such entries are typical. Trepka was only too ready to challenge individuals in the courts and was not afraid to accuse great families of conniving in the acquisition of nobility by commoners, listing 64 such cases involving the Lubomirski family alone.[^24]
