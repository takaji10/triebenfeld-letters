In 1674, when many Mazovians stayed away, it was a French-backed faction-leader – John Sobieski – who was elected.[^98] Even in Lithuania, traditionally seen as dominated by magnate clientes, the middling and lesser nobility in the first half of the seventeenth century demonstrated its clear attachment to the political ideals of the Commonwealth, and a willingness to act independently – or at least to manoeuvre to its own advantage between the magnate factions.[^99]

While the ordinary nobility had always been suspicious of royal reform initiatives which might threaten szlachta liberties, it by no means looked favourably on magnate behaviour, despite outward deference. Indeed, the growth in magnate power brought an increasing assertiveness from the mid-seventeenth century, in which the ordinary nobility sought to safeguard egalitarian principles. The ideals of the Execution Movement were not forgotten, and there was a constant strain of anti-magnate feeling within political life: during the crisis of the 1650s there were strong attacks on the system of distributing royal land, which was seen as responsible for civil strife and government poverty.[^100] The 1660s saw several attempts to subordinate the Senate to the Chamber of Envoys, while a clause in Wiśniowiecki's 1669 pacta conventa (election charter) banned more than one government minister per family. When the 1690 hearth tax statute referred to the 'lesser nobility' it provoked a storm, and the offending word was struck out in 1699 as violating szlachta equality.[^101] In Lithuania, where the Sapieha family did manage briefly to establish a dictatorial grip on politics in the latter period of Sobieski's reign, the middling szlachta exercised a vicious revenge after the Sapieha forces were destroyed at the battle of Olkieniki in 1700, slaughtering Michał Sapieha in cold blood and destroying the Sapieha hegemony forever. Magnates might still be able to appeal to noble solidarity in the name of szlachta republicanism, but they could not presume too much.

This was no oligarchy. There was no stable élite and success was dependent on conflict, not cooperation. Competition for heiresses, office, starosties and political influence was rife; rivalry was bitter and occasionally murderous. No one faction, or coalition of factions, could control central politics or access to patronage; indeed only the loose coalitions which formed royal support came close, but although the king could manoeuvre effectively among the great factions, using his control of patronage, he could not break free of reliance upon them: support of one led to the alienation of its rivals, and he could always be frustrated by szlachta suspicion of royal intentions and opposition groups which exploited the Commonwealth's decentralized political structure, or looked to foreign rulers who were only too eager to masquerade as protectors of the republican constitution.
