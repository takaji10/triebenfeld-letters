could only be the building begun in 1782 of the Mariannenvorstadt named after his wife, a settlement whose purpose was to foster trade in his small residence. To raise the attractiveness of the settlement he put ground and land for the building of houses at the disposal of the tradespeople partly free of charge, so that craftsman families, such as clothmakers and stocking-knitters or hatters, could soon make their home there. A gold wares factory founded afterwards, partly with princely money, partly on shares, had however to cease its operation in the long run as well, since it yielded no profit.[^p37-17]

The good will of Friedrich Ludwig von Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen to advance his heartland economically cannot be denied him, all the more as he could look with pride on a numerous posterity which it befitted his rank to provide for. Even before the beginning of the French Revolution four of six children had been born to him by his wife. The youngest child, Prince Ludwig Wilhelm Eduard, however died a few years later on 9 February 1790. There followed the trio named so by the princess of Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen: Friedrich August Carl, born on 27 November 1784 at Breslau, the future heir, then Adelaide (Adelheid), (1787–1858), who later married Ludwig of the line Hohenlohe-Kirchberg, and on 20 November 1788 Emilie, who was later wedded to the count of Erbach-Fürstenau († 1859).[^p37-18] On 16 November 1793 Auguste saw the light of the world, and on 29 January 1797 Prince Adolph. The latter became Prussian minister president and married Princess Luise von Langenburg. On 3 July 1798 Prince Alexander was born last († 1829).[^p37-19] From the trio a sextet had become, which Marianne zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen sought to educate with the greatest care. Because of the outbreak of the French Revolutionary War on 20 April 1792, through which her husband was ordered to his post, she was at times separated from her children, whom she loved above everything. Her personal displeasure she by no means concealed:

The fatal king of France, (…) the revolution in my heart, for which he is guilty, is truly greater still than the one in his realm, and many a struggle in it is as serious as the one before the National Assembly.[^p37-20]

In her principles of education she was an enthusiastic adherent of the views represented by Jean-Jacques Rousseau in his work "Emile ou de l'education". Of the Hofrat Kahlert, later teaching as professor at Breslau, who during her absence oversaw the children's upbringing, she required

that no stays be put on the little daughters. My hatred of these shells goes so far that I must repeat it - no stiff politeness, no artificial bouton. - If on my stepping down from the carriage all Three do not spring to my neck with the ordinary entrechats that mother nature taught them, embracing and kissing me as awkwardly as possible, and if for that my Adele makes me a stiff, grand curtsy with elbows stretched out backwards by way of welcome, truly, I believe, I could bring an action against you.[^p37-21]

[^p37-21]: 21 Ebd., S. 292. The welcome that was finally prepared for her by her children, who had been brought to their grandmother in Berlin, must have been quite to her taste. To her husband she wrote: "Mon cher ami! Enfin me voici arrivée après bien des peines en bonne Santé ici – le Mercredi après minuit j'ai été rendue ici, – et j'aurai peine à Vous exprimer la sensation de mon Coeur en me rapprochant de nos chers Enfants – ces chers petites Creatures ayant été éveillée par le Postillon sont sortie tous hors de Leurs Nits pour me recevoir et me temoigner Leur joie – Ils ont très bien grandies – Auguste s'est beaucoup formés par ses Etudes – Il parle coulament le francais, le prononce bien – et s'est appliques en general – Adele est come Elle a toujours été – vive, drôle, mais excellente – elle était si touchée en me revoyant qu'Elle a pleurée et rie en même temps et n'a pue s'arracher de mon Coeur – aussi a-t-Elle voulue recevoir tous ses Droits, Elle loger chez moi, ne me quitte pas – et s'attacher plus que jamais à moi. (…) Emilie a grandie – et bien plus regulièrement bien que sa Soeur – mais sa Physionomie est moins Spirituelle – et Elle est beaucoup plus indolente qu'Adele – mais c'est un bon Enfant, et Nous ne saurions rendre assez grée au Ciel des ces bonheur de les posseder tous trois tel qu'ils sont." [My dear friend! At last here I am, arrived after much trouble in good health here - on Wednesday after midnight I was returned here, - and I shall have trouble expressing to You the sensation of my heart as I come near to our dear Children - these dear little creatures, having been woken by the postilion, all came out of their beds to receive me and show me their joy - They have grown very much - Auguste has much formed himself by his studies - He speaks coulament [fluently] the French, pronounces it well - and has applied himself in general - Adele is come as She has always been - lively, funny, but excellent - she was so moved at seeing me again that She cried and laughed at the same time and could not tear herself from my heart - She also wanted to receive all her rights, She lodges with me, does not leave me - and attaches herself more than ever to me. (…) Emilie has grown - and much more regularly well than her Sister - but her physiognomy is less spiritual - and She is much more indolent than Adele - but she is a good child, and We cannot thank heaven enough for the happiness of possessing all three as they are.] Letter of Marianne Fürstin von Hohenlohe Ingelfingen to her husband of 4 February 1792, HZAN 47, K 1, F2
