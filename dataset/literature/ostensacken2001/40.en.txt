# ostensacken2001  40  en
# Kraus (2001), p. 40
# translation by Claude

When Prince Friedrich von Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen after the end of the battle wanted to withdraw to his Upper Silesian possessions, to look after the smelting works that had run into trouble, this was at first denied him. He was brought to Oehringen as a prisoner. The engineer John Baildon, with whom he stood in correspondence to keep him informed of the state of the works, he could no longer help out of his own strength.[^p40-30]

The only person who was in a position to halt the collapse of the works was the princess Sacken. At first she was not ready to do so, because Prince zu Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen had carried out the purchase of the Rittergut Bytkow without consulting her, and she was of the opinion that the estate could have been brought to the house on more favourable conditions than it had been. Of the acute misery in which the prince found himself she had in the end nonetheless received knowledge. She now wanted to await the bankruptcy, in order then to acquire the Rittergut Bytkow. Only the earnest plea of Prince Hohenlohe-Ingelfingen to preserve the estate "durch ihre Fürsorge auf ehrenvolle Art der Familie zu erhalten" [through her care, in an honourable way, for the family] changed her attitude.[^p40-31] On 13 October 1808 she therefore wrote to him:

We would both now have a large portion less of sorrow if you had earlier granted me the trust that I deserve so completely. Through want of trust you have harmed the good cause, and it can now no longer be carried out so well as it could have been done before.[^p40-32]

After the prince had thus appealed to her sense of family duty, she had the possession transferred to herself, paid the debts resting on it, and united Bytkow with Schlawentzitz into a Fideikommiß.[^p40-33] Although she was no longer willing to make money available for the completion of the blast furnace building begun by John Baildon, because she herself had no reserve of money left, John Baildon succeeded under the most difficult conditions in building at Blechhammer a first plate-rolling works after English plans, which could begin operation in November 1807.[^p40-34]

This changed nothing however in the disastrous financial situation of the prince, who, although he no longer had the means for it, kept up great house as before. Adolf Fischer, the historiographer of the house of Hohenlohe, described him thus:

In matters of money he was like a child that does not know the worth of money. As often as he received any, existing debts were paid, wherefore he did not long want for credit. Even after the catastrophe of 1806 he could not for a long time accustom himself to the necessary restriction. (…) When money arrived, a pressing creditor was satisfied, even if nothing remained over.[^p40-35]
