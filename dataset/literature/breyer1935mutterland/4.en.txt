# Breyer (1935b), p. 4
# translation by Claude

In what follows, the origin of three groups of the Germans of central Poland, by their station, is to be proved:

> 1. the rural settlers;

> 2. the German burghers and the German craftsmen;

> 3. the Lutheran clergy, the German teachers and the members of the free professions.

The first *rural settlement* on the soil of central Poland is, to our present knowledge, the Vistula village of Słońsk, founded in 1605 and called Schlonske in the popular speech[^2], not far from the well-known salt-water spa of Ciechocinek. In quick succession a whole series of new foundations followed up the Vistula, such as Alt-Bogpomoż, Lentzen, Neu-Bogpomoż, Woluszewo. The Polish historian J. Baranowski says in his treatise "Wsie holenderskie na ziemiach polskich" (Dutch villages in the Polish lands):

> *"In this way at the beginning of the 17th century both banks between Thorn and Breslau [sic - probably meant is Leslau/Włocławek - JD] were sown with Dutch colonies*[^3]*."*

The settlers of these lowland villages, who are also called "Holländer" at Lentzen, at Wolluschewo and so on in the 17th-century church books of the Lutheran congregation at Thorn, came for the greater part from the Vistula villages around Thorn, Kulm, Graudenz and Schwetz. Schmidt says in his "Deutschtum des Landes Posen" (The Germans of the Posen country) that as early as about 1600 numerous Lutheran "Holländer" were settled below and above Thorn, in the villages of Gurske, Nessau, Silno and Dybow, because the strictly orthodox town government of Thorn did not like Mennonites as settlers. The Thorn lowland villages were mostly daughter settlements of the Holländereien (the Dutch-law settlements) lower down the Vistula, which had arisen about the middle of the 16th century. The inmates of the Holländereien were by no means Dutch by stock.
