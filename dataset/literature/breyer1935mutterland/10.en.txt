The Treaty of Warsaw of 1768, which guaranteed freedom of belief to the Dissenters, brought a stronger stream of German craftsmen and members of the free professions. In 1781 the splendid Lutheran church in Warsaw was consecrated. Among the Germans of that time many came from the neighbouring Prussian areas. It would go beyond the bounds of a short lecture to list all the names. Some outstanding representatives shall nonetheless be named. The well-known coach-maker Thomas Michael *Dangel*, raised to the Polish nobility in 1792, came from Pasewalk in Pomerania. The outstanding Warsaw architect Ephraim von *Schröger* was born in Thorn; he too was ennobled, in 1775. Another important architect, Theodor Gottlieb *Seyfried*, was born in Buk near Posen. Of the forebears of the leading Polish booksellers' firm *Gebethner* and *Wolff*, Gebethner came in 1727 from Krotoschin and Wolff from Brätz. The family of the well-known Warsaw wholesale merchant *Herse* comes from Schwiebus. About 1776 the forebears of the Warsaw factory owners *Schlenker* came from Thorn. The dyer *Werner* in Warsaw, whose descendants now own extensive landed estates [Goßgrundbesitz] in the neighbourhood of Łęczyca, came from Obersitzko. The list could, as I said, be continued at will.

Let us now turn to the urban Germans who arose from settlement in closed groups. The second *German town settlement* after Władysławów, which the Germans called Rostarschütz, is the small craftsmen's town of *Neuhof*, laid out in 1782 by the Archbishop of Gniezno. According to the church books of the local Lutheran congregation, the cloth-makers came from Schwersenz, Czarnikau, Schönlanke, Bütow in Pomerania, Braunsberg [Brausnsberg] in Silesia, Fraustadt, Neudamm and Gilgenburg in East Prussia. The stocking-knitters, shoemakers, locksmiths and tailors came from Sonnenburg, Jastrow, Pasewalk, Johannisburg in East Prussia, Löbau, Thorn, Straßburg in West Prussia, Bischofswerder and from Ohlau in Silesia, all of them, then, from the neighbouring Prussian provinces.

When, by the second and third partitions of Poland, considerable territories of the Polish Republic fell to Prussia, German craftsmen and merchants streamed into the depopulated Polish towns together with the Prussian officials. A fine example of this is the voivodeship town of *Kalisch*, which had fallen victim to a terrible fire in 1793. The first concern of the Prussian authorities was the rebuilding of the town, which lay well placed for trade and administration.
