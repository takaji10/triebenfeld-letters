# Breyer (1935b), p. 9
# translation by Claude

If we sum up the statements of origin for the Kalisz country, we obtain the following distribution: *Silesians* 54 in a hundred, *Pomeranians* 21 in a hundred, *people of the Mark* (Brandenburg) 12 in a hundred; *to the rest of the Reich fall* 10 in a hundred.

This provisional reckoning is unfair to the Silesians in so far as the statements of the congregations of Prażuchy and Sobiesęki near Kalisch are still missing; they are inhabited almost exclusively by Silesian farmers. The strong showing of the Pomeranians and the people of the Mark comes from the fact that our reckoning took in mainly the northern congregations, neighbouring the Pomeranian dialect. It is natural to suppose that in the parishes lying further south the number of Pomeranians and people of the Mark will be comparatively small.

With few exceptions all the German villages of the *Łódź industrial area* are daughter settlements, mainly of the German colonies on the Kuyavian lake plateau and in the Kalisz country. On the whole, in the Łódź area the two stocks of the Silesians and the Pomeranians balance each other. The Swabian villages make up a certain percentage only in the neighbourhood of Łódź and in the Gostynin country. In the *Gostynin country*, about 1800, the wholesale merchant v. Treskow settled farmers from the western Warthe marshes, from the neighbourhood of Landsberg on the Warthe and from Driesen on his extensive woods, as we have been able to establish from the Gostynin church books.

In conclusion we state: *the German rural population in central Poland comes in each case from the German areas neighbouring it to the west. In the great majority the German villages of central Poland are daughter settlements of the mother provinces of Silesia, Posen, West and East Prussia. This finding also holds for the German farmers of the Chełm and Lublin country, and likewise of Volhynia. For the settlers of these areas come, with few exceptions, from the German districts (Gaue) of central Poland.* They moved away from here between 1855 and 1885; many thousands of colonists from central Poland found their way overseas after 1890.

The *urban Germans of central Poland* look back on a history of a good 200 years. The immigrants of the 18th century, it is true, as shows most starkly in Warsaw, were lost in the host people; only small groups have kept their nationality (Volkstum), for example in the town of Władysławów, founded in 1738. As everywhere, the urban Germans who arose from single immigrants have here too a far smaller national (völkisch) power of resistance than the German farmers settled together.
