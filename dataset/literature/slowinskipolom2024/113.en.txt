Hauländer settlement in the lands of Poland reaches back to the sixteenth century, when actual Dutchmen — or, as it is safer to say, inhabitants of the Netherlands, famed for their skill in combating the geographical depression of their country — arrived to transplant that skill onto the soil of the lands of Eastern Europe. In time the inhabitants of northern Germany, of Pomerania, but also of Mazovia and of Poland's northern lands acquired the same skill. All of them, dropping the mute 'h' in many languages and polonizing the name, came to be called Hauländers. It was to one of these settler groups that the aforementioned Andreas Beutler belonged, and presumably his wife Eva Rosine Schnell as well. Settlers were most often second, third or last sons in a family, for whom there was no longer enough of the paternal inheritance to divide, and who faced either hunger and vagabondage in their homeland or an uncertain tomorrow — yet with a chance of survival in a completely foreign, though neighbouring, country.

One can imagine the uncertainty with which Andreas Beutler set out into the unknown. Nothing good awaited him in the family home apart from the aforementioned hunger and vagabondage, seasonal work, the life of a hired hand, for he had no land of his own with which to feed himself and a numerous — as was often the case in those days — family. He packed up his modest belongings, perhaps on a wretched cart, or perhaps simply on his back, and set off eastward. What surely did not guide him was the idea of *drang nach Osten* that the propaganda of the Third Reich would exploit many years later, but simply the desire to survive and to secure a livelihood for his family.
