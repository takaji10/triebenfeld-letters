As Gerard's aunt Kuska from Lądek defined those years, with sorrow and simplicity: "Hitler came and spoilt everything." Time, as it flows, has its hard laws. But it also gives the possibility of looking into the past without the emotions that accompanied the dramas now gone.

My family home and the home of my grandparents stood on Konińska Street (Hindenburgstraße during the war) in Zagórów, in the closest neighbourhood of the Evangelical church, which we called the German church. As a very young person, in the second half of the nineteen-fifties, I attended a kindergarten which was housed in the former cantorate house, at No. 4 on the same street. I am unable to explain to myself a certain fear that always accompanied me when I passed (especially in the evening) by that house of worship. I am unable, because at home nobody frightened me either with that building or with those people who appeared once a month at the Sunday mass. A certain strangeness of the place was softened by the mighty chestnut trees, whose beautiful fruits, gleaming light brown, we gathered in racing competition. Over many years, perhaps once or twice, I managed to glimpse the inside, but the threshold of the church I never crossed. Nor, at that time, did I ever manage to free myself from the feeling of the strangeness of that place. Of the recent war I knew more from lessons at school or from the reading of books than from tales at home. Perhaps that is why those short stories, which my dear ones told, lodged themselves so firmly in my memory.

My mother's father, beloved Grandpa Bronisław Maciejewski, was a prisoner of the concentration camps in Oświęcim (No. 171 181), Brzezinka, Sachsenhausen and Buchenwald (No. 79 608). He landed in the camp for the killing of a pig. A neighbour – a Pole – denounced him to the police; to him, in confidence, Grandpa had sold two kilograms of ribs. In January 1945 the Russian soldiers entering the town were catching and killing on the spot marauders or over-zealous functionaries of the Third Reich.
