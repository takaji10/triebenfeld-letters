A chest was placed on the runners of a sleigh, and a horse was hitched to it. In such a vehicle one drove around the neighbourhood and through the snow-covered forests. But in the end spring came. It took another four weeks or so until the last snow melted. It was a very wet period. Once that was over, summer was not far off. As a rule the summer was very warm and dry.


Our farmstead covered 20 hectares. We raised a few pigs for our own needs; for milk production there were at most 12 cows in the byre. Mama kept about 100 hens. Most of the eggs, the milk or the heifer were sold. Thanks to this an important regular source of earning money was assured. Three horses did the heavy work. During the war we kept sheep and geese. In times of general want and restrictions they provided wool and meat. Thus, for example, our geese were taken away to Dortmund in exchange for textiles, or father cycled to Kalisz, some 40 (50 in the previous edition) kilometres away, in order to exchange meat there for other everyday items. Above all it was important to obtain the materials from which Mama then sewed clothes. For most of the war the Łazińsk region was spared all the horrors of the war – apart from the letters, which one feared and which contained the news of the "heroic" death of a son, a brother, a husband or a father. Not a single bomb fell here, and at the beginning of 1945 the front was still far away, even if with the east wind one could hear the sounds of the cannonade. Until the arrival of the first Russians on 18 January we knew the war only from afar.

"It will be harder for us than during the war, with all its restrictions," Mama used to say. "The Russians and the Poles will take revenge on us." I could not imagine that. Had we done anyone wrong? After all, Germans and Poles had lived for a long time peacefully side by side, without major conflicts. For me the whole order collapsed. What remained was a dull fear.
