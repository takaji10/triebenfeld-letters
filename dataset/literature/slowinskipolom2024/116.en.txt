Meanwhile Louise married a teacher, Fryderyk Wilhelm Schulz — their daughter Mathilde Schulz later married her cousin Ferdynand Beutler, who was the son of Johann. Martin married Ludwika Hirsekorn, and Johann Ernestine Steinborn. The farming family of Beutlers grew and slowly began to move about. One conclusion suggests itself: the lands their father had chosen as a place to live favoured the family and allowed both its rapid growth and its social advancement. The first and second generations of the Beutler family were above all farmers, ploughmen, husbandmen — one could say: the salt of the earth. To every piece of land, no matter in what corner of the world the little plot lies on which the farmer tills the soil, thereby sustaining himself and his family, while the surplus produced makes it possible to keep alive those who live in towns and small cities or in the great metropolises.

The Beutlers have strong genes; in every generation many children were born to them. Unfortunately, many died, but to say it outright — though it may seem brutal — such were the times. Knowledge of hygiene, medicine and so forth, especially in the villages, was scant, and so many tiny children died, and it was not rare for young mothers to die in childbirth as well. It was the sons who carried the name Beutler into the future and onto successive generations, and so the four sons of Andreas-Andrzej and Justyna — Ludwik, Andrzej, Gottlieb and Wilhelm — founded four further lines of the family. In the nineteenth century Europe experienced many upheavals: Napoleon's battles, the dissolution of the old Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation, the rise of the Austrian Empire, the November Uprising, the Spring of Nations, the January Uprising, the founding of the German Empire under the Hohenzollerns' sceptre, the industrial revolution. Momentous and fraught with consequence was, of course, the industrial revolution, but also many innovations in technology, medicine and other fields of science.
