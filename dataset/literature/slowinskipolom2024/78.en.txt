Yet Bishop Bursche did not hesitate to engage the Church in nationality actions in Masuria and Upper Silesia during the plebiscites, which for the German opposition constituted a point of criticism against the General Superintendent. Unfortunately, the provisions of the Treaty of Versailles meant a defeat for Bishop Bursche. An action was begun of creating Polish Evangelical Societies in Wielkopolska and Pomerania, but the effects of these actions were not impressive. The complexity of the nationality relations was influenced by the process of assimilation of the evangelicals into the Polish environment of the former Russian partition, and, on the other hand, by the growth of German national consciousness, strengthened by the influence of the events of the First World War, and later by the influence of contacts with the German evangelicals of the former Prussian partition, including those of the Evangelical Church of the Union, whose parishes bordered Congress Poland on the west. Aware of the threats to the position of the Evangelical-Augsburg Church in Poland given its ambiguous national character, Bishop Bursche opposed the politicization of the Church and its being drawn into nationality games. He invoked the consensus worked out before the war as to the essence of church ministry and called upon the clergy and laypeople: "Whether one of my pastors is pro-Polish or pro-German in orientation is all the same to me. I demand, however, one thing: that he not allow national questions to influence his activity in office in a decisive way. In one word, his task is not to proclaim either Polishness or Germanness, but the Gospel, to Poles as well as to Germans, and also to Poles who were once Germans and bear German surnames." Were he to reproach the latter for their Polishness or consider it his task to restore them to Germanness – as is now demanded of us from all sides – he would only arouse resentment and bitterness, and moreover would achieve nothing.
