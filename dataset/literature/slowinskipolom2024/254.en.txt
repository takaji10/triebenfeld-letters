# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 254
# translation by the editor's files

 Ferdinand's wife, Pauline, died suddenly. The young widower with three little daughters returned to the vicinity of Zagórów and settled in the village of Anielewo (Ingelfingen). There, too, he came to know the daughter of Gottlieb and Ernestine Drewitsch, the next Pauline of his life. Pauline Drewitsch accepted Ferdinand's proposal and became the next Mrs Brudler. She brought into the marriage her own daughter, Lene Drewitsch, who from then on was raised together with Ferdinand's daughters by his first wife. Further Brudler children began to come into the world. In January 1923 the spouses became the parents of twins: Otto Richard and Erwin Waldemar. The boys, unfortunately, died soon after birth. The following year Ferdinand and Pauline became the parents of a little daughter, whom they named Martha, and in the following years Gertrud (1927) and Lydia (1929) came into the world as well. But fickle fate did not allow the spouses to enjoy their happiness for long. Suddenly and unexpectedly Pauline Brudler died in 1930, and Ferdinand was once again a widower with a brood of under-age daughters. Unable to look after such a large brood, he entrusted the youngest little daughter, Lydia, to his brother Theodor and his wife Berta. A year after the death of his second wife Ferdinand Brudler married for the third time. He was still a relatively young man, and his little daughters needed a mother. His chosen one was a widow – Emilie Dahlke née Kalisch. The family continued to live in the village of Anielewo (Ingelfingen) until Ferdinand's sudden death, which took him in June 1933. Ferdinand and each of his wives gave a splendid example of unity and of the enduring strength of the family. Despite many upheavals, successive deaths and bereavements, Ferdinand was able to keep the family together with him, making use of his brother's help only in the case of the youngest little daughter. Yet the Brudlers never lost touch with one another. In the photograph from around 1990 of the reunion of Ferdinand's descendants, one can see six cheerful, elegant elderly ladies. They are the daughters of Ferdinand and his wives: Gertrud, Martha, Erna, Elly, Lene and Lydia. It was precisely this clan solidarity that often allowed the settlers, the Protestants, to survive the hardships of fate and the upheavals of history. It was the strength slumbering in the family that gave the people of those days the motivation to act and allowed them effectively to build their position.
