The evangelical-Lutheran Church in the Kraj Warty was given the legal status of a private association, donations and church taxes were prohibited, and the church cemeteries were expropriated. Pastors of Polish orientation were removed from their church offices; of the evangelical population the signing of the Volksliste was expected. In place of the Polish farmers murdered or resettled to the General Government, German settlers were brought in from Volhynia, Bessarabia and Galicia. The evangelicals also found themselves among the victims of the Soviet assault on Poland on 17 September 1939. Among those murdered in Katyn and the other places were Major Adolf Laube, the Konin pharmacist, and General Ryszard Paszko, pastor of Koło, chaplain of the Evangelical Military Chaplaincy, and many others.

The entry into Poland of the German troops and administration was not always accompanied by the euphoria of the evangelicals, even those of German orientation. The signing of the Volksliste, likewise, was not connected solely with evident descent – sometimes it gave a sense of security and peace, and sometimes – as in the case of the Konin entrepreneur Jerzy Trenkler – the signing of the Volksliste, consulted with his Polish employees, secured work precisely for them, and not deportation or a camp. It happened that persons who in September 1939 wore the uniform of the Polish Army, after signing the Volksliste, a few months later went to the front in the uniform of the Wehrmacht. An interesting counterweight to Bruno Mielke – a soldier of the Wehrmacht, the author of the letters found in Łazińsk – is the figure of Eryk Stark, likewise an inhabitant of Łazińsk, who, deported to forced labor in the Reich, fled across the Alps and in Italy joined the Polish Army as a rifleman in a sapper unit, went through Monte Cassino, and remained in Great Britain after the war.
