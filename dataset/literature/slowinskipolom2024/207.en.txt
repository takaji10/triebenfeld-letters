# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 207
# translation by the editor's files

Only years later, already as a regionalist researching the history of our little town, did my interlocutor put the facts together into a whole…

Mirosław Słowiński remembered Breede from childhood:

– More than once I saw how an old, gaunt woman in a nightshirt, with grey windswept hair, chopped wood on the block at midnight. That was on Konińska Street, exactly opposite the site of the kantorówka, where we now have "Gęsi Puch". That was precisely Janina Breede of the Stawicki family. That image cannot be forgotten. Death claimed Janina Breede in August of the year one thousand nine hundred and seventy-five. She had just been visiting friends, the Szymkowiaks, to whom she was showing the wedding photographs of her sons, sent from abroad. The great emotion evidently caused her tired heart to refuse obedience. Breede collapsed limply to the ground. An ambulance was called, but the woman died on the way to the Słupca hospital, never regaining consciousness. Her body was handed over to the town authorities, and the burial was carried out as quickly as possible. The then head of the town did not permit a Catholic funeral, so in enormous haste the deceased's body was driven to the cemetery and buried in the Evangelical section, as if ridding oneself of a shameful burden. Not one of the deceased's close acquaintances accompanied the coffin. Her old and still well-disposed friends did not manage to bid Mrs Breede farewell: Cieślewiczowa and Marczukowa, as well as her cousin Tenia[^75].

– There was no way of catching up with the coffin being carried. Before we reached the grave, it was all over. – Mrs Halina is bitter about this to this day.

It is said the curious gravedigger did not fail to peek into the coffin before covering it with earth: the greatest elegant lady of pre-war Zagórów was buried just as she had died – in poor clothing fastened with a safety pin and in worn-out shoes.
