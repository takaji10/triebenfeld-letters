# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 124
# translation by the editor's files

Ella Beutler was born in 1908 in Dąbrowica as the first daughter and first child of Adolf Beutler and his second wife Emilia. She married Reinhold Hintz from Tarszewo, a village likewise situated in the Warta's floodplain, not far from Dąbrowica. Mrs Gertruda recalls that Reinhold Hintz met his future wife when she was in the first grade of primary school, that is, as a girl of about six or seven, and on seeing her is said to have declared that she must be his wife. At the turn of the 1920s and 1930s Reinhold left with his mother for the USA. After a few years he returned to Poland, bringing with him on the ship a large American automobile in order to impress Ella, for the visit, which was to last three months at most, had been arranged for the sole purpose of allowing him to propose and marry Ella — which in fact came to pass. The newlyweds departed for the USA. They proved a well-matched couple, as their relatives and descendants confirm, above all the daughters Alice and Shirley, who lived until recently. Reinhold died at the age of 88 in 1989, and in 2004 Ella died; she was 95.

Erwin Beutler, Adolf's first son, the future heir to the farm in Dąbrowica, which, because of the vicissitudes of war and the post-war period, he never managed to take over. In the photograph in which he poses with a horse bred on the family farm one can see a handsome young man, holding the horse confidently by its bridle, a born farmer and breeder. Erwin was born in 1910 in Dąbrowica. The fates of Erwin, as well as of his brother Gerhard, show what a cruel elemental force war is and how much evil ideologies of every kind, aimed at setting people against one another, can do. When the war broke out, Erwin was a young man. For tens — indeed almost hundreds — of years no one had asked his family of which country they wished to be citizens. They were simply Beutlers. In 1918, after Poland regained its independence, they were given Polish identity documents and recognized as citizens of Poland; in 1939, occupying Poland, the Germans recognized them as Germans, gave them German documents and made them citizens of the Third Reich.
