# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 265
# translation by the editor's files

 the misfortune to be born, come of age and live in times uncertain, then wild and cruel, and then grey and cold.

She did not live to see calmer times. She died without family, without kin, without friends. She was buried at the state's expense. At the same expense, twenty years later, she was exhumed, and her remains were placed in a collective grave with those who had been forgotten. But in the shared history of the universe and of humankind they all, though often nameless, still exist and call to us from oblivion, from being forgotten. I think it was precisely such a call that reached me as I walked the cemetery paths. I did not come to the ossuary by chance, nor was Ida's surname there by chance. She passed through life, presumably often unnoticed, needed when something had to be done. She departed just as quietly, but I have an irresistible impression that after years she decided to remind us of herself. Someone might ask: why now, why only now? And perhaps earlier, aware of the burden that the world and people had laid upon her, she had resigned herself to her fate. Now Ida Disterheft is a kind of monument to all those Polish citizens of German origin whom the cruel world entangled in nationalisms, wars and restless, chaotic times of peace. Ida's fate is also a kind of warning. A warning whose sounds, clothed in Ida's voice, arranging themselves into rows of syllables, could ultimately take the form of a text reading: never again discord, division and war.
