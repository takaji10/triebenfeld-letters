#### The right to education for boys and girls

Martin Luther, both in his sermons and in his writings, argued for the necessity and importance of education. He was perfectly aware that learning from one's parents and from one's own experience alone would not suffice for a person to understand the world around them. The aim of education according to Luther is the capacity to make independent choices, and the chief instrument – one's native, German language. The Reformer advocated the accessibility of schooling for children and young people of both sexes; he prepared for pupils the *Small Catechism* and the *Large Catechism*, which significantly facilitated the education of successive generations[^37].

When the colonists, called Hauländers by their Polish neighbors, arrived on the new lands in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, they first had to concentrate on the considerable challenge of providing a roof over their heads and preparing the poor soil for cultivation. This guaranteed a source of livelihood for the families and the chance of further expansion of the settlement. Only then could the newcomers attend to the second – equally important for them – aspect of life: building their cultural and religious identity. The denominational schools, organized in accordance with the suggestions of Martin Luther and his successors, supported them in this.
