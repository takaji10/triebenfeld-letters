During the time of their wanderings they performed the forced labour imposed by the new masters of these lands, the communists — they worked for lodging and food. In Skulsk Reinhard Schwemmer, as a skilled craftsman, managed a mill for one Domiński, who together with the UB made the family's departure for Germany difficult, because Domiński needed a good craftsman at the mill, while the UB in turn took the stance: the Germans destroyed Poland, so now they must rebuild it.

In later years the Schwemmers applied for permission to leave Poland, but did not receive it. The authorities of the time refused Gertruda in 1972 even the journey to the funeral of her mother, Emilia Beutler, who since 1958 had been living with her husband in Burgdorf. Reinhard Schwemmer died in Skulsk in 1984 after a long illness, never again seeing many of his relatives and in-laws alive. Only in 1992 did Gertruda, already 74 years of age, herself decide to move to Berlin, where her two daughters had been living for years. The great courage of this frail woman, her incredible will to live and inexhaustible vitality allowed her to acclimatize quickly in a foreign country. An advantage impossible to overestimate, and one that facilitated her good integration in Berlin, was her excellent command of German, to a degree as good as her command of Polish. Polish, after all, must be regarded as her mother tongue, and when one conversed with Mrs Gertruda one could have the impression that she thought even in Polish, despite occasionally slipping in some German phrase. An example of her attachment to the language was, for instance, the fact that she replaced the unpronounceable Asian name of the caregiver at the senior citizens' home where Gertruda lived in the last months of her life with the homely Celinka, and addressed her so.
