What was, for the Jews and the Germans – or did it mean anything at all – the idea of a free Poland, nurtured through generations? Both the German and the Jewish children went through a short education strictly bound to the religion they professed. For the German Evangelicals it was the Bible. For the Jewish children – the Talmud. The mastery of the basic skills of reading and writing, of arithmetic and a knowledge of the holy books – these were the foundations of that education. Setting aside the harsh regime of the partitioners' law, from the sources mentioned, nor from their own teachers could they obtain the basic knowledge of the history of the country in which they lived.

In 1918, when independence was regained, wealthy Jewish and German families, as well as Protestant clergymen originating from the Reich, leave Poznań and Wielkopolska en masse for Berlin and other German cities. For a whole century the Jewish and Evangelical communities had been subject to the Tsarist administration. The experiences of ancestors who had crossed the borders of the plundered and disrespected noble Commonwealth were neither dwelt upon nor particularly cultivated. They had the opportunity and they functioned within the closed circles of their communities. The Jews, behind the tight veil of language and religion, conducted their business. The German settlers, likewise bound by language and religion, literally hewed out or dug out their place on this earth, in the forests beneath Zagórów and on the meadows of the Warta. When they travelled to Prussia, the majority considered that they were going on a visit. Their homeland was this scrap of earth, into which their sweat and tears had soaked. But the German Reich consistently conducts an intrusive agitation, and not only through the state administration.

Strong pressure is exerted on the clergy of the Protestant Church. How complicated and difficult these processes and individual dilemmas were is broadly illuminated by Rev. Waldemar Wunsz in the text "Kościół Ewangelicko-Augsburski w dwudziestoleciu międzywojennym".
