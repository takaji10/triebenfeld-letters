He writes: "Gladly we wish to do everything for the Führer and the fatherland, but to become a traitor and a Judas to ourselves and to our conscience, and to the Lord of all lords – that is something no one may demand, and which a reasonable man (certainly also the Führer) will not demand. If they (those overzealous improvers of the world) do not want us as we are, do not want to have need of us, then let them 'dismiss' us. Better to follow this simple, ordinary life than to remain with a broken conscience and without moral support in this bloody – grave period"!

He therefore congratulates his brother that, despite being honoured with important offices, he wants to remain faithful to his faith, that he does not act like the many who for a miserable Judas's pay sold their Lord. "It may displease someone that in accordance with our conscience we seek in our dear, unsullied Church inner care and strength". We do not have to choose (presumably it was expected of the brother that, together with taking up the office, he would sign? some ideological? declarations), "we already fought in the hard period of Poland, and woe to us if we depart from our, so clearly marked, path. The decision, therefore, lies on the other side: whether they will allow believing people to work, or whether they will want to get rid of them". (Presumably the second option was chosen, for from another letter it is known that Gustav was soon called up to service in the navy.) "Now, when the time is dangerous, we must remain with the Lord. Our lot and that of our people is in His hands. Before the war, 'in the hard period of Poland', we abode in the faith, whereas in the young Wartheland everyone intoxicated themselves with 'the new fame'. They abandoned it and fell away far". This "weakening" in participation in the services was also described in his letter by the pastor Gross. Bruno confesses that he too was not free of that sin. Yet at this moment, when the Lord has granted them the grace of conversion and forbearance, "they wish to fight knightly, unto blood", so that nothing evil befalls them.

The letter is written by Bruno Mielke in the first days of May 1944. It is puzzling that the remarks concerning the course of the war were not questioned
