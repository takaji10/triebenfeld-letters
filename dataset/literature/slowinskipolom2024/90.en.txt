#### The cantor, that is, the chief singer in the temple

In Latin the word *cantare* means to sing, and hence the name cantor was given precisely to the chief singer in the temple – the guide and teacher of liturgical singing. The term was already used in antiquity, and a mention of the role of singers can be found in the Bible: "He set singers before the altar, and their song was sweet in its sound" (Sir 47:9). From the Judaic tradition the office of cantor and the custom of liturgical singing passed into Christianity; the first mentions of their role appear as early as the third century AD. It was then that St. Hippolytus, speaking of the cantor, also points out that he need not – indeed, ought not necessarily – belong to the clergy. The term became more widespread only in the fifth century after Christ. The position of the cantor is dated from the moment of the founding of the *schola cantorum*.

According to the historical record, a definite hierarchical order prevailed among the cantors belonging to the *schola cantorum*. At the head of the ensemble of cantors stood the *primicerius*, also called the *magister scholae*, followed by the *secondarius*, the *tertiusscholae* and the *quartusscholae*, also known as the archiparafonista. Over time, schools of this kind began to be founded also in other cathedral and monastic centers (among others Metz, St. Gallen, Reichenau, Fulda), which led to the introduction of the office of cantor. The cantors fulfilled their duties primarily in the church, but they also taught in schools. In the later period the office of cantor spread through the monasteries and collegiate churches"[^30]. Over the centuries the scope of the cantor's duties expanded considerably. Until the sixteenth century the office of cantor was held essentially by clergymen, mainly because they were educated people. Only an experienced and educated singer could be a cantor.
