# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 172
# translation by the editor's files

Today too I was able to do it. […] I went out into the snow-covered mountains and found again an altar for prayer… How good it is then for a poor, lonely soul, when it finds itself near Him who said: 'I am with you all days'". But at once he qualifies: "I cannot tell everyone about it. The majority would take me for a dreamer". Yet for him this is a blissful and beatifying dreaming, a real happiness which the majority of pitiable people do not know. "Dreamer" is one of the milder names he could have been called. Many comrades steeped in the new faith often jeered coarsely at the humble adherents of the religion of the weak, the eternal penitents, as Christians were contemptuously called. Hence that search for a solitary place. It is a kind of escape, a hiding away, in order to give oneself to prayer in peace

In Berlin the ministry of propaganda is at work! On 18 February, a dozen or so days after the capitulation of Field Marshal Paulus's 6th Army, in the capital's *Sportpalast* Joseph Goebbels delivers the famous speech in which he announces the beginning of total war against Russia and the Allies. After the definitively lost battles of Moscow and Stalingrad, when the leading Nazis see the growing threat, above all a general mobilisation is proclaimed by Hitler. Goebbels admits publicly for the first time that the crisis on the Eastern Front has reached its peak, and that Stalingrad is a great alarm for the fate of the German nation. No German may think of compromise. The nation is called to hard war and is to be ready for any sacrifice, should the Führer give such an order. In the sports palace, under the slogan "Total war – the shortest war", only chosen and vetted guests were gathered. These are 14,000 fanatical Nazis, who sing and applaud after each of the sharper passages of this chiefly anti-Jewish philippic.
Goebbels calls with the words of the poet: Now, people, arise, and let the storm break loose ("*Nun, Volk steh auf, und Sturm brich los!*"). The whole staging of the rally is to serve, among other things, to rebut the Allied voices saying that Germany has lost faith in victory. But at the same time he wants to "feed" his ambitions, counting that Hitler will give him greater power.
