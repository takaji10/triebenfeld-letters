The term kantorat could, however, have a much broader meaning – as a territorial unit of the parish, and not only the Evangelical school or the house of prayer (chapel)[^38], which may at times introduce a slight confusion into the heads of persons attempting to explore the topic for the first time. Let us therefore try to make it precise: the kantorates as territorial units grouped the inhabitants of several localities within a radius of several to a dozen or so kilometers, and the seat of each individual kantorat was the building of the same name. It usually stood out slightly in architectural terms from the rest of the dwellings and simultaneously served as a chapel and a schoolroom. Usually one half of it constituted the prayer hall, while the other was the dwelling of the teacher and cantor.

#### The cantors' school

In the denominational school, called the *Kantoratschule* (sometimes: *Lektoratschule*), that is, the cantors' school, lessons began and ended with a prayer led by the cantor, and instruction took place in German, since those schools were most often attended only by the children of Evangelical settlers, both boys and girls. The Polish-Catholic peasant of the time remained in a state of bondage to his landlord and at a low cultural level. The German-Lutheran colonist, by contrast, under the influence of the ideas of the Reformation, was a free man, and in his education and more advanced farming methods he far surpassed his Catholic neighbor.
