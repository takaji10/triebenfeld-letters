# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 46
# translation by the editor's files

The painful memories, cherished for long years after the war, engraved themselves permanently in the memory of the people of Zagórów. To this day, the image of the descendants of the settlers as vindictive and treacherous enemies lingers in the consciousness of many inhabitants of the little town.

#### V. After the Second World War

Both during the liberation and immediately after it, lynchings took place, carried out upon often innocent Protestants. So it was in Olchowo, where in January 1945 Soviet soldiers, set on by a certain Pole, murdered almost the entire Winterfeld family from Zagórów. These people were innocent. The Germans who had something on their conscience had long since managed to leave the neighbourhood – rightly foreseeing that the wronged Poles would seek revenge on their recent persecutors. After the liberation, the Protestants lost their farms, their workshops, their means of livelihood; they were driven out of their own premises. Most of them resolved to leave the Polish lands on which they had lived and worked for generations, for over 150 years. Many parishioners experienced deportation; they worked for the Russians in harsh conditions and on starvation rations of food. Among them was also the last pre-war pastor of the Zagórów parish, the Rev. Aleksander Gross, whose body did not endure the hardships of the work in the quarry. The pastor died in the camp hospital at Finolne (near Stalino) in November 1945[^20]. Many parishioners shared the fate of their pastor. Those who had the good fortune and survived went straight from Russian captivity to Germany, for fear of persecution at the hands of the Poles. The remaining Protestant population had to serve the Catholic farmers, or was deported to farms belonging to the state farms (PGR).
