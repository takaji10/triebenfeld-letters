# Słowiński and Połom (2024), p. 126
# translation by the editor's files

The example of the two Alfred Beutlers shows how convoluted the fates of members of one family can be when war breaks into a calm and settled life. Polish history, after all, knows many such examples. The next child of Adolf and Emilia Beutler was Gerhard Beutler, born in Dąbrowica in 1915. Gerhard, like many like him, was also a victim of the terrible Second World War. Called up to the Polish Army in 1939, he did not fight for long, if indeed he was called upon to fight at all. After the occupant had entrenched itself in Poland he had to demonstrate loyalty to the new authority, and as a man of German roots recognized as a German he was employed in the municipal office in nearby Rzgów. Gerhard concentrated on work and on supporting his family. He married Erna Byczyńska from Sługocin in the municipality of Lądek, and here once more the Byczyński family steps onto the stage.

Gerhard is probably the most tragic figure in the Beutler family. In 1945 he packs his belongings onto a cart — his family, his wife, his year-and-a-half-old little son Baldur, his nine-month-old little daughter Edith — and together with the family of his brother-in-law Leo Byczyński they set off westward. It was 25 January 1945, in the locality of Małe Cieśle, when their little caravan was stopped by men hostilely disposed towards them. Although everyone on the cart spoke Polish — Gerhard may even have mentioned his service in the Polish Army — the men attacked. Gerhard received a blow of a hand axe to the head and died on the spot; his brother-in-law Leo Byczyński was struck by two bullets in the larynx and died on 3 February in the hospital in Konin. The murderers, fortunately, did no harm to the women and children.

The women, in turn — as far as I know from the stories of the already almost legendary Erna of the Byczyńskis, as my aunt Aurelia of the Duszyńskis, Dogoda, used to say — it was under Erna's leadership that they turned the carts around and reached home. Gerhard's body was left in the field where he fell, lightly covered with snow. There was no room on the cart; it was taken up by the women with the children, and after the whole incident the gravely wounded Leo had joined them besides.
