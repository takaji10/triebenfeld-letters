Quite different phrases flow when he declares his fidelity and obedience to the Führer, his readiness for every sacrifice for the sake of the great Germany repairing the world. This is a set of ready-made clichés from the language of the Third Reich (LTI – Lingua Tertii Imperii).

The last of the surviving letters is dated 14 May 1944 in Norway. They must therefore have been transferred fairly quickly to an entirely new place. The field post number 48 341 remained the same. The letter travelled by air, as the *Luftfeldpost* stamps stuck on the envelope attest. This is interesting information in the context of what was to happen three months later. Namely, on 4 September the Finns halted their participation in the fighting with the Red Army, and on the 19th of that month an armistice was signed between the USSR and Finland. One of its conditions was the participation of the Finnish troops in an attack on the German troops, which were withdrawing from Lapland to Norway. And so it happened. Already from 15 September the Finns are fighting on the side of the Allies, together with their former and quite recent enemy: the Red Army. In October there will set out with full force the operation of the Red Army – as it was named, Petsamo-Kirkenes – as a result of which the German 20th Mountain Army was pushed out of the northern territories of Norway and Finland. Bruno writes: "How it will be for me here and to what extent my wishes will be fulfilled here remains in the hands of God. His will has led me here, to His guidance I wish to continue entrusting myself".

How does he understand his part in this war? As an act of the divine will. Is this war, then, also the work of God?

From the company in which he had been until recently he was suddenly transferred to a medical company. He writes the letter, as usual, on Sunday afternoon. In the holy forenoon hours they had known no peace. They had to scrub the rooms and clean the weapons. Just before dinner he managed to slip away to a quiet place for "the eternal word". The last sentences of this letter, the last sentences of the 33-year-old Bruno Mielke that have come down to us, are the words of a man who seems to perceive the whole tragedy of the war. In them there is also a muted trace that something is beginning to stir in his conscience.
