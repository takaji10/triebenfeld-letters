Worth mentioning are also: the engineer Henryk Kliem, son of the pastor of Chodeń, builder of the port in Gdynia; Edward Hauptmann, son of the cantor of Sompolno, professor at the gymnasium and one of the founders of the Polish evangelical congregation in Poznań, engaged in the Home Army in the region of Koluszki and Brzeziny; as well as many significant Konin families: the Reymonts, the Trenklers, the Rosins, the Peczkes.


The growing awareness of the approaching armed conflict intensified the tensions and the distrust directed at persons of the evangelical confession. An interesting read are the memoirs of Pastor Richard Kneifel, a native of Sompolno, pastor in Izbica Kujawska – it must be remembered that these are postwar memoirs. As one conscious of his German nationality and transparent in his views on maintaining the distinctness of the German minority in Poland, Pastor R. Kneifel cites information about the difficulties made for him by the education authority (Kuratorium Oświaty) at the time when he was a teacher of religion at the gymnasium in Sompolno, about the surveillance in the period before the outbreak of the war, or about his arrest in September 1939, together with the pastors of Koło, Dąbie and Babiak, among other persons politically unreliable. Their internment in Bereza Kartuska prevented the destruction of the railway station in Koło, and the subsequent course of the September campaign allowed them to return to their parishes.

Before Pastor Krempin of Koło returned to the parish, the parsonage had been demolished and plundered. In these memoirs mention is made of many German evangelicals who were killed or interned in the first days of the war – there occurred gravestones bearing the information that the deceased had been bestially murdered by *polnische Banditen*. The family of Pastor Krempin found shelter with the Beutler family of Koło, as did Pastor Leon Sachs of Turek with his wife, who had been removed from his post by the new German administration.
