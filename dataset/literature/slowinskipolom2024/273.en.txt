### From memory, or spoken history


Compiled by Katarzyna Połom

The scraps of memories we note down on these pages are above all subjective, emotion-filled utterances of direct participants in the events described, as well as of persons closely connected with them. The written-down memories sometimes illuminate the same events from different perspectives. At times they exclude one another. Human memory is impermanent; some facts remain with us, others become deformed or perish in time. It is worth citing here an important remark of Professor Krzysztof Makowski: “Attempts to verify accounts […] are nevertheless beside the point. The researcher (apart from the possibility of correcting individual facts) is here almost entirely helpless. After all, it is impossible to establish to what degree memory underwent retouching and verification in the course of a lifetime, the awareness of which has been effaced. But that subjectivism of oral sources also has its advantages. It enables, namely, the analysis of human experience in a broader context. For we can learn not only what the author did, but what he wanted to do and believed he had done, as well as how he thinks about it today”. In oral history, diaries and memoirs – Professor Makowski notes further – “far more important than objective truth (if such a thing exists at all) is emotional truth”[^86].

All this makes us aware, once again, that the truth about a human being and about history is never one-dimensional.



[^86]: *Dwa życia jak dwa psalmy. Historia mówiona Leona i Toshy Jedwab opowiedziana Evie Urbach*, Poznań 2012. Here: Introduction by Krzysztof Makowski, p. 61.
