### Letters of a Wehrmacht soldier discovered in the wall of the cantor's house


Mirosław Słowiński

German settlers admitted under Hauländer law in the villages and forest settlements surrounding the town of Zagórów formed a closed community which, despite two hundred years of dwelling on these lands, was not susceptible to assimilation. A strong and probably decisive bond was the Lutheran religion and the German language. They were all free people, whose relations with the landed proprietors who leased them poor grounds were regulated by separate laws, customs and self-government. That was so in the eighteenth-century Commonwealth, under the partitions and in reborn Poland. As one of the then inhabitants of Zagórów recalls – Lech Słowiński, a professor at the Poznań university: "In many villages right up to 1939 one heard only German. It must nevertheless be admitted that until then coexistence between the Polish and German populations was, on the whole, correct"[^50].

The largest settlement in this ring of homesteads was Łazińsk – situated eight kilometres south of Zagórów. To this day it is a place that perfectly illustrates the manner of organising, under Hauländer law, the space entrusted to the settlers: the siting of the farmsteads, the building of roads and drainage canals.
