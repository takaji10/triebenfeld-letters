#### Educating children was not to everyone's benefit…

Teaching in the cantors' schools initially (until the mid-nineteenth century) lasted two years, and the not-so-numerous pupils attended classes only in the period free from intensive farm work – that is, in winter and in late winter before spring. In the remaining months every pair of hands on the farm was worth its weight in gold, and from their earliest years children were trained for work in the farmyard and in the field. School education was not compulsory, and therefore not every parent was in a hurry to send their child "to school" – at any rate, not until the date of confirmation began to approach by leaps and bounds. This was somewhat at odds with the precepts of Martin Luther, who as early as his "Sermon on the duty of sending children to school" urged parents not to delay in securing access to schooling for their children. The level of teaching and its results were, however, rather unimpressive at the time, usually being the resultant both of the education and pedagogical abilities of the teacher and of the attitude toward the school of the parents and the children[^41]. Yet there were among the teachers those who, despite their own low level of education, strove constantly to fill the gaps in their own knowledge and encouraged their pupils to do the same, not only imparting knowledge but also influencing the shaping of their young characters.
