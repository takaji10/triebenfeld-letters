### Cantors and kantorents, or education the Lutheran way: denominational schools in the parish of Zagórów


Katarzyna Połom

> "What full-of-character, Bible-versed, respected personalities our Augsburg church had among its cantors! They were men with calloused hands and thick skin, yet often fervent in faith, with a strong desire and readiness to serve, men who sought their equals."[^28]

As a graduate of pedagogical studies, I certainly encountered the name "cantor" back in my university days, so it does not sound foreign to me in the context of schooling and the history of education. Yet if you were to ask a random person what a cantor was – we could expect bewilderment. In the best case – the interlocutor would think of the figure of Tadeusz Kantor[^29]. In the worse one – if we are not joking, that is – he or she would think, after all it is common knowledge that a cantor is nothing other than a currency exchange booth. What schools? What churches? And yet this ecclesiastical lay office still officially functions today – in the Catholic, Evangelical, Orthodox churches as well as in Judaism – although its role has been reduced largely to matters connected with the musical setting of services. Where, then, did this church office, little known in our country, come from? What did it contribute to the old educational system? And – most importantly – what did the history of the cantors, the kantorates and the cantorate schools associated with them look like in the lands closest to us: in the very Lutheran parish in Zagórów. I will try to answer these questions below.



[^28]: Kneifel Eduard, *Die Bedeutung des Kantors für Gemeinde und Kirche*, translated from German by Magdalena Hoffman, 2021.

[^29]: A distinguished director, painter, stage designer and avant-garde actor.
