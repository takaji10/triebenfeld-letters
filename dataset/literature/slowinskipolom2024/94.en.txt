Holding the function of cantor more or less on the side, they occupied themselves on a daily basis with their ordinary gainful occupations. Besides the duty of teaching the children of the Hauländer farmers and performing specific religious offices, the cantor usually also served as the gmina secretary: he kept the gmina register, handled the gmina's correspondence, and presumably also the private affairs of the Hauländers[^34].


#### Cantors with qualifications wanted

Along with the professionalization of schooling and of the teaching profession, ever higher demands began to be placed on cantors, and after 1870 a requirement of formal education was introduced for persons wishing to serve as teachers in the schools. Candidates for teachers could train on specialized courses in theological and pedagogical knowledge, after which they sat for the state examination. From 1889 an annual cantorate conference was organized, one of whose aims was to provide cantors with courses in theological and pedagogical knowledge so that they could pass the state examinations qualifying them for this function. Cantors could also prepare for the profession of teacher at the German Evangelical Teachers' Seminary in Warsaw, transferred in 1911 to Łódź[^35].

In the middle of 1942 the occupying authorities in Poland deprived the cantors of the right to hold services. In a letter to the teacher Mielke of 13 October 1942, the Zagórów pastor Aleksander Gross complains of this state of affairs: "Thereby [one] deprives the council of cantors of the possibility of acting and of living through the readings during services every Sunday"[^36].
