FL, although the letter above conveys otherwise, was not free in his decisions. Sovereignty over finances and administration lay in the hands of his son and his officials in Öhringen.[^96]

Already years before, FL had attempted, in the vicinity of the estates there belonging to his long-divorced wife Countess Marianne, and of the manor of Bytkow which he himself had acquired in 1801 without the involvement of his mother-in-law, to gain a foothold in the long-established coal and ore mining industry and the emerging metal industry (zinc, brass). Bytkow and the neighboring places Josephsdorf, Jakobswalde, and Ignazdorf were later known collectively as "Hohenlohehütte." The Princess von Osten-Sacken accepted from him that he was doing this with a view to the future of his children, her grandchildren. He had concluded an advisory contract with an English mining expert named Baildon, who had already held corresponding Prussian government offices.

The felling of timber for the smelting of the ore proved at first to be a critical matter (as it also was in Hohenlohe). It was not to become a clear-cutting. Wood could also be used as building timber and thus brought quicker profit. Princess Sacken still had a supervisory role over the accounts at that time and had to establish that FL was aware of the immediate opportunity for profit and made use of it, for more timber was felled than was needed for smelting. This caused trouble; she tried to restrain him. Here too the Öhringen administration, at least after the death of Princess Sacken, had a strong hand in matters.[^97] The same, incidentally, applied to the smelting works as well.[^98]

It was only a descendant during the era of the German Empire who truly became successful and wealthy there in mining—naturally, a Hohenlohe. In the days of "Hohenlohehütte" he was considered the richest man in Prussia after the King! — Upper Silesia
