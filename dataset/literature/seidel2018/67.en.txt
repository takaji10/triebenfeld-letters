The citizenry drew up a petition to the "Most Serene Imperial Prince, most gracious Prince and Lord!"[^44] The letter is long and detailed, full of the stereotyped phrases customary at the time. It concerns the Prince's past services to his residence town, a reverent thanks

> *for the most exalted arrangements [...] whereby His Highness had laudably striven to raise the prosperity of the local town through the establishment of various trades and the reception of artists and craftsmen [...] most respectful attachment, the continuance of His good and most gracious sentiments, most humbly and obediently [...].*

It is not entirely easy to infer, from documents of this kind, genuinely collective veneration on the part of the Ingelfingen subjects. Again and again the sources point out how satisfied they were said to have been with their lord (the "Much-Beloved"), how affable the Prince was said to have been — but whether corresponding feelings are honestly expressed in letters whose vocabulary was, after all, ultimately prescribed, whether the formulas express a genuine sentiment? Perhaps it is indeed so! — This same problem applies equally to the ornate homage texts that adorn the house inscriptions in Ingelfingen (p. 41).
