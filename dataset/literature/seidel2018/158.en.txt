## 15 Excursus V: The New Kingdom of Württemberg

In 1806 the Duke of (Old) Württemberg had become King, as Frederick I, of the greatly expanding land. The area and population doubled through the mediatization of the principalities and the end of the independence of the Imperial cities. Among the principalities may be named Hohenlohe, Waldburg-Zeil, and Thurn und Taxis; among the Imperial cities, above all Ulm and Esslingen, but also many smaller ones such as Schwäbisch Hall. Frederick, the former Duke,

> *could begin to order and rebuild the state, with its so disparate components, and thereby also to unify it. He did so with force and energy, but without leniency, mildness, or reconciliation with the old order.[^130]*

This also entailed a new constitution; he had summarily abolished the old one on 30 December 1805. Until the new one took effect in 1819, he — and, from 1816, his successor William I — ruled as in an absolute monarchy. Württemberg was, after all, a fully sovereign state. Emperor Francis II had laid down the imperial crown on 6 August 1806. The mediatized lords of the principalities had become subjects of the new state.

It was foreseeable that the princely houses would have to surrender privileges and rights as well, for instance in policing, jurisdiction, financial administration, and taxation. The King then announced to the Hohenlohe princes, in a single decree, that their lands had fallen to him and his successor to the throne with full sovereignty (exception: Hohenlohe-Schillingsfürst passed to Bavaria; Hohenlohe-Kirchberg did so as well at first, but was later returned). The essential form of government was the issuing of "Royal Decrees."
