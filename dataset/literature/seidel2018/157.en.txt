FL himself also attempted to find a remedy. He wrote a begging letter[^128] to His Majesty the King, in which he set out an optimistic plan for how he intended to bring about an improvement through pledges of his property. In addition, in 1813 he turned to Massenbach,[^129] who was living in Bialokosch, somewhat west of Posen, and asked him to assist in collecting 5,000 reichsthalers. He names a Count Tercinoky as the owner of the estate of Zagorow, who he says had owed him such a payment since Christmas 1812. He enclosed with the letter a certificate stating that this sum was to be paid out to the "bearer" and "holder" of the document. Massenbach replied that the Count did not respond to any of his letters and "appears not to exist" as far as he is concerned. — There is a further uncertainty on this point, since the town of Zagorow does in fact record a partial sale of FL's property to the landowner Weigel.

### 14.4 Interim Summary II

"Having debts" and "making debts," at least bank debts, was something quite ordinary at the time. FL was certainly a front-runner in this respect, and it is solely to demonstrate his situation that many documents are reproduced in the appendix. But, as cited above, Triebenfeld too, who "owned" an estate at Kontopp near Grünberg in Upper Silesia, and Massenbach as well, had debts. It was already noted earlier that even at Bellin Castle, the residence of FL's ex-wife Marianne, a mortgage of 43,000 thalers was registered, for which Count Hardenberg had stood surety. When he wanted to end this arrangement, Marianne temporarily found herself in difficulties. Her son Adolph, otherwise quite rarely mentioned, had a rescuing idea on this occasion and offered relief from his inheritance from his grandmother. — The peculiar feature in FL's case is the scale of the debts and the hopelessness of ever emerging from this misery.
