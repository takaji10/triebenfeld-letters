# Seidel (2018), p. 80
# translation by Claude

Paragraph 13 refers to the marriage contract of 1781 in Leipzig and to two further agreements made in Ingelfingen in 1782 and 1784, the "so-called Ingelfingen Final Testamentary Ordinances." Both parties undertake, "once this Transact has been fully substantiated, to annul the latter."

Further paragraphs again concern provisions for the children and grandchildren; the three princesses, for instance, are each to receive 25,000 reichsthalers as a marriage portion upon their marriage. As regards the sons, FL retains the right to observe the Hohenlohe house laws and, importantly, to provide for "natural and rank-appropriate maintenance and the flourishing of the House." This also includes an inventory of his "own immovable allodial estate [...] at present, insofar as it may be devoted by His Highness to the entail."

This inventory of the year 1799 can readily be compared with later ones, which Triebenfeld, FL's General Plenipotentiary, repeatedly submitted. The mere listing of assets, of course, says nothing as to whether loans still remained to be repaid for their acquisition — and it is from this that FL's greatest problem would soon arise.

*I. In the house at Breslau. II. In the park laid out near Breslau and a house and garden recently purchased in addition to it. III. In the South Prussian estates and manors, namely the Conin estates, the Cujavian and Meseritz estates, consisting of three towns, one hundred and thirty villages, twenty-one manorial farms,[^63] as well as land and twenty sheepfolds, twenty-three mills and ironworks, six brickworks, and thirteen [...] and breweries.*
