# seidel2018  152  en
# Seidel (2018), p. 152
# translation by Claude

> *Pomerania was treated no less harshly. [...] No fewer than 7,139 officials, thrown out of bread from the South Prussian provinces, begged for the scantiest daily bread, and nothing remained, in order to protect them from despair, but to make deductions from the pay of those serving officials still in office. [...] The cruel dismissal of all officials [in South Prussia] aroused throughout the whole of Prussia great bitterness against the Poles, which the crafty French policy gladly saw, because the Duchy of Warsaw was intended to watch over the revival of the Prussian monarchy, and, when the time came, to check it. [...] However one may think of the earlier partitions of Poland, it is nevertheless certain that much had been done for the Poles living under Prussia's sceptre, indeed far more in a few years than previously under the long Polish government. Efforts had been made to turn slaves into human beings and to secure for them a human existence; great sums and noble energies had been devoted to this, and they had been showered with benevolence, which was now repaid in the manner indicated. Everyone will respect the patriotic feeling with which a people fights for its independence; but it must not lead to ingratitude for benefits received, or indeed tempt one into cruelty.[^124]*

So much for some facts and an impression of the mood of the time.

It is not necessary here to present FL's financial situation in all its aspects. Gerlinde Krauss has done this in detail, focusing primarily on FL's mother-in-law, Princess Sacken.[^125] This Princess controlled an inconceivable extent of estates and other property and evidently knew exactly what needed to be done in each case (today one might say she also knew the rules of finance and real estate management). But cash was generally scarce. FL, according to Krauss, found himself in this "post-war period"
