Massenbach, as shown above, wrote a great deal after Prussia's military defeat (and presumably gladly, but also for reasons of earning money). The fact that Volume Four of the memoirs was not permitted to appear, or was destroyed, has brought him a certain recognition down to the most recent times. Jan V. Röhnert dedicated an article to him in the *FAZ* titled "The Thwarted German Chateaubriand,"[^157] in which he writes of an "autobiographical grand project" that unfortunately is not available to us in published form (though it is preserved in an archive). In content it presumably contains little beyond what he reported to FL in his many letters.

Besides his role as a writer, Massenbach also played a further role as a politician after the Wars of Liberation. This will be addressed below, since it further illustrates the contrast with FL. After his resignation in 1806 and the Prussian King's rejecting letter of March 1813 — namely, that he would be given "no further appointment" (see p. 120) — FL stood aside as a public figure, occupied with his mountain of debt.

The circumstances of the lost battle and the capitulation were investigated by a commission appointed by the King under Prince Henry. In the process, FL was exonerated, while errors, including a piece of misinformation, were proven against Massenbach. FL had, however, in a letter to the King, assumed all responsibility. He had, after all, been the commander-in-chief shortly after the death of the Duke of Brunswick on the battlefield. A shadow always remained attached to FL after this, particularly in the estimation of the Prussian King.
