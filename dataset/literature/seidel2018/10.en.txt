# Seidel (2018), p. 10
# translation by Claude

### 1.2 The House of Hohenlohe and the Lords of Ingelfingen

Concerning the House of Hohenlohe as a whole, and its division — arising from the old law of inheritance — into smaller local dominions, we are very precisely informed. The inheritance was divided among the male descendants in equal shares. A first great division of the territory in 1555 led to the formation of the Neuenstein line, which took part in the Reformation, and the Waldenburg line, which later, in 1667, reversed this step for itself.

The fragmentation of the territory continued. An institution eventually became necessary to guarantee administration in accordance with the feudal system (the whole of Hohenlohe was to be regarded as an imperial fief). The Senior of the House of Hohenlohe was always the reigning Count or Prince who was oldest in years; he was to exercise oversight over this. A "Feudal Court" (*Lehenhof*), consisting of a "Councillor" (*Rath*), a secretary, and a treasurer, assisted the Senior (here, "Rath" designates prominent "officials" who worked directly for the ruler). The entire feudal records were put in order beginning in 1773 by the archivist Court Councillor Hanselmann. By 1782 matters had progressed far enough to conclude a formal contract. One had recognized "the value of common action" in representing the House to the outside world. The Senior now appears as the official feudal tenant. But to return:

Count Ludwig Casimir (1527–1568) had inherited the castle and bailiwick of Neuenstein, Langenburg Castle, and further "bailiwicks," among them the town of Ingelfingen. He must also be mentioned here, since in Hohenlohe the people of Ingelfingen are called the "Kasimirlich" (the people of Künzelsau are called "Maddeslich," presumably after the Matthias fair they held; the people of Niedernhall, "Distelfinken" [thistle-finches]; the people of Crailsheim, "Horaffen" [horn-apes]...). Whether this, in the case of the Ingelfingers, goes back to the fact that the Count had many descendants there, or whether many people of Ingelfingen named their sons after the pious Count in his honor — that shall play no role here.[^2]
