He got to know France and the lands of the Danube Monarchy, and eventually came to Silesia as well, where he visited his mother's (former) archenemy, Frederick II, with whom he got along well. As reported above, Frederick had played a role in bringing about the marriage between Marianne, Countess of Hoym, whom he knew, and FL. The two had married in 1782, and Frederick II will not have lost sight of them, having also become godfather to their first son August, born in 1784. He could therefore have told his visitor about her.

In the posthumously published novel, Marianne, mistress of a castle called "Breitenstein," is greatly admired by the principal narrator (letter-writer) Ferdinand von Falkenstein. He spends a few days at Breitenstein with some of Marianne's friends and acquaintances. She is a calm person who enjoys drawing and painting (like the "real" one). They talk with one another, go for walks, and plan a carriage excursion to nearby Breslau. Marianne is married to a man named "Ingelfingen," who is given no first name; he is frequently away. He is a womanizer, and one of his mistresses is also present. He is moreover a tyrant and a boor, snubbing and abusing his wife. He is a drinker and a gambler, so Count Falkenstein writes to a friend in one of the 39 letters that make up the book (all six adults gathered there write letters). He demands that she accompany him and his current favorite to Karlsbad. There, however, he falls ill and dies, apparently as a result of one of his excesses. Falkenstein now regards Marianne as free and wrestles with himself over whether he may bind himself to her. After much deliberation, the book ends with him falling to his knees before her to make his proposal. — Any further comparison between the novel and the reality to be described here is pure speculation. The novel's Marianne and the real one of Ingelfingen surely have things in common; but the husband probably does not. FL a womanizer, gambler, and drunkard?
