# Seidel (2018), p. 231
# translation by Claude

Of the greatest significance for the assessment of his character is without doubt the event that, in the first general perception, was regarded as the absolute low point of his military career: the capitulation of Prenzlau on 28 October 1806. He had declined a battle that, by the assessment of the situation, was hopeless for his side, and had achieved, through negotiations with the opposing French general, that the lives of thousands of soldiers — his own and, mostly forgotten, also those of the enemy — were thereby saved. One must bear in mind, as the history books record, that more than 100,000 soldiers were deployed in these engagements, of whom only 12,000 survived! That he was a credible negotiator, one who could be trusted, was already known, for example from the talks around Frankfurt some ten years earlier. Commanders are generally not made war heroes by capitulations. Even in FL's case it took some time before the first contemporaries, and later historians, came to recognize his decision. The letter, too, that he wrote afterward as a prisoner to his son (see p. 93f.) bears witness to great human solicitude.

And now? What can one find about FL as a private person? What do we nowadays even expect in the way of information about a person's private sphere? What do we know about his contemporaries Schiller or Goethe as fathers, as husbands? Loving letters between the married couple from around 1792 do exist. But when, as with FL, a particular event in this sphere of life sets the course thereafter — the separation of Marianne from him and, at the same time, from their common children, two of whom were still very small — then (perhaps) particular traits of character are also revealed. This is without doubt the case with FL. He showed himself to be a caring, considerate father, interested in and attentive to his children's health and their whole lives.
