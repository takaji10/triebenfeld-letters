# Seidel (2018), p. 17
# translation by Claude

*unaffected manners, loved to take part in hunts or to paint. In Ingelfingen she knew how to free herself from the Sunday afternoon church services and other constraints. She likewise warded off the stiff court customs for her children. Her daughters were never made to wear a corset. Her warm relationship with her children was not to be impaired by rigid court protocol.*[^6]

A covered walkway leads from the castle to the princely gallery in the nearby church. It can be recognized as an arcade on the second upper floor of the "Schwarzer Hof," and again along the town wall just before the church (the "Schwarzer Hof" is part of a building complex that housed the seat of the princely administration). Marianne would therefore not have needed to leave the castle at all for the Sunday afternoon church service. But she preferred to spend that time otherwise, and she was permitted to do so.

However, the growing family was not often gathered in Ingelfingen, with the possible exception of the year 1796 (before that the Prince had been occupied, not too far away, in the Wars of the Coalition against France) and then once more during his time in Ansbach. Only one of the eight children is verifiably known to have been born in Ingelfingen (1794), and that child died after four weeks. The family did nonetheless come to visit from time to time; thus the old Prince wrote to his son in 1788:

> *The latest newspapers give strong hope of a general peace, which pleases me all the more since you promise to come here in that event. When this occurs to me and seems even half credible, I am filled with joy at the prospect of truly enjoying your company once more, my dearest son. In the event of peace it may well happen that your two brothers may also come here, which would probably be the last time I could have all my children together.*[^7]
