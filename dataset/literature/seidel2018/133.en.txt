We know today that nothing came of the entire enterprise. But it is a project from FL's box of ideas and belongs here. – The author Adolf Fischer did not distinguish, in his 1871 text, between Glenk sen. and Glenck jun., as indeed other authors also failed to do. The search for hard coal plays no role in Fischer's account.

There is, however, a substantial number of letters from Glenck to FL extant, as well as a few of the addressee's replies.[^103] The two remained in contact from 1802 to at least 1815, and the correspondence concerned almost exclusively the saltworks and the hard-coal project. In 1802 there are six letters, in 1804 seven. The content at first consisted of often very technically emphasized progress reports, figures on wood consumption together with its costs, and information about an accident. In 1804 complaints were reported from financial backers (shareholders from Schwäbisch Hall) who had committed their entire fortunes, could provide no new capital, and now had no further hope. On 7 August 1804, 3,000 florins arrived for Glenck from a Braunsbach address (that is, quite nearby), and he mentioned the purchase of estates (probably meadow lands) in order to improve the roads. He wrote further that part of the saltworks' income (the salt fund) had been put by him into the mining project. This could become dangerous, since he had to reckon with soon having to present the account books!

The Prince of (Hohenlohe-)Schillingsfürst (who is certainly also a shareholder) seemed to have grown especially uneasy and would, according to Glenck writing to FL, presumably visit him in Breslau "in the course of the month" (August 1804). He added a wish: "May God and princely wisdom avert all misfortunes to be feared." So things once again looked bad, but he hoped "to have to write no more laments."
