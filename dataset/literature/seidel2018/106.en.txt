# Seidel (2018), p. 106
# translation by Claude

> *Besides these, the Prince had taken two other officers into his headquarters, Major v. Loucey, an émigré and an older man, and Captain von Blumenstein, likewise an émigré and a former engineer officer. He had been quite young at the outbreak of the Revolution, for he was now only my own age. One can imagine how I must have felt, given this total lack of occupation, after three years of a very busy life, and while my actual affairs, to my greatest pecuniary detriment, lay entirely idle. Already on the third day of my presence I went to the Prince and asked him to give me something to do. He replied that his affairs were all very well taken care of and that he had none for me. At the same time he entered into a lengthy conversation with me about our situation, in the course of which, although he did not conceal that the march to Erfurt was contrary to purpose and most distasteful to him, he nevertheless expressed his conviction that we would still come to the aid of the Austrians in time. [...] What this flanking march of 30 (German) miles was meant to signify, and where it was meant to lead, I have never been able to comprehend. [...] The Prince went on leave to his territories, and the rest of us dispersed again just as we had come. We did not part without tears, for we had all come to love him, and many of us to hold him in the highest esteem. The Prince had known how to awaken, sustain, and animate a quiet, earnest, and noble spirit at headquarters, and he was truly loved as a child loves, and held in the highest esteem, by all who surrounded him. [...] In general, for as long as I knew him, he showed a visible effort to conceal the failings of our Cabinet and to keep secret the bad news that constantly arrived — evidently in order to preserve the spirit of the army.
