## 14 Friedrich Ludwig, the Debtor

### 14.1 How did Friedrich Ludwig manage all this financially?

The question has long since been answered, though not correctly. For the fact is: he did not manage it! He passed on to his succeeding son a mountain of debt, which, however, was probably not attributable to the Ingelfingen activities. In 1795/96 he had still stated that "his edifice" (meaning his life's plan) was now firmly founded and essentially complete, needing only to be finished off in order to pay back the borrowed money, which, as he hoped, would in future be easily possible for his son out of surpluses.

When he then "resigned" in 1806 (stepped down as reigning Prince of Hohenlohe-Öhringen), the calculated sum of total debts (both personal and those of "the House") amounted to 280,000 florins — an enormous sum even by the standards of the time, one that had presumably still been underestimated. And matters continued to worsen even after his mother-in-law had fully recognized the disaster.[^117] She pressed August in Öhringen not to assume any further guarantees for debts. She herself came to an agreement with FL over 20,000 florins; but this became known, the creditors were standing by and would have seized it at once. The transfer was called off. It is thus not surprising that in 1815 the rumor arose that the King of Prussia had set aside 800,000 thalers in order finally to free FL from his debt crisis. The rumor was false. Insight into this misery is provided, among other things, by Triebenfeld's schedules of debts, he having served for over twenty years as FL's plenipotentiary in this domain.
