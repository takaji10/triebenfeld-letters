# seidel2018  101  en
# Seidel (2018), p. 101
# translation by Claude

There follows a lengthy account by von Cölln of how the defeat in the last battle is likely to have come about.

> *His enemies say that the prince has never shown character; this is proven by the story of his wife, who ran away from him and heaped shame upon him; this is proven by his so frequently circulating promissory notes, his ever-empty pockets. This is proven by those around him, whose reputation is in part decidedly bad, in part ambiguous. A Triebenfeld, a Grattauer, and others are his advisers and agents, the Jew Goldstecker his money-broker [...] How often has he proposed exchange schemes to the King between his own and the royal estates, in which he naturally sought to profit? How has he not profited from the South Prussian squandering of estates? How could the King entrust an army to a foreign prince sunk in debt, greedy for money, whose lands are in the power of the enemy?*

Then again another topic: "That the Princess Hohenlohe was a Messalina was not the prince's fault." (A "Messaline" — the expression is meant, more or less polemically, to point to a particularly dissolute life, as is attributed to Messalina, wife of the Roman emperor Claudius; in any case, a foreign-word dictionary of the 19th century does not distinguish between Messalina and Messaline. Alternatively: Messaline is also a term for a lustrous artificial silk fabric.)

> *That Hohenlohe is not a good manager of his affairs, cannot reckon accounts, and keeps no budget — this is true, but it is no proof of a bad character. That Triebenfeld is his friend, that is true; but whether this man is as bad as he is portrayed, that is not proven. He serves the prince faithfully, that I know. Whether out of ambition, or out of pure devotion? That is immaterial.*
