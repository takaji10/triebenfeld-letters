Marianne's marriage, soon after the separation, to Count Friedrich von Osten-Sacken (later a colonel in Grand Ducal Mecklenburg service) and the birth of their first child together, Angelica, in 1802, do not warrant any speculation about a pre-existing relationship with the new partner, who, as a distant relative, presumably came and went at her mother's house. The marriage to the man fifteen years her junior presumably took place in 1801. Why the formalities of the divorce from FL were not notarized until 1804 cannot be established.

Only at the end of the twentieth century was the already-mentioned statement on the separation from the year 1811 discovered (see p. 83f.). A look at the divorce documents is significant in this connection. They bear the date of 6 August 1804. Two documents, each bearing a seal and officially countersigned, in the hand of a scribe ("I" refers throughout to Marianne), stated the following:

> *Doc. 1: Since I well understand that, through my departure — undertaken of my own accord in the year 1799, during my husband's absence from Breslau, without any occasion given by him for it, and with the intention of not returning to him — and through the separation now continued for five years, I have made any reunion and restoration of good relations with him impossible, and have given the most well-founded occasion for a suit for divorce to be brought against me [...] [pending]; I hereby declare, after due deliberation, that in the event my husband shall bring, before the court whose jurisdiction I acknowledge, a suit directed toward the complete dissolution of our marriage as it has existed, and shall not only consent to a judicial separation of our marriage but shall also wish to declare me the party solely at fault by reason of the aforesaid willful departure, I shall likewise readily submit to the statutory penalty of divorce with respect to property, and shall abide by whatever may be determined concerning the upbringing and provision for our children —
