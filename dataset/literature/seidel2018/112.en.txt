There was in any case cause to reprimand the so-called Magazine Intendant of Jena: for the next three weeks there was a shortage not only of ammunition but also of food. — Goethe met FL there regularly for several days at the midday table, in a large company. In his "Annalen" Goethe wrote of these meetings, or at least of one of them:[^84]

> *In the meantime I had moved into the side wing of the castle, in order to make room for Prince Hohenlohe, who, advancing reluctantly with his detachment of troops, would rather have wished to march against the enemy along the road to Hof. — With Colonel von Massenbach, the hothead, I had a strange scene. In him too the inclination to write got in the way of political prudence and military activity. He had composed a curious opus, nothing less than a moral manifesto against Napoleon. Everyone sensed, everyone feared, the overwhelming power of the French, and so it happened that the printer, accompanied by several members of the council, approached me, and all of them urgently begged me to refuse the printing of the manuscript submitted, which, upon the entry of the French army, would necessarily bring ruin upon the town. I had it handed over to me and found a sequence of periods, the first of which began with the words, 'Napoleon, I loved you!' The last, however, 'I hate you!' In between were expressed all the hopes and expectations that one had at first entertained of the greatness of Napoleon's character, in which one had fancied one must suppose morally human purposes in the extraordinary man, and at the end all the evil that one had had to suffer from him in recent times was reproached in the sharpest terms. With few changes one might have translated it into the vexation of a deceived lover over his unfaithful beloved, and so this essay appeared as ridiculous as it was dangerous.*
