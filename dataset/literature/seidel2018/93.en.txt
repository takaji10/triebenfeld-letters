## 9 After the Defeat against Napoleon in 1806

Now the good fortune that had once accompanied him, so successful before, deserted him. Two weeks after the great battle of Jena and Auerstädt was lost, he capitulated on 28 October at Prenzlau, together with the remainder of the troops he commanded, to the French. In a letter to the King — unfortunately not preserved — he resigned from his command duties.

The news of the defeat evidently did not spread so very quickly after all. The Weimar Privy Councillor Voigt informed Goethe, in a letter dated as late as 2 November 1806, that Duke Charles Augustus of Saxe-Weimar had marched with his troops as far as Havelberg and intended to hand over his command to FL! In "early November," Voigt referred, likewise in a letter to Goethe, to a French army report according to which FL had been taken prisoner with 10,000 men after the capitulation of Prenzlau. Duke Charles Augustus, with his corps, had been cut off from the Elbe.[^69]

### 9.1 Jena and Auerstädt and the Capitulation of Prenzlau

For his soldiers FL had negotiated favorable terms, but he himself and his staff were of course taken prisoner. On 3 November they were staying at Lithin. From there he himself writes (that is, in his hard-to-read hand) the following letter to his son August in Öhringen, which shall be reproduced in full and verbatim. It is of interest what all he considers worth reporting and important:
