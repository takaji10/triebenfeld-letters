As an officer in the Franconian Circle Regiment, he had fought against Prussia in the Seven Years' War, and had spent years imprisoned in Magdeburg (one of his three daughters was born in Magdeburg). To avert the disadvantages of the previous law of inheritance, he introduced primogeniture in 1774, as did the other Hohenlohe lines around this time — that is, from then on the firstborn was, as a rule, to succeed his father alone. This was Friedrich Ludwig (1746–1818, Prince from 1796). He had two younger brothers, Prince Friedrich Carl (1752–1815) and Prince Georg (1757–1807), and four sisters, of whom only Princess Sophie Christiane (1762–1831) reached adulthood and was to become especially important to Friedrich Ludwig.

A brief account of the court household: for 1738 there exists a list of court personnel, numbering as many as 68 persons at that time.[^4] Adding their family members and the manservants and maidservants, this comes to 104. Further added to this were the "clergy" financed by the court and administrative staff of various ranks, so that in total 172 persons were closely bound to the court and economically dependent upon it. Heinrich August and his wife Wilhelmine Eleonore, Princess of Hohenlohe-Neuenstein-Öhringen, were a rather modest and unassuming ruling couple. Whether the court household retained this size under Heinrich August is rather unlikely. Nevertheless, this makes it understandable that the citizens of Ingelfingen were worried, when in 1805 the transfer of the court household to Öhringen threatened a disadvantage for their town (see p. 64).
