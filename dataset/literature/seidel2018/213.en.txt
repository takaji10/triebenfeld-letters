# Seidel (2018), p. 213
# translation by Claude

FL the dynamic practitioner in all military-strategic matters, who always kept his soldiers in mind as well, and who was often not in agreement with the overall Prussian strategy determined by the Duke of Brunswick. Yet King Frederick William II [sic] followed the latter's plans almost invariably, and FL obeyed the King unconditionally.

Concretely, from 1805 onward the matter at hand was the impending struggle against Napoleon's army, culminating in the defeat at Jena and Auerstädt in October 1806 and the march of the remaining army by way of Magdeburg toward a hoped-for place of safety beyond the Oder. On 28 October 1806 came the capitulation of Prenzlau, which had been negotiated by FL with a French general. How this came about was for some time a matter of debate (see also Kahlert's letters to FL in chapter 16). Had Massenbach inadequately, or even wrongly, informed FL about the strength of the French troops? FL's own troops, moreover, were in poor condition. A period of rest at Magdeburg had not been possible, supply was inadequate, and discipline could be maintained only with difficulty. Fischer records an account of an episode, a conversation between FL and Massenbach, on 25 October 1806, that is, three days before the capitulation at Prenzlau:

> *[Massenbach] spoke even of the perniciousness of the alliance with Russia, of the necessity of one with France instead, and said that the moment such an alliance were concluded, he would leave Prussian service in order to enter French service. The Prince told him that if he were to carry this out, he would have to have him arrested at once — but laughed, remarking that with Massenbach the deed was never so bad as the words.[^153]*
