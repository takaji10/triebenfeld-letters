# Seidel (2018), p. 218
# translation by Claude

> *Men who, while holding strictly to truthfulness, were well disposed toward the Prince describe him [Massenbach] as a person who knew how to impress through the airs of profound thought and through bold assertion even more than through genuine learned knowledge — as an overwrought, eccentric enthusiast who had dabbled in various branches of learning and whose outward bearing truly possessed something capable of deceiving. He had acquired a kind of intellectual mastery over the Prince and had made himself indispensable to him. [...] Toward this man, who must be called his evil genius, Prince Friedrich Ludwig was weak and blind.[^159]*

This negative assessment surely also rests on statements by the previously mentioned Marwitz.

*[...] quite unbelievable [...] how this man [Massenbach] could attain such a reputation and so completely dominate Prince Hohenlohe [...]. When Massenbach spoke of operations in learnedly confused terms, it happened that the prince — whether because these expressions, and thereby the matters themselves, were foreign to him, or because his advancing age and Bonaparte's uninterrupted good fortune in war robbed him of all the confidence he otherwise still liked to display in public — would usually listen to him in silence, sometimes agree, but even more often would, on the following day, order these same things or speak of them as though they were his own ideas. In doing so he would each time assert, with incomprehensible self-deception, that Massenbach had no influence over him, and would very correctly explain why he [Massenbach] could have none, since he was far too fickle in his principles and mistaken in his political opinions.*[^160]
